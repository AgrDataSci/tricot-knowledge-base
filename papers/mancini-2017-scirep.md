## Questions this paper answers

- Can smallholder farmers reliably evaluate hundreds of unfamiliar genebank accessions?
- How do farmer scores relate to traits measured by agronomists?
- Do women and men, or farmers in different locations, value wheat traits differently?
- Can farmer evaluations identify landraces worth releasing or using in breeding?
- What happened to the top-ranked varieties and landraces after this study?

## Summary

Centralized breeding often fails to reach smallholder farmers in low-input, high-risk environments, where landraces may still be preferred for their adaptation and end-use quality. Participatory methods had so far tapped little of farmers' traditional knowledge in a quantitative way. This study asks whether farmers' evaluations of a large, unfamiliar collection can be broken down into measurable traits and used to select varieties for local breeding.

In the 2012 season, 400 wheat accessions from the Ethiopian Biodiversity Institute genebank, mostly landraces plus 25 released modern varieties, were grown in replicated trials at two highland sites, Hagreselam in Tigray and Geregera in Amhara. At each site, 30 farmers (15 women and 15 men) first took part in gender-separated focus group discussions on climate change, local varieties and selection criteria. Farmers then scored every plot in the field for earliness, tillering, spike morphology and overall quality on a five-point scale, using a secret-ballot method with seeds held in a closed hand so that farmers did not influence each other. This produced 192,000 data points. Technicians measured ten agronomic traits on the same plots.

Trait priorities differed by gender and location. In Table 1, women in Geregera listed the most traits, including four end-use qualities, although the text attributes these to women in Hagreselam. Marketability mattered to women in one site and men in the other. Agreement between women and men was higher for agronomic traits than for quality traits, and lower in Hagreselam for all traits. Spike scores explained most of the overall score (correlations above 0.9), while earliness, though ranked important in discussions, contributed little to overall scores. Farmer scores correlated with related metric traits, such as earliness with days to booting, flowering and maturity, and spike with seeds per spike. Canonical correlation showed that overall scores related positively to yield, biomass, seeds per spike, grain weight and plant height, in a different combination at each site. Metric traits only partly explained farmer scores.

Weighted farmer scores produced a ranking of all 400 accessions at each site. After overall (fixed weight 1), spike carried the most weight, then tillering, with earliness last. Twenty of the top 50 were shared between sites, including four modern varieties, and the two communities preferred the same combination of traits even when they picked different varieties. Blind evaluation was consistent. A modern variety scored in the field ranked similarly to the same variety judged from farmers' own experience.

The authors argue that eliciting traditional knowledge quantitatively is a step toward locally adapted ideotypes. They report that 50 of the evaluated landraces were crossed with an Ethiopian modern variety to build a nested association mapping population, and that a collection of more than 1,200 recombinant inbred lines was also evaluated by smallholder communities. Top-ranked varieties, including several landraces that ranked above modern varieties, were being redistributed to farmers through crowdsourcing, reaching more than 900 households in 24 villages by 2016. The authors propose testing fewer varieties across more environments as the next step.

## Methods in brief

- **Material.** 400 genebank accessions from the Ethiopian Biodiversity Institute (25 released modern varieties, the remaining 375 landraces by subtraction), two locations, 20 × 20 partial lattice with two replications, 2012 season. Farmers had no previous knowledge of the accessions.
- **Farmers.** 30 per site (15 women, 15 men), focus groups, then participatory evaluation in six groups of five with a rapporteur.
- **Scoring.** Five-point scale for earliness, tillering, spike and overall, with secret seed-ballot scoring.
- **Blinding.** Plots had anonymous labels, each group's entry point into the field was randomized, and a rapporteur recorded scores and kept dominant personalities in check. Scoring took place during seed maturation.
- **Agronomy.** Ten metric traits measured by technicians, plus WorldClim climate characterization.
- **Analysis.** ANOVA, Spearman correlations, canonical correlation analysis, weighted ranking (each trait weight is the mean of a consistency weight and its correlation with overall), PCA.

## Stated limitations

- Trial fields received more fertilizer and weeding than farmers' fields, so they act as mother trials rather than farm conditions.
- Quality and end-use traits could not be evaluated in the field.
- Only two locations, chosen to maximize the number of varieties rather than environments.
- The two sites were scored at different maturity stages (Zadoks about 85 in Hagreselam, 75 in Geregera), which may explain the non-normal earliness scores in Hagreselam.

## Notes

- Not a tricot trial. Farmers scored every plot of all 400 accessions on a 1 to 5 scale in researcher-managed trials. The paper cites van Etten 2011 (vanetten-2011-idsbull) for the crowdsourcing approach used to redistribute top varieties. The link to later work (fadda-2020-fps, vanetten-2019-pnas, desousa-2021-commsbio, gesesse-2023-pnas) comes from those cards, not from this paper.
- The agronomic data for the same 400 accessions are analysed in Mengistu et al. 2016 (Plant Biotechnol J, ref. 14), which has no card yet.
- Source issue: the text (p. 4) says women in Hagreselam named the most traits and four end-use traits (enjera, bread, tella, kolo). Table 1 (p. 4, checked by hand on the PDF) lists these four under women in Geregera (14 traits), while Hagreselam women list 11 traits with a single "Use" trait. The Summary follows Table 1.
- The paper states 25 improved varieties among 400 accessions (p. 2). 375 landraces is derived and not printed. Fadda et al. 2020 describes the same panel as 373 farmer varieties and 27 improved varieties. This paper cannot resolve the difference.
- Source issue: the text (p. 7) cites Fig. 3b for the top 50 varieties projected onto the PCA, but this is Fig. 4b.
