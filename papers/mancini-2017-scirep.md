## Questions this paper answers

- Can smallholder farmers reliably evaluate hundreds of unfamiliar genebank accessions?
- How do farmer scores relate to traits measured by agronomists?
- Do women and men, or farmers in different locations, value wheat traits differently?
- Can farmer evaluations identify landraces worth releasing or using in breeding?
- How did the Seeds for Needs line of work move from genebank to farm?

## Summary

Centralized breeding often fails to reach smallholder farmers in low-input, high-risk environments, where landraces may still be preferred for their adaptation and end-use quality. Participatory methods had so far tapped little of farmers' traditional knowledge in a quantitative way. This study asks whether farmers' evaluations of a large, unfamiliar collection can be broken down into measurable traits and used to select varieties for local breeding.

In the 2012 season, 400 durum wheat accessions from the Ethiopian Biodiversity Institute genebank, mostly landraces plus 25 released modern varieties, were grown in replicated trials at two highland sites, Hagreselam in Tigray and Geregera in Amhara. At each site, 30 farmers (15 women and 15 men) first took part in gender-separated focus group discussions on climate change, local varieties and selection criteria. Farmers then scored every plot in the field for earliness, tillering, spike morphology and overall quality on a five-point scale, using a secret-ballot method with seeds held in a closed hand so that farmers did not influence each other. This produced 192,000 data points. Technicians measured ten agronomic traits on the same plots.

Trait priorities differed by gender and location. Women in Hagreselam used more quality and end-use traits, and marketability mattered to women in one site and men in the other. Agreement between women and men was higher for agronomic traits than for quality traits. Spike scores explained most of the overall score (correlations above 0.9), while earliness, though ranked important in discussions, contributed little to overall scores. Farmer scores correlated with related metric traits, such as earliness with days to booting, flowering and maturity, and spike with seeds per spike. Canonical correlation showed that overall scores combined yield, biomass, seeds per spike, grain weight and plant height, but metric traits only partly explained them.

Weighted farmer scores produced a ranking of all 400 accessions at each site. Twenty of the top 50 were shared between sites, including four modern varieties, and the two communities preferred the same combination of traits even when they picked different varieties. Blind evaluation was consistent. A modern variety scored in the field ranked similarly to the same variety judged from farmers' own experience.

The authors argue that eliciting traditional knowledge quantitatively is a step toward locally adapted ideotypes. They report that 50 of the evaluated landraces were crossed with a modern variety to build a nested association mapping population, and that top-ranked varieties were being redistributed to farmers through crowdsourcing, reaching more than 900 households in 24 villages by 2016. This is the starting point of the genebank-to-farm line of work that leads to fadda-2020-fps, vanetten-2019-pnas, desousa-2021-commsbio and gesesse-2023-pnas.

## Methods in brief

- **Material.** 400 genebank accessions (375 landraces, 25 modern varieties), two locations, 20 × 20 partial lattice with two replications, 2012 season.
- **Farmers.** 30 per site (15 women, 15 men), focus groups, then participatory evaluation in six groups of five with a rapporteur.
- **Scoring.** Five-point scale for earliness, tillering, spike and overall, with secret seed-ballot scoring.
- **Agronomy.** Ten metric traits measured by technicians, plus WorldClim climate characterization.
- **Analysis.** ANOVA, Spearman correlations, canonical correlation analysis, weighted ranking, PCA.

## Stated limitations

- Trial fields received more fertilizer and weeding than farmers' fields, so they act as mother trials rather than farm conditions.
- Quality and end-use traits could not be evaluated in the field.
- Only two locations, chosen to maximize the number of varieties rather than environments.
