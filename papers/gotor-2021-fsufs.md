## Questions this paper answers

- Does a crowdsourced variety evaluation programme increase varietal diversity on farms?
- Does varietal diversity translate into livelihood benefits for households?
- How large were the effects of Seeds for Needs in Bihar?
- How can impact be estimated when participants were not randomly assigned?

## Summary

Growing two or more varieties of a crop can lower disease, raise productivity and reduce yield variability, but earlier evidence came from experiments or observation of existing systems, not from interventions that introduce new varieties. This study evaluates whether such an intervention works in practice, using Seeds for Needs in Bihar, India, which the authors describe as the largest citizen science intervention to date, with 25,000 farmers and 47,000 plot-seasons from 2010 to 2017.

The analysis follows the sustainable rural livelihoods framework. The intervention is expected to build human capital (knowledge and skills), natural and physical capital (new seed) and social capital (connections among farmers), leading farmers to adopt promoted varieties, diversify their rice and wheat, and gain in productivity and in their ability to recover from shocks. In 2018, enumerators surveyed 600 households in 12 villages of three districts using a questionnaire adapted from RHoMIS and collected on tablets with ODK. The questionnaire covered the wheat and rice varieties sown over five years, seed sources, preferred traits, harvests, sales and climate-related losses, alongside household assets and shocks. Half the households were participants drawn from project records. The other half were non-participants, 150 from the same villages and 150 from nine similar villages never reached by the programme.

Because participation was voluntary and no baseline existed, the authors used a doubly robust estimator, combining a model of who participated with a model of the outcome, and then a system of simultaneous equations estimated by generalized method of moments with inverse probability weights to test each link of the causal chain. Diversity was measured with the Simpson index, productivity as self-reported yield change over five years, and recovery capacity as a self-assessed index across four kinds of shock.

Participation increased varietal diversity, by 0.03 on the Simpson index for rice (5%) and 0.06 for wheat (11%). Perceived yield change rose by 0.185 points for rice and 0.245 for wheat, which the authors report as 3.94% and 4.80% gains, and recovery capacity rose by 0.40 points, about 7%. The equation system supported the chain. Adopting promoted varieties raised diversity, diversity raised perceived yields of both crops, and higher wheat yields improved recovery capacity, though not for rice. Benefits for recovery were largest among households that started with the lowest recovery capacity. The authors note that the estimates are conservative because spillovers to non-participants were ignored.

They conclude that participatory programmes built on local varietal diversity strengthen rural livelihoods, and that the results offer policymakers evidence on the size of these effects.

## Methods in brief

- **Sample.** 600 households (300 participants, 300 non-participants) in 12 project villages and 9 comparison villages, three districts of Bihar, surveyed February to August 2018.
- **Survey.** 17-section questionnaire including RHoMIS modules, collected with ODK.
- **Outcomes.** Simpson diversity index, self-reported yield change (−4 to +4), recovery capacity index (−4 to +4).
- **Analysis.** Doubly robust estimator, then five simultaneous equations by GMM with inverse probability weighting.

## Stated limitations

- No baseline or randomized control, because the programme expanded in response to local demand.
- Productivity and recovery are self-reported.
- Floods affected village selection in Saran and Samastipur, skewing the sample.
- Spillovers to non-participants were ignored, so estimates are conservative.
