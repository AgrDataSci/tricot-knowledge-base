## Questions this paper answers

- How much does a common bean tricot trial cost per farmer in a national programme?
- Why do staff time and transport dominate the cost of some tricot trials?
- How much could a programme save by delegating routine fieldwork, centralizing training and clustering farmers?
- What should a lean research supervision protocol for tricot look like?
- How costly are seed and materials compared with labour and travel?
- Which researcher time should a project pay for, and which belongs to the breeding budget?

## Summary

Stephen Angudubo and colleagues costed the common bean tricot trial that NARO ran through the National Crops Resources Research Institute (NaCRRI) in the first season of 2025, with support from AVISA and 1000FARMS. The trial put 24 varieties from four product profiles (yellow, sugars, red mottled and red, white) on 120 farms in Lwengo, Nakaseke and Hoima districts, across two agro-ecological zones, with 360 plots in total. Breeders, research officers and research assistants worked with technicians and extension field agents.

The team applied the 1000FARMS costing template and guidelines. As in the Zambia maize case, only marginal costs that grow with the number of farmers are counted, and office-based preparation and central training are fixed costs left out of the total.

Marginal costs came to USD 11,070, or USD 92.25 per farmer. Field labour was the largest item at 37%, followed by transport (fuel and vehicle costs) at 29% and travel expenses (per diem and allowances) at 27%. Materials took only 6% and communication under 1%. Monitoring took 86% of labour costs, and two research-team visits to about 25% of farms took 77% of monitoring labour, because breeder, research officer and research assistant travelled together. Research travel made up 51% of travel expenses. Seed took only 16% of material costs, while sisal strings for row planting took about 35%.

The authors name two cost drivers. The first is multiple research roles doing routine fieldwork that local field agents could do, duplicating visits. The second is the delivery model. Field agents were trained in the field, seed went to many parish-level points, and farmers met in many small sessions, all of which multiplied trips. Visits sampled at random without clustered routes also added distance.

Scenarios put numbers on the alternatives. Delegating monitoring, data collection and yield measurement to trained field agents lowers the cost per farmer to USD 63.97, mostly through labour, and to USD 58.81 when the travel savings are added. Centralizing training and seed distribution and clustering farmers lowers it to USD 81.32. Combining all changes brings it to USD 47.40, a 49% saving of about USD 5,382.

The authors conclude that tricot is cost-effective and that the constraint is delivery design, not the approach. They recommend local field agents as the default for routine work, a single trial manager auditing 20% to 30% of farms with a quality checklist, central training of field agents, bulk seed delivery to hubs, hubs of 10 to 20 nearby farmers with bundled activities, and SOPs and MOUs that fix these rules.

## Methods in brief

- **Tool.** 1000FARMS activity-based Excel costing template, filled following the costing guidelines (angudubo-2026-guide).
- **Standard rates.** UN travel reimbursement rates for vehicle costs, ITU country baskets for communication, inflation-adjusted market prices for materials and NARO salary scales for labour.
- **Assumptions.** Trials follow the ten tricot steps. The research team leads preparation, design and packaging, distributes seed to drop-off points and supports yield measurement on about 30% of farms and farmer feedback. Field agents lead monitoring and data collection.
- **Scope.** Marginal costs only. A 10% to 15% share of researcher staff costs is charged to on-farm testing, with 15% applied here.
- **Inputs.** Fertilizer and pesticide were not supplied, so trials reflect farmers' own practice. Sisal strings were supplied to support row planting.
- **Scenarios.** Delegating routine fieldwork, central training, cutting seed-distribution distance by 60%, halving meeting sessions through clustering, and all changes combined.

## Stated limitations

- Fixed costs are excluded, so the total understates the full cost of running the trial.
- Decentralization needs careful selection and training of field agents, routine spot checks, accountability and feedback loops to protect data quality.
- Farmers may already meet in groups of 15 to 20, so the gain from hubs depends on making each group a geographically compact unit served by the same field agents.

## Notes

- Part of the 1000FARMS costing case studies by Stephen Angudubo and colleagues, with angudubo-2026-zari (maize, Zambia). Both apply angudubo-2026-guide. Together they are the main evidence in the knowledge base on what a tricot trial costs and why.
- Costs are marginal costs per farmer. They are not comparable with the cost per equivalent experimental unit in desousa-2024-asd, a single case study of potato in Rwanda that compares tricot with RCBD.
- The executive summary calls travel expenses the largest operational item, while Table 1 and the body rank them third after labour and transport.
- The body says travel, labour and transport total USD 10,070.4 (about 91%). That sum leaves out the travel allowance. With it the three components total USD 10,325.9 (about 93%).
- An 81% cut in field labour is about USD 3,296, slightly below the USD 3,393.7 scenario saving.
- Compared with the Zambia maize case (USD 142 per farmer, travel and materials largest), costs here are lower per farmer and driven by labour and transport. Both reports attribute cost mainly to delivery choices.
