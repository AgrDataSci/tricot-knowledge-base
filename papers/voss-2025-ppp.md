## Questions this paper answers

- Can tricot support breeding of neglected and underutilized (opportunity) crops?
- What drives farmer preference for leafy amaranth varieties?
- How can farmers be segmented by gender and business orientation?
- How can segments guide breeders and seed enterprises?

## Summary

Opportunity crops, also called neglected and underutilized species, can diversify food systems with nutritious, climate-resilient foods, but few improved varieties exist and farmers struggle to access quality planting material. The study tests whether citizen science can support demand-driven breeding and seed production of these crops, using leafy amaranth as a case.

The World Vegetable Center and partners ran tricot trials of 14 amaranth varieties with 2,063 farmers in Benin, Mali and Tanzania (794 in Benin, 969 in Mali and 300 in Tanzania), including trials in the Mtwara and Lindi regions of Tanzania in 2022. Women made up the majority of participants overall. Farmers ranked their three varieties for overall performance and traits, and the team collected household and farm characteristics, including household structure.

Overall preference was driven mainly by plant survival, yield, leaf size, taste and marketability. Cluster analysis of farmer characteristics identified four segments, older women generalists, young women specialists, older men generalists and young men specialists, which differed by gender, age and business orientation. These segments preferred different varieties, so a single best variety would not serve all users.

The authors argue that the segments and their preferences give breeders and seed enterprises actionable information on market segments, and support demand-driven amaranth breeding and seed system development. They present the methods and lessons as applicable to other opportunity crops in Africa and elsewhere.

The study places amaranth within the renewed attention to neglected crops amid food insecurity, poor nutrition and climate change. African traditional leafy vegetables are increasingly demanded, can raise smallholder incomes and improve diets, but seed systems for them are weak and improved varieties scarce, so breeding needs clear information on what different users want. The wide variation in preferences across countries and farmer groups points to context-specific breeding strategies rather than a single target. Because the segments combine gender, age and business orientation, they also show where improved amaranth varieties could expand economic opportunities, especially for women, who are central to vegetable production and trade in these countries. The authors present the study as a model for participatory research on other opportunity crops, linking on-farm evidence directly to breeding and seed-enterprise decisions.

The trials also show tricot working at scale for a leafy vegetable, extending the method beyond the grain, legume and root crops where it was first developed, across three countries with different languages, markets and farming systems. Women made up 1,159 of the 2,063 participants.

## Methods in brief

- **Trials.** Tricot with 14 amaranth varieties, three per farmer, in three countries.
- **Participants.** 2,063 farmers with gender and household data.
- **Analysis.** Plackett-Luce models of overall and trait rankings, Kendall correlations between traits and overall preference, and cluster analysis to define farmer segments with segment-specific variety rankings.

## Stated limitations

- Segments are defined from survey characteristics in these three countries and may differ elsewhere.
- Variety availability and seed supply constrain uptake of preferred varieties.
