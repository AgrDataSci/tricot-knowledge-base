## Questions this paper answers

- How does a tricot experiment work, step by step?
- Who does what, from implementers to local facilitators and farmers?
- How many farmers and options should I start with?
- When should farmers make observations, and what should they be asked?
- How is data collected by phone or visit, and how many farmers can a field agent cover?

## Summary

This illustrated guide explains how to run tricot experiments with hundreds or thousands of farmers. It presents tricot as a way for research centers to validate and disseminate new technologies quickly, with farmers acting as researchers on their own farms. It uses a sports analogy to explain the analysis, where scores depend on the matches players win and lose, just as varieties gain worth from the comparisons they win.

The guide follows ten steps, each with clear roles for implementers (research and development organizations), local facilitators and farmers. Implementers define the crop attributes to evaluate, train facilitators, organize the initial workshop, prepare and distribute trial packages, track data collection, compile and analyze data in ClimMob, and return results in a final workshop. Farmers plant and manage the trials exactly as they manage their own fields, for example not irrigating trial plots if they do not irrigate their crops.

Its recommendations are concrete. Trials typically include 8 to 12 technology options. A first project should involve around 100 to 200 farmers, which is enough for good results in most situations, and more farmers should be added each cycle. Workshops work best with a maximum of 20 farmers. Observation questions ask which option was best and which was worst for a trait, at set moments such as sowing, 30 to 75 days after planting, and after harvest. The guide lists four elements every trial package should contain, including the three options and the observation card.

Data can be collected by field visits, where one field agent can usually cover up to 25 farmers, or by phone calls entered into ODK Collect, with WhatsApp and interactive voice response as newer options. Results are analyzed in ClimMob, which shows which options performed best and under which conditions, such as one variety under irrigation and another under drought. Feedback workshops in small groups of five to seven people let farmers discuss results and help design the next cycle. A FAQ notes that both ClimMob and ODK Collect are free.

The FAQ notes that tricot has mostly evaluated varieties of annual crops such as beans, rice and wheat, but can test options of any agronomic technology, such as fertilizer management, and that modest charges may apply in future for commercial or very large trials. Field agents photograph trials and record the farmer's name and package code with each photo, and can collect data by calling farmers or a neighbor's phone when visits are not possible. The guide stresses that including more farmers in each cycle should be a constant objective, so that more households benefit from the research.

## Methods in brief

- **Ten steps.** Planning, design, recruitment, package preparation, execution, observation, data collection, analysis, feedback, evaluation.
- **Design.** Three options per farmer from 8–12, assigned by ClimMob.
- **Observation.** Best and worst questions at fixed times after planting and harvest.
- **Data.** ODK Collect by visit or phone, compiled in ClimMob.
- **Feedback.** Final workshops in small groups with a facilitator.

## Stated limitations

- Field visits limit how many farmers one agent can follow.
- Results depend on farmers following their normal management, which implementers cannot control.
