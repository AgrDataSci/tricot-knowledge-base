## Questions this paper answers

- How does a tricot experiment work, step by step?
- Who does what, from implementers to local facilitators and farmers?
- How many farmers and options should I start with?
- When should farmers make observations, and what should they be asked?
- How is data collected by phone or visit, and how many farmers can a field agent cover?
- How should trial packages be randomized and handed out across villages?

## Summary

This illustrated guide explains how to run tricot experiments with hundreds or thousands of farmers. It presents tricot as a way for research centers to validate and disseminate new technologies quickly, with farmers acting as researchers on their own farms. It uses a sports analogy to explain the analysis, where scores depend on the matches players win and lose, just as varieties gain worth from the comparisons they win.

The guide follows ten steps, each with clear roles for implementers (research and development organizations), local facilitators and farmers. Implementers define the crop attributes to evaluate, train facilitators, organize the initial workshop, prepare and distribute trial packages, track data collection, compile and analyze data in ClimMob, and return results in a final workshop. Farmers plant and manage the trials exactly as they manage their own fields, for example not irrigating trial plots if they do not irrigate their crops. Packages are blind, so farmers learn the names of their options only after data collection. ClimMob balances the randomization, and each village must receive packages with consecutive numbers so that every option is present in every village.

Its recommendations are concrete. Trials typically include 8 to 12 technology options. A first project should involve around 100 to 200 farmers, which is enough for good results in most situations, and more farmers should be added each cycle. Training and distribution workshops work best with a maximum of 20 farmers, held about four weeks before the trial starts. Observation questions ask which option was best and which was worst for one trait at a time, at moments that implementers set for each criterion, for example at sowing, about 30 days later and at harvest. The guide advises no more than ten criteria, always including overall performance and why the farmer prefers the best option. The guide lists four elements every trial package should contain, including the three options and the observation card.

Data can be collected by field visits, where one field agent can usually cover up to 25 farmers, or by phone calls entered into ODK Collect, with WhatsApp and interactive voice response as newer options. Results are analyzed in ClimMob, which shows which options performed best and under which conditions, such as one variety under irrigation and another under drought. In feedback workshops, farmers discuss results in small groups of five to seven people with a facilitator, and their comments feed the evaluation that improves the next cycle. A FAQ notes that both ClimMob and ODK Collect are free.

The FAQ notes that tricot has mostly evaluated varieties of annual crops such as beans, rice and wheat, but can test options of any agronomic technology, such as fertilizer management, and that modest charges may apply in future for commercial or very large trials. Field agents can also photograph observation cards, noting the farmer's name and package ID with each photo, or call farmers on their own or a neighbor's phone.

## Methods in brief

- **Ten steps.** Preparation, design, recruitment, distribution, execution, observation, compilation, analysis, feedback, evaluation.
- **Design.** Three options per farmer from 8–12, assigned by ClimMob.
- **Plot.** Three same-size sub-plots side by side (A left, B middle, C right), next to or inside the regular field, on an average spot.
- **Observation.** Best and worst questions, one criterion at a time, at times set by implementers (for example at sowing, about 30 days later and at harvest).
- **Data.** ODK Collect by visit or phone, compiled in ClimMob.
- **Feedback.** Final workshops in small groups with a facilitator.
- **Evaluation.** Five success indicators: completion rate, gender ratio, share of returning farmers, change in technology use, and spread in the community. After each cycle, drop the one or two options farmers liked least and add new ones.

## Stated limitations

- The guide gives no limitations section. Its practical constraints are that each field agent can cover only up to 25 farmers by visits, and that more farmers means more work to assist them (p.7, p.23).
- Trial plots must be managed exactly like the farmer's regular plot. Extra care or neglect will distort the results (p.19).
- The guide says it can only assist local design, because every tricot project differs by country, crop, farming system and people (p.27).
- It recommends tricot only where agronomic innovation can be part of the solution, after a problem analysis (p.6).

## Notes

- Card writer's comparison, not stated in the guide: a more practical, illustrated successor to steinke-2016-guide.
- The glossary (p.30) defines tricot by three features: blocks of three options, ranking as the way to report, and limited control of conditions to keep results realistic.
- The guide suggests extra seed of the preferred variety as a reward and says money is not needed (p.9, p.32).
- ClimMob can give location-specific results from GPS points and environmental maps, and ClimMobTools loads data into R (p.32).
- The example observation sheet (p.2) has a typo: in Data Collection 2 both bacterial wilt questions ask for the least damage. Do not copy it as a template.
- The guide's diagram (p.3) says local facilitators, and its text says local field agents. Both mean the same role.
- Criteria: the text advises no more than ten (p.8), the glossary says 5 to 10 (p.28). This is an inconsistency in the source.
