## Questions this paper answers

- Why do Kenyan smallholders keep buying old maize hybrids?
- Which traits do farmers look for when buying maize seed?
- How do farmers behave at an agro-dealer when their preferred variety is out of stock?
- Does seed price signal affordability or quality to farmers?
- What could speed up varietal turnover?

## Summary

Kenya's maize seed market is competitive, yet old varieties dominate, slowing varietal turnover as climate stress grows. Researchers at the University of Nairobi and CIMMYT studied why farmers in semi-arid Machakos County stay loyal to market leader hybrids.

They randomly selected 20 villages in three sub-counties and invited up to 32 eligible farmers per village who had bought hybrid seed in the last three years and took part in seed decisions, aiming for equal numbers of women and men. Each village got a mock agro-dealer store stocked with real hybrids suited to the zone. Farmers were randomly assigned to six cells. In half, all hybrids were available. In the other half, their preferred variety was out of stock. Some cells added an information poster or a price promotion. Surveys and focus groups covered motivations and risk. Farmers were grouped by the variety they said they planned to buy: market leader (Duma 43, released in 2003), competitor (such as Sungura 301 or Bayer hybrids) or low-cost (priced 20% lower).

Overall, 70% planned to buy the market leader, 21% a competitor and 7% a low-cost variety. Drought tolerance was the most valued trait (72%), followed by yield potential (70%), consumption qualities such as taste and cooking (57%) and grain qualities (55%). Market leader buyers emphasized consumption quality and resilience, competitor buyers yield and grain quality, and low-cost buyers drought tolerance (90%) and early maturity (64%). Low-cost buyers planned more land under maize and sold a larger share of their harvest.

Experience was narrow. A third of farmers had grown only one variety in the past two years, and market leader buyers had grown 2.5 varieties on average against 3.2 and 3.5 in the other groups. Over 40% of market leader buyers had grown only that variety. About two thirds said price mattered, and more of them saw it as a sign of quality than of affordability.

In the store, farmers chose in 34 seconds when their variety was in stock and 78 seconds when it was not. The delay was about 50 seconds for market leader buyers and 20 for competitor buyers. Only 3% asked questions when their variety was in stock, against 59% when it was not. The authors say most farmers ignored posters and price offers when their variety was available, but show no results by treatment. A multinomial logit model with low-cost buyers as the base linked market leader preference most strongly to planting the same variety last season, and positively to valuing food and harvest traits. Valuing early maturity and having bought more varieties before were linked to lower odds of market leader or competitor preference. Drought tolerance, gender and education were not significant.

The authors conclude that loyalty rests on brand familiarity, past performance and agronomic traits. They argue that breeding must go beyond performance, that seed companies and extension should build trust in new varieties through demonstration plots, field days and early adopter testimonials, and that regulation could set timelines for phasing out outdated varieties.

## Methods in brief

- **Sampling.** Multistage, 3 of 8 sub-counties, 20 villages, up to 32 eligible farmers per village, target 50/50 gender split. Men had more no-shows, so 57% of participants were women. Fieldwork February to March 2021, follow-up April to May 2023.
- **Experiment.** Mock agro-dealer store in each village with nine real hybrids at market prices (KES 500, or KES 400 for the two low-cost Kenya Seed Company hybrids, per 2 kg). Six cells crossed stock availability (preferred variety in stock or out of stock) with three conditions (control, technical information poster, or 10% price promotion on two random products). 293 farmers in the in-stock treatment and 294 out of stock.
- **Data.** Survey on traits, experience and price attitudes, focus groups, recorded selection time and questions asked.
- **Analysis.** Chi-square tests, t-tests and one-way ANOVA across the three groups. Multinomial logit with low-cost buyers as base category (pseudo R² 0.25, n = 587, Hausman test for IIA).

## Stated limitations

- Correlational design limits causal inference; experimental or longitudinal studies are needed.
- Recall of varieties grown over two years may be biased, though the recall period was kept short.
- Gender dynamics, extension access and social networks need further study.

## Notes

- The abstract reports the 70/21/7 split. The in-stock and out-of-stock counts by category (210 and 207, 60 and 68, 23 and 19) give slightly different shares; use the paper's stated percentages.
- The 70/21/7 groups come from farmers' stated intentions to buy (p. 4), although the abstract says farmers "preferred" and "chose". The paper does not report which seed each farmer actually took in the store.
- Category shares differ across the paper: abstract 70/21/7 (sums to 98), Table 1 69.7/22.4/7.1, and the treatment counts 417/128/42 of 587, which give 71.0/21.8/7.2 (card's calculation).
- Price attitude: the text says 67% saw price as important and 33% not, but Table 4 gives 62.9% (22.4 + 40.5) and 36.0%. Low-cost buyers were the least price-focused (68% said price is not important).
- The discussion says low-cost buyers had less education and market leader buyers valued yield potential, but Table 3 shows no significant education differences and Table 6 shows no significant yield coefficient for the market leader.
- The variety lists do not match: PHB 3253 is called excluded on p. 4 but is a competitor in Table 1. Table 1 has P2809W, which is missing from Table S1. Variety ages in Table 1 do not always match the release years in Table S1.
- A follow-up with the same farmers in 2023 is mentioned but not described, so it is unclear which data come from which round. This is a gap in the source.
- Simtowe et al. 2019, cited for the 40-70% Kenyan adoption range, is a Uganda study. This is a problem in the source.
- Related CIMMYT market intelligence work on product concepts and market segments (rutsaert-2023-mib, donovan-2025-expagric). Its finding that farmers stick to known varieties matters for tricot, which exposes farmers to new varieties on their own farms (vanetten-2019-expagric).
- See voss-2021-outlook on gender and maize breeding, and occelli-2024-natplants on how trait preference studies are designed.
