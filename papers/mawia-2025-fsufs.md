## Questions this paper answers

- Why do Kenyan smallholders keep buying old maize hybrids?
- Which traits do farmers look for when buying maize seed?
- How do farmers behave at an agro-dealer when their preferred variety is out of stock?
- Does seed price signal affordability or quality to farmers?
- What could speed up varietal turnover?

## Summary

Kenya's maize seed market is competitive, yet old varieties dominate, slowing varietal turnover as climate stress grows. Harriet Mawia, John Mburu and Evans Chimoita at the University of Nairobi, with Pieter Rutsaert of CIMMYT, studied why farmers in semi-arid Machakos County stay loyal to market leader hybrids.

They randomly selected 20 villages in three sub-counties and invited up to 32 eligible farmers per village, namely those who had bought hybrid seed in the last three years and took part in seed decisions, aiming for equal numbers of women and men. A mock agro-dealer store was built in each village and stocked with real hybrids suited to the zone. Farmers were randomly assigned to six cells. In half, all hybrids were available, while in the other half the farmer's preferred variety was out of stock. Some cells added an information poster or a price offer. Surveys and focus groups added data on motivations and risk. Farmers were grouped by their preferred variety as market leader (Duma 43, released in 2003), competitor (such as Sungura 301 and Bayer and Pioneer hybrids) or low-cost (priced 20% lower).

Of the participants, 70% preferred the market leader, 21% a competitor and 7% a low-cost variety. Drought tolerance was the most valued trait (72%), followed by yield potential (70%), consumption qualities such as taste and cooking (57%) and grain qualities (55%). Market leader buyers emphasized consumption quality and resilience, competitor buyers yield and grain quality, and low-cost buyers drought tolerance (90%) and early maturity (64%). Low-cost buyers farmed more land and sold more of their harvest.

Experience was narrow. A third of farmers had grown only one variety in the past two years, and market leader buyers had grown 2.5 varieties on average against 3.2 and 3.5 in the other groups. About two thirds said price mattered, and more of them saw price as a sign of quality than of affordability.

In the store, farmers chose in 34 seconds when their variety was available and 78 seconds when it was not, with market leader buyers taking about 50 seconds longer. Few asked questions, and most ignored posters and price offers when their preferred variety was in stock. A multinomial logit model found that trait preferences, prior experience and farm size drove seed choice.

The authors conclude that loyalty rests on brand familiarity, past performance and agronomic traits. They argue that breeding must go beyond performance, that seed companies and extension should build trust in new varieties through demonstration plots, field days and early adopter testimonials, and that regulation could set timelines for phasing out outdated varieties.

## Methods in brief

- **Sampling.** Multistage, 3 of 8 sub-counties, 20 villages, up to 32 eligible farmers per village, target 50/50 gender split. Fieldwork February to March 2021, follow-up April to May 2023.
- **Experiment.** Mock agro-dealer store with real hybrids and prices, six cells crossing stock availability with information poster and price offer. 293 farmers in the in-stock treatment and 294 out of stock.
- **Data.** Survey on traits, experience and price attitudes, focus groups, recorded selection time and questions asked.
- **Analysis.** Chi-square and t-tests, multinomial logit with low-cost buyers as base category.

## Stated limitations

- Correlational design limits causal inference; experimental or longitudinal studies are needed.
- Recall of varieties grown over two years may be biased, though the recall period was kept short.
- Gender dynamics, extension access and social networks need further study.
