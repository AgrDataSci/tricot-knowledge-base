## Questions this paper answers

- What are the steps of a tricot project, from preparation to evaluation?
- How many varieties and participants should a first project include?
- How do I calculate seed quantities and prepare trial packages?
- How should observation questions be worded for farmers?
- How do I evaluate whether a tricot project worked?

## Summary

This is the first practical manual for implementing tricot, written for researchers, NGOs and extension organizations. It frames tricot as a way for farmers to adapt to a changing climate by finding farm-specific solutions, since no single variety suits all conditions. Each farmer receives a package with small quantities of three varieties drawn from a pool of up to 20, grows them on their own farm, and reports which performed best and worst.

The guide sets out the strengths of the method, including fast dissemination of new varieties, many households reached, and the ability to match unreleased varieties to the environments where they perform best. It is equally clear about limitations. Farmers may need initial training, some may not report back, a large number of participants must work independently, and some may find a variety they like that is not yet available.

A tricot project runs in ten steps. These are preparation, design, finding participants, an initial workshop with distribution of packages, planting, observation, data compilation, analysis, a final workshop, and evaluation. The guide explains each with roles for the implementing organization, field facilitators and farmers, and uses a case from the Yojoa area of Honduras, where an NGO ran a common bean project with 200 farmers.

Practical advice is specific. Start with 8 to 12 varieties. A recent study worked well with 100 observers and 12 varieties. The total number of seed bags equals participants times three, from which bags per variety follow. Workshops work best with no more than 20 farmers at a time. Questions must be unambiguous and in the words farmers use for their crops. ClimMob generates the randomized list of packages, the observation cards and the analysis reports, including an information sheet that tells each farmer the names of their three varieties and how they performed.

Evaluation uses simple indicators, including the share of farmers who participate again in the next cycle, which the guide treats as a clear signal of motivation. Participants also receive a practical agronomic lesson at the final workshop as an incentive.

The guide also models the farmer's experience. A participant receives a package with a code such as FR_30, plants the three varieties next to her own crop, fills an observation card through the season, and at the final workshop receives an information sheet revealing that her varieties A, B and C were, for example, Vaina Morada, Jamapa and Bayo, with their results. Stakeholder roles are defined for researchers, implementing organizations, field facilitators and farmers, and a glossary explains the method's terms for first-time users.

## Methods in brief

- **Ten steps.** Preparation, design, recruitment, initial workshop and package distribution, planting, observation, data compilation, analysis, final workshop, evaluation.
- **Design.** Three options per farmer from a pool of 8 to 12 (up to 20), randomized in ClimMob.
- **Packages.** Three bags with equal seed quantities, an observation card and instructions.
- **Data.** Observation card filled through the season, compiled by facilitators, analyzed in ClimMob.
- **Feedback.** Individual information sheets and a final workshop with harvest samples.

## Stated limitations

- Farmers may need training before they can run trials independently.
- Some participants will not report back, so attrition must be planned for.
- Varieties farmers prefer may not yet be available to them.
- Running everything at once in the analysis can be slow and hard to interpret, so covariates should be chosen carefully.
