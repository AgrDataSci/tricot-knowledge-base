## Questions this paper answers

- What are the steps of a tricot project, from preparation to evaluation?
- How many varieties and participants should a first project include?
- How do I calculate seed quantities and prepare trial packages?
- How should observation questions be worded for farmers?
- How do I evaluate whether a tricot project worked?

## Summary

This is a practical manual for implementing tricot, written for researchers, extension agents and NGO staff. It frames tricot as a way for farmers to adapt to a changing climate by finding farm-specific solutions, since no single variety suits all conditions. Each farmer receives a package with small quantities of three varieties from a pool of up to 20, grows them on their own farm, and reports which performed best and worst.

Strengths include fast dissemination of new varieties, many households reached, and matching unreleased varieties to the environments where they perform best. It is also open about limitations. Farmers may need training and assistance at first, which costs money. Some or all participants may find none of the tested varieties better than their own. The method needs many participants to work well, and if too few report back, the results may be of little use to researchers, although the trials can still help the farmers.

A tricot project runs in ten steps: preparation, design, finding participants, an initial workshop with distribution of packages, planting, observation, data compilation, analysis, a final workshop, and evaluation. The guide explains each with roles for the implementing organization, field facilitators and farmers, and uses a case from the Yojoa area of Honduras, where an NGO ran a common bean project with about 200 farmers.

Start with 8 to 12 varieties. For a first project, the guide advises around 100 to 200 participants, never more than the local facilitators can support (up to 25 each). Seed bags total participants times three. Divide by the number of varieties and round up to get bags per variety, then multiply by the seed per bag. Workshops work best with no more than 20 farmers at a time. Questions must be unambiguous and in the words farmers use for their crops. ClimMob generates the randomized list of packages, a data spreadsheet, the analysis report and an information sheet for each farmer with the names of their three varieties and those recommended for their farm. Its randomization includes every pair of varieties equally often, so packages should follow its list in order. Observation cards are adapted locally from a template on the ClimMob website. Several projects reward farmers with extra seed of the variety they prefer, which ties the incentive to the trial.

Evaluation uses five simple indicators, including repeat participation in the next cycle, which the guide treats as a clear signal of motivation. After each cycle, the lowest-ranked varieties can be replaced with new material. The final workshop also offers a practical agronomic lesson as an incentive.

The guide also models the farmer's experience. A participant receives a package with a code such as FR_30, plants the three varieties within her own production plot, fills an observation card through the season, and at the final workshop receives an information sheet revealing that her varieties A, B and C were Vaina Morada, Jamapa and Bayo, with their results. A glossary explains the method's terms for first-time users.

## Methods in brief

- **Ten steps.** Preparation, design, recruitment, initial workshop and package distribution, planting, observation, data compilation, analysis, final workshop, evaluation.
- **Design.** Three options per farmer from a pool of 8 to 12 (up to 20), randomized in ClimMob.
- **Packages.** Three bags with equal seed quantities, an observation card and instructions.
- **Planting.** Trial next to or within the production plot, on an average spot. All three varieties sown on the same day, side by side in equal rows (A left, B middle, C right), and managed exactly like the farmer's own crop.
- **Observation.** One characteristic at a time, best and worst of three, usually at three stages (early growth, later growth, after harvest). Yield and overall performance are always included, with no more than ten characteristics.
- **Data.** Observation card filled through the season, collected by local facilitators, then compiled and analyzed in ClimMob by the implementing organization.
- **Analysis.** Rankings for each characteristic, plus results by explanatory variables such as irrigation, altitude or gender, added one at a time.
- **Feedback.** Individual information sheets and a final workshop with harvest samples.
- **Evaluation.** Five indicators: rate of completed trials and causes of failure, gender ratio, rate of repeat participation, changes in seed choice, and changes in seed and information exchange.

## Stated limitations

- Farmers may need training and assistance at first, which requires an investment.
- Some or all participants may find none of the tested varieties better than their own.
- Tricot needs many participants to work well. If too few farmers report back, the results may not be useful for researchers, although the trials can still be useful to farmers.
- Practical caution (p. 28): including all explanatory variables in one analysis can take a very long time and give results that are hard to interpret, so the guide advises adding them one by one.

## Notes

- Superseded in parts by vanetten-2020-guide and the tricot book (chase-2026-book). Check those for current practice.
- Version 1 of May 2016. The guide does not call itself the first tricot manual. Its early date suggests it is one of the first (curator's note, outside the source).
- Source issue: the "recent study" with 100 observers and 12 varieties (p. 9) has no reference in the guide. Do not credit it to a specific paper without checking.
- Source issue: the bean seed example assumes six rows of 8 m per variety (120 g, p. 10), but the example farmer planted four rows of 8 m with the same 120 g (pp. 20, 23). Treat plot sizes as local choices, not a standard.
- Honduras example (pp. 8, 28): eight bean varieties, five field facilitators, eight characteristics, about 200 packages and data from more than a hundred trials. Chepe was preferred in the lowlands but was diseased at higher altitude. These are narrative examples, not a reported study.
