## Questions this paper answers

- Where does tricot come from, and how does it differ from PVS, mother-baby trials and diversity kits?
- What went wrong in the first tricot pilots, and what was changed?
- How should varieties be assigned and labelled in packages?
- How can women's participation be increased?
- What drives the cost of tricot?

## Summary

This is the first full description of tricot as a method, based on pilots run from 2011 under the Seeds for Needs initiative. It positions tricot within participatory variety selection, mother-and-baby trials, informal research and development, and diversity kits. Those approaches proved the value of farmer evaluation but stayed hard to integrate into breeding programmes because they need intensive facilitation.

Tricot combines elements of these approaches with crowdsourcing. Each farmer receives three varieties drawn at random from a larger set, grows them under normal management, and ranks them on a few simple questions. Many small, independent evaluations are aggregated statistically, and the trials are linked to geographic and climate data so results can be analysed against the environment. The design deliberately keeps the farmer's task minimal and individual, which makes it scalable but calls for extra effort to keep farmers motivated.

The pilots tested five aspects, namely data formats and statistical design, data relevance and quality, distributed weather data collection, farmer reception and motivation, and gender inclusion. Early data formats assumed too much literacy. Asking farmers to order three test varieties plus their local variety, with packages labelled 1, 2 and 3, caused confusion, since "number 3 is second best" is easily mixed up with "number 2 is third best". Most farmers needed field agents to fill in forms, often after harvest. Later versions used letters, best and worst questions, and a sequentially balanced, randomized assignment of varieties.

Reception was positive. In November 2014, 96% of Indian participants and all participants in the Ethiopian and Honduran pilots wanted to continue. Women made up 42% of participants in Honduras, where CIAL farmer research groups had a gender focus, while participation in India rose only after working through women's self-help groups. Comparison with expert rankings in Honduras supported the validity of farmer observations, with plants easier to judge than pests and diseases.

Staff time, not seed or materials, drove cost, and unit costs fell as more farmers joined. The authors announce ClimMob, a digital platform to support tricot, and plan interactive voice response by phone to reduce field visits further.

The design draws on several research traditions, including crowdsourcing and citizen science concepts, ethnobiological work on how farmers' knowledge varies with experience and the observability of plants, pests and diseases, and environmental analysis of trials linked to target population environments. Diversity kits had shown that three varieties per farmer works well, which informed the triadic design. The authors also anticipate interactive voice response, where farmers press a phone key to name the best variety, to reduce the need for field visits further.

## Methods in brief

- **Design.** Incomplete blocks of three varieties per farmer, assigned by a sequentially balanced, randomized procedure that also randomizes package labels.
- **Evaluation.** Simple best and worst rankings on a few questions, collected by field agents or phone.
- **Analysis.** Rankings aggregated statistically, with environmental covariates from geolocation.
- **Pilots.** Crop trials in several countries plus one pilot on distributed weather data collection.

## Stated limitations

- Early data formats were too complex for many farmers and needed field-agent help.
- The individual, low-contact design weakens the group learning that motivates farmers in other participatory methods.
- Women's participation depends heavily on local recruitment channels.
- Several results were reported in grey literature, with peer-reviewed follow-ups still to come.
