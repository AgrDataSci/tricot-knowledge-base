## Questions this paper answers

- Why do farmers in Africa adopt modern varieties of roots, tubers and bananas so slowly?
- How important are consumer preferences compared with agronomic traits in RTB adoption?
- Do gender differences in preferences explain slow adoption?
- Do informal seed systems help or hinder the spread of new varieties?
- What should breeding programmes and adoption surveys change?

## Summary

Roots, tubers and bananas are the main staples in much of humid sub-Saharan Africa, yet adoption of modern varieties has been slower than for cereals. In 2010, modern varieties covered about a third of the RTB area, with international centres behind three quarters of that. Graham Thiele and colleagues from the CGIAR Research Program on Roots, Tubers and Bananas, CIRAD, CIP, IITA, the Alliance and Cornell reviewed the literature to test three hypotheses about what drives varietal change, as part of the RTBfoods special issue.

They examined five case studies with the best adoption data, namely banana in Uganda, cassava in Nigeria, potato in Kenya, sweetpotato in Uganda and yam in Côte d'Ivoire.

The first hypothesis, that breeding programmes gave too little priority to consumer-preferred traits, was strongly confirmed. Where consumer traits lined up with good agronomy, turnover was fast, as with yam C18's suitability for foutou and potato Shangi's short cooking time. Landraces still dominate yam, banana and sweetpotato, and several of the most successful modern varieties are introduced or selected landraces or farmer selections (Florido and C18 yam, TME 419 cassava, Shangi potato). Seed traits also matter, such as Shangi's short dormancy or TME 419's straight stems that are easy to bundle. Farmers keep portfolios of varieties for different uses, so asking which one they prefer can mislead.

The second hypothesis, that neglect of gender differences in quality and post-harvest preferences weakened product profiles, remains unresolved because most case studies did not collect gender-disaggregated data. Where cassava studies looked, differences appeared in cyanogen content, ease of peeling and varieties suited to particular foods.

The third hypothesis, that informal seed systems slow uptake, gave mixed results. Informal systems spread C18 yam and Shangi potato rapidly, and most RTB planting material reaches farmers informally anyway. But inconsistent variety names cause frequent mix-ups. DNA fingerprinting in Nigeria showed many farmers misidentified whether they grew a landrace, and free vine distribution in Uganda may have undermined a working informal system.

The authors call for demand-led breeding with product profiles disaggregated by region and gender, using the RTBfoods five-step method, for faster release of farmer-preferred landraces where breeding is hard, and for more inclusive priority setting. They suggest tools such as tricot to let farmers' choices guide dissemination through informal systems. Adoption surveys should record trait preferences and gender, and use DNA fingerprinting, since breeder elicitations have overestimated adoption.

## Methods in brief

- **Design.** Literature review of varietal adoption in sub-Saharan Africa, starting from the 2010 DIIVA breeder elicitations and farmer surveys (Walker and Alwang 2015), followed by case studies for the countries with the best adoption data.
- **Framework.** Three hypotheses on consumer traits, gender and informal seed systems, assessed crop by crop.

## Stated limitations

- Few adoption studies record the traits behind adoption decisions or gender-disaggregated preferences, so hypothesis 2 cannot be fully tested.
- Breeder elicitations overestimate adoption, and farmer surveys misidentify varieties without DNA fingerprinting.

## Notes

- Part of the RTBfoods special issue. arnaud-2023-jsfa describes how RTBfoods data on consumer preferences were linked to breeding databases, and alamu-2023-jsfa and olaosebikan-2023-jsfa test cassava food quality with tricot consumer panels.
- Béla Teeken is a co-author. His cassava work shows clear gender differences in trait preferences (teeken-2021-fsufs), which this review could not yet draw on.
- Its call to let farmers' choices guide dissemination matches moyo-2021-fsufs (tricot consumer tests of sweetpotato) and mawia-2025-fsufs on seed choice and varietal loyalty in maize.
