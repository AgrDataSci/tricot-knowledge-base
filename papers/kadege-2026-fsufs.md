## Questions this paper answers

- Which traits do Tanzanian farmers use to choose common bean varieties?
- Which bean genotypes did farmers prefer in the Lake, Northern and Southern Highlands zones?
- Do women, men and youth prioritize different bean traits?
- Which new or context-specific traits should enter bean product profiles in Tanzania?
- How does tricot compare with participatory variety selection (PVS), and what are its drawbacks?

## Summary

Common bean is a major food and cash legume in Tanzania, yet adoption of improved varieties remains low, which the authors attribute in part to breeders' limited knowledge of farmers' trait preferences. Conventional PVS gathers farmers at a few centralized plots at maturity, so participants may not recall vegetative performance and do not represent the diversity of bean-growing environments. Edith Kadege and colleagues, in a TARI and CIAT study under a tricot PhD scholarship, used tricot to capture preferences across the season and across three agro-ecological zones.

Eighteen genotypes were tested, namely seven advanced breeding lines, ten released varieties and one farmer-preferred local cultivar (Boroto), chosen for anthracnose response and market class. Six districts with the highest anthracnose incidence were selected, two per zone, using incidence data from 1,682 earlier on-farm trials, and 62 farmers per district took part, 372 in total. Each farmer planted three blind-coded genotypes in an incomplete block design generated in ClimMob and managed them with their usual practices. Field agents supported data collection in Swahili with ODK.

Genotypes differed significantly for anthracnose resistance and grain yield. NUA 64 and NUA 48 (red mottled) were most preferred overall, especially in the Lake and Northern zones, while Sweet Violet and VTT923-23-10 (sugar beans) led in the Southern Highlands. Boroto ranked eighth of 18. New genotypes resembling well-known market varieties in colour, size and shape were more likely to be chosen. The authors link these patterns to cross-border trade, with red mottled beans demanded in Kenya and Uganda and sugar beans in Malawi and Zambia.

Across zones, high yield, market preference and disease resistance were the top three of 14 selection criteria. Market preference ranked first in the Lake and Southern Highlands zones and high yield first in the Northern zone, where disease resistance came second. Suitability for cooking with banana, cited only in the Lake zone, and compatibility with intercropping are reported as preferences documented for the first time in Tanzania.

Preferences differed by group. Women put post-harvest traits higher than men did (short cooking time, no flatulence, good taste, cooking with banana), alongside market preference and disease resistance, partly because unsold beans or diseased plots force them to rely on men's help. Men favoured yield, disease resistance, intercropping and early maturity, and youth favoured premium price, market preference and colour. Men made up 53.9% of participants.

The authors call for zone-specific, gender- and youth-responsive product profiles that add culinary, intercropping and market traits to yield and disease resistance, and for seed systems to prioritize market-linked grain types. They also list tricot's drawbacks relative to PVS and suggest follow-up surveys or focus groups to add depth.

## Methods in brief

- **Design.** Incomplete blocks of three genotypes per farmer from a pool of 18, randomized in ClimMob, single season, farmer-managed plots of four rows with 30 holes per row, two seeds per hole, 50 cm by 20 cm spacing.
- **Sampling.** Purposive choice of zones, districts and villages by bean prevalence and anthracnose incidence, then random selection among farmers with at least three seasons of bean experience, willingness to participate and a link to an NGO or extension officer.
- **Data.** Semi-structured ODK questionnaire in Swahili. Anthracnose scored by field agents on a 1 to 9 scale and converted to best and worst ranks. Yield ranked by comparing harvested grain volume in four-litre containers. Farmers ranked their preferred genotype and gave first, second and third reasons.
- **Analysis.** Plackett-Luce rankings in R, chi-square tests and preference plots of Pearson residuals across gender and zone, and cross-tabulation of reasons by demographic group.

## Stated limitations

- One season only, so multi-season trials are needed to confirm the patterns.
- The sample and coverage may miss local variation in preferences.
- The study rests on farmer perceptions rather than quantitative trait measurements, and the authors recommend pairing it with on-farm performance evaluations.
- As drawbacks of tricot, the authors list coarser data than PVS, the need for statistical expertise, heterogeneity when pooling many farmers, possible evaluation biases and the assumption that farmers have comparable evaluation skills, each with a suggested mitigation.

## Notes

- The abstract and results say Northern zone farmers ranked high yield first and disease resistance second, while the discussion says they treated disease resistance as the most important criterion. Use the results tables.
- Sampling is described as simple random, but farmers had to meet experience, willingness and extension-link criteria, so the sample is random only within that eligible group.
- The rankings for anthracnose and yield were derived from field-agent severity scores and grain-volume comparisons, not from free farmer judgement. Overall preference and reasons came from farmers.
- Table 2 is labelled ANOVA but reports log-likelihoods and chi-square probabilities. Treat it as a model test of genotype differences, without more detail on the model.
- Youth are reported as a third "gender category" next to women and men. The paper does not define the youth age range.
- The paper cites van Etten et al. (2019) for 100 to 200 farms giving reliable results with about 12 entries, the same guidance as chase-2026-book. This trial used 372 farmers for 18 entries.
- The tricot book interviews Edith Kadege about this work in its case studies (chase-2026-book). Kauê de Sousa is acknowledged for trial design but is not an author. See daudi-2026-fes for groundnut in Tanzania and voss-2023-fsoc and teeken-2021-fsufs on gender and trait preferences.
