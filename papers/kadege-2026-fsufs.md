## Questions this paper answers

- Which traits do Tanzanian farmers use to choose common bean varieties?
- Which bean genotypes did farmers prefer in the Lake, Northern and Southern Highlands zones?
- Do women, men and youth prioritize different bean traits?
- Which new or context-specific traits should enter bean product profiles in Tanzania?
- How does tricot compare with participatory variety selection (PVS), and what are its drawbacks?

## Summary

Common bean is a major food and cash legume in Tanzania, yet adoption of improved varieties remains low, which the authors attribute in part to breeders' limited knowledge of farmers' trait preferences. Conventional PVS gathers farmers at a few centralized plots at maturity, so participants may not recall vegetative performance and may not represent the diversity of bean-growing environments. Edith Kadege and colleagues from NM-AIST, TARI and CIAT, funded through a tricot PhD scholarship, used tricot to capture preferences across the season and across three agro-ecological zones.

Eighteen genotypes were tested, namely seven advanced breeding lines, ten released varieties and one farmer-preferred local cultivar (Boroto), chosen for anthracnose response and market class. Six districts with the highest anthracnose incidence were selected, two per zone, using incidence data from 1,682 earlier on-farm trials, and 62 farmers per district took part, 372 in total. Each farmer planted three blind-coded genotypes in an incomplete block design generated in ClimMob and managed them with their usual practices. Field agents supported data collection in Swahili with ODK.

Genotypes differed significantly for anthracnose resistance and grain yield. NUA 64 and NUA 48 (red mottled) were most preferred overall, especially in the Lake and Northern zones, while Sweet Violet and VTT923-23-10 (sugar beans) led in the Southern Highlands. Boroto ranked eighth of 18. New genotypes resembling well-known market varieties in colour, size and shape were more likely to be chosen. The authors link these patterns to cross-border trade, with red mottled beans demanded in Kenya and Uganda and sugar beans in Malawi and Zambia.

Across zones, high yield, market preference and disease resistance were the top three of 14 selection criteria. Market preference ranked first in the Lake and Southern Highlands zones and high yield first in the Northern zone, where disease resistance came second. Farmers preferred the market-favoured Calima Uyole and Uyole 18 less because of their high anthracnose susceptibility. Suitability for cooking with banana, cited only in the Lake zone, and compatibility with intercropping are reported as preferences documented for the first time in Tanzania.

Preferences differed by group. Women put post-harvest traits higher than men did (short cooking time, no flatulence, good taste, cooking with banana), although their top three reasons were market preference, disease resistance and high yield. In interviews, women explained that aggregators collect market-preferred beans at the farm gate, while other beans and diseased plots need men's help, which can delay sales and spraying. Men favoured yield, disease resistance and market preference, then intercropping and early maturity, and youth favoured premium price, market preference and colour. Men made up 53.9% of participants.

The authors call for zone-specific, gender- and youth-responsive product profiles that add culinary, intercropping and market traits to yield and disease resistance, and for seed systems to prioritize market-linked grain types. They also list tricot's drawbacks relative to PVS and suggest follow-up surveys or focus groups to add depth.

## Methods in brief

- **Design.** Incomplete blocks of three genotypes per farmer from a pool of 18, randomized in ClimMob, single season, farmer-managed plots of four rows with 30 holes per row, two seeds per hole, 50 cm by 20 cm spacing.
- **Sampling.** Purposive choice of zones, districts and villages by bean prevalence and anthracnose incidence, then random selection among farmers with at least three seasons of bean experience, willingness to participate and a link to an NGO or extension officer. The six districts were Karatu and Siha (Northern), Kyerwa and Misenyi (Lake), and Mbarali and Njombe (Southern Highlands).
- **Data.** Semi-structured ODK questionnaire in Swahili. Anthracnose scored by field agents on a 1 to 9 scale and converted to best and worst ranks. Yield ranked by comparing harvested grain volume in four-litre containers. Farmers ranked their preferred genotype and gave first, second and third reasons.
- **Analysis.** Plackett-Luce rankings in R, chi-square tests and preference plots of Pearson residuals across gender and zone, and cross-tabulation of reasons by demographic group.

## Stated limitations

- One season only, so multi-season trials are needed to confirm the patterns.
- The sample and coverage may miss local variation in preferences.
- The study recorded farmer perceptions rather than quantitative measurements of trait performance, so future work should add on-farm performance evaluations to validate the traits farmers named.
- Future studies should link on-farm traits to consumer, market and nutrition needs and add climate-resilience traits such as drought and heat tolerance.

## Notes

- The results (Table 4 and the text on page 5) say Northern zone farmers ranked high yield first and disease resistance second, while the discussion (page 6) says they treated disease resistance as the most important criterion. Use the results tables.
- Source problem: the text says women ranked post-harvest traits highest, but in Table 5 their top three reasons are market preference (87), disease resistance (82) and high yield (79). Post-harvest traits follow at ranks 4 to 7.
- Source problem: the reason counts do not fit the design. With 372 farmers giving three reasons each, the maximum is 1,116, and Table 3 sums to 1,101. But the Northern zone in Table 4 has 434 reasons and 140 first reasons from only 124 farmers (two districts of 62). Table 5 sums to 1,392 reasons, and women, men and youth together give 467 first reasons, so youth are probably also counted as women or men. Trait totals in Table 5 differ from Table 3 (high yield 275 against 236). Compare groups by rank, not by raw counts. (Our calculation from Tables 3 to 5.)
- Source problem: the 53.9% and 46.1% shares do not fit 372 farmers (53.9% of 372 is 200.5, our calculation). The paper does not give the denominator or say how youth relate to the women and men counts.
- Source problem: the paper names the PlackettLuce package but reports only rank positions (Figure 1). It gives no worth estimates, confidence intervals or test of overall preference between genotypes, and no ranking of genotypes for yield or disease. The abstract still calls NUA 48, NUA 64, Sweet Violet and VTT923-23-10 top performers for yield.
- Source problem: the paper sometimes calls its own tricot activities PVS (abstract, section 4.2) and says PVS was conducted in Karatu. Read these as the tricot trial unless stated otherwise.
- Sampling is described as simple random, but farmers had to meet experience, willingness and extension-link criteria, so the sample is random only within that eligible group.
- The rankings for anthracnose and yield were derived from field-agent severity scores and grain-volume comparisons, not from free farmer judgement. Overall preference and reasons came from farmers.
- Table 2 is labelled ANOVA but reports log-likelihoods, F values, about 1,090 degrees of freedom and chi-square probabilities. Treat it as a model test of genotype differences, without more detail on the model.
- Zone and gender interact. Women in the Lake and Southern Highlands zones favour post-harvest traits, women in the Northern zone favour agronomic traits, and men and youth in the Southern Highlands focus on agronomic traits (text on page 5, Figure 2).
- SMC 18 was preferred in the Lake zone for cooking with banana because of its white colour. RWR 2154 and VTT923-23-10 were named for intercropping in the Northern and Southern Highlands zones, where farmers prefer bush types.
- Youth are reported as a third "gender category" next to women and men. The paper does not define the youth age range.
- The paper cites van Etten et al. (2019) for 100 to 200 farms giving reliable results with about 12 entries, the same guidance as chase-2026-book. This trial used 372 farmers for 18 entries.
- The tricot book interviews Edith Kadege about this work in its case studies (chase-2026-book). See daudi-2026-fes for groundnut in Tanzania and voss-2023-fsoc and teeken-2021-fsufs on gender and trait preferences.
