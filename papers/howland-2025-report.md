## Questions this paper answers

- Did 1000FARMS help scale the use of tricot data for breeding decisions, and where?
- Which breeding and seed system decisions do national programmes actually take with tricot data?
- How far has tricot entered official breeding guidelines and variety release processes?
- What drives a breeding programme to adopt tricot, and what stops it?
- How do breeders use ClimMob in practice, and what do they want from it?
- What makes collaboration with field agents and farmers work in tricot trials?
- Where do the experiences of national breeders differ from what the tricot literature claims?

## Summary

This is an independent outcome study of the 1000FARMS project, run by the PISA4i evaluation team of the Alliance of Bioversity International and CIAT between July 2025 and January 2026. It asks two questions. First, how much 1000FARMS activities contributed to system-level change in the use of tricot data for breeding decisions in Ethiopia, Ghana and Uganda, plus Haiti as a country the project did not target directly. Second, which lessons emerge on tricot and on collaborative arrangements.

The team reconstructed the 1000FARMS theory of change and tested it against rival explanations through contribution analysis. Evidence came from an online survey of 195 respondents (from about 1,800 contacts), 21 remote interviews across the four countries, and project documents. Nigeria was left out because its tricot scaling is already well documented, and Benin dropped out after a single reply.

Awareness is high. Of the respondents, 96% knew on-farm testing, 94% tricot and 88% ClimMob. Some 64% trusted tricot data more than data from other on-farm methods, 64% of applicable respondents said their programme used tricot data for breeding decisions, and 66% used ClimMob. Programme leadership decision was the most cited driver of use (60%), ahead of evidence from 1000FARMS and training. Some 42% reported tricot-inspired protocols in official breeding guidelines, though interviews gave a less clear picture.

Country cases show tricot data informing variety recommendations, release dossiers and seed promotion. EIAR breeders recommended two wheat and two teff varieties for multiplication. Ghanaian institutes in the north and south tested the same genotypes with farmers and consumers. Prioritized sweet potato varieties entered official technology packages in Haiti. Ugandan breeders gained the confidence and data to build release dossiers. Ethiopia was rated the most advanced, with universities and an agricultural institute funding tricot from their own resources, helped by an earlier start.

The contribution of 1000FARMS was rated commingled in Ghana and Uganda, where CIP, CIAT, AVISA, RTB, NextGen Cassava and other programmes acted alongside it. It was rated weak in Ethiopia and Haiti. Weak here means these countries received little direct project support, yet national universities and institutes still implemented tricot, drawing on earlier Dutch-funded projects, Seeds for Needs and USAID Innovation Labs. This shows national partners able to run tricot on their own, and 1000FARMS reaching actors beyond its project scope, mainly through its webinars and online resources. Its distinct role elsewhere was awareness, training, ad hoc support, grants and links with other programmes, though not all informants knew the project by name.

Lessons cover funding dependency, the weight of breeding directors, the need for incentives and formal arrangements with field agents, feedback to farmers, and weak seed systems that block the scaling of preferred varieties. Breeders valued ClimMob for design and data collection but often exported data to R. The study ends by noting a gap between published claims, such as cost-effectiveness and data quality, and what national implementers report.

## Methods in brief

- **Design.** Theory-based evaluation using contribution analysis, following Howland et al. (2024), with six components rated per country (impact trajectory, explanatory mechanism, statement, body of evidence, strength of evidence, strength of contribution).
- **Survey.** Online closed-ended survey sent to about 1,800 project contacts, 195 responses (Ethiopia 20, Uganda 18, Ghana 8, Haiti 3, other countries 146); descriptive statistics and a bigram analysis of the open question on decisions influenced by tricot data.
- **Interviews.** 21 semi-structured remote interviews (Ethiopia 5, Ghana 8, Haiti 2, Uganda 6) with NARES, university, CGIAR and NGO breeders, socio-economists and managers.
- **Secondary data.** Project proposal, documents, blog posts and websites, used to triangulate.
- **Case selection.** Countries targeted by 1000FARMS with reported tricot use (Ethiopia, Ghana, Uganda), Haiti to test spillover; Nigeria excluded as already documented, Benin excluded for low response.

## Stated limitations

- Purposive sampling of informants may bias the primary evidence.
- Few informants per country in both the survey and the interviews, so representativeness is limited, notably in Haiti (3 survey respondents, 2 interviews) and Ghana (8 respondents).
- The survey response rate was low, partly because mailing lists held duplicates and one-off webinar participants.
- Contributions are hard to attribute because 1000FARMS was designed to work through other CGIAR and funder programmes.
- The evaluation may come too early to detect spillover in non-target countries such as Haiti.
- Interview findings on enablers and barriers are informants' perceptions.

## Notes

- `papers-pdf/howland-et-al-2025.pdf` is a one-page pointer to the CGSpace record and the reference, not the report. The card was checked against the full 117-page report.
- The authors are the Alliance's PISA4i evaluation team, not the tricot team, so the card is `source: external`. It is the main independent assessment of 1000FARMS and the best source in the knowledge base for how national breeders experience tricot. Follow "Fair disagreement" in `SKILL.md` where it diverges from team papers.
- A "weak" contribution rating in Ethiopia and Haiti does not mean 1000FARMS failed there. It means the project gave little direct support, while national partners implemented tricot by themselves, which points to stand-alone national capacity and to 1000FARMS reaching beyond its scope. In the report's own terms, the Haiti spillover is shown for knowledge through webinars, not yet for implementation.
- The citation year is 2025, but fieldwork ran from July 2025 to January 2026 and the PDF is dated February 2026.
- Survey percentages use different denominators per question (195, 163, 143 or 89). The 64% use figure excludes respondents who skipped the question.
- Some numbers differ slightly between text and tables, for example Ghana's guideline integration given as 12% and 12.5% (1 of 8). Rounding in the tables also makes some rows sum above 100%. Use the counts, not the percentages.
- The report says BMGF promotes "on-farm trial verification", which it treats as another name for tricot and flags as a source of confusion for national partners.
- In Ghana a NARES director disagreed with farmers' prioritization in tricot trials, and breeders stopped the approach. In Uganda the NARO rice programme dropped tricot as too costly. Both cases matter when answering questions on institutionalization.
- For the network's own view of mainstreaming, see 1000farms-2025-report. For the cost and impact case the informants partly contest, see desousa-2024-asd. For evidence on the accuracy of farmer data, see nabateregga-2025-eja and ulzen-2025-agrirxiv.
