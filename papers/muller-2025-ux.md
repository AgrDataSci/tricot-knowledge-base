## Questions this paper answers

- Does a redesigned feedback sheet work for tricot farmers?
- Do farmers understand ranking visuals and trait icons?
- How do farmers and implementers rate the new materials?
- Can feedback sheets also serve as evidence for variety release?

## Summary

The deck reports the redesign and field validation of tricot farmer feedback, following the 2024 review (schumann-2024-ux). The design requirements were to show farmers the variety rankings and address seed availability, use icons and visuals, prompt discussion, communicate in local languages, and stay cheap and easy for implementers to produce.

The tested feedback sheet had modules for the farmer's personal information, overall and regional variety rankings, recommendations from implementers on planting and managing varieties, and communication channels between institutions and farmers. A separate participation diploma recognized farmers' contribution. Validation ran in Nigeria with NRCRI and IITA on a cassava trial of eight varieties, with 44 participants in ten communities across five states (Oyo, Osun, Abia, Imo and Akwa Ibom) and sessions in three languages.

Farmers valued the sheet. 70% of farmers rated its usefulness at 4 or 5 out of 5, no one chose the lowest score, and 67% could accurately discuss at least one section. Farmers' Net Promoter Score was 70.45, rated excellent, and implementers' was 33.3, good but with room to improve. Icons remained the weak point. Only 48% of farmers decoded trait icons correctly, and elaborate pictograms worked better than simple ones, with icons for visible everyday traits clearest. Farmers had not expected a diploma, but once they received it they valued it and asked for larger, framed versions.

Implementers reported that the sheet let them talk less because farmers grasped the information, condensed twelve months of data, and served as documented proof for the variety release committee that farmers were at the center. Feedback sessions strengthened trust in the institutions and the trial. The authors conclude the redesigned sheet is largely effective, while icons and the robustness and speed of producing materials need more work.

The validation combined quantitative and qualitative methods to answer several research questions, namely whether the new design makes sessions more attractive for implementers, what farmers expect from sessions, whether the diploma conveys recognition, whether farmers understand the visualizations and icons, and how both groups rate the materials on standard user-experience indicators. Using a single sheet of paper also simplified transport and reduced costs. The authors conclude that feedback sessions do more than return results, since they build trust in research institutions and in the quality of the trial, and they identify icon design and faster production of durable materials as the next things to fix. The work is part of an inclusive design effort within the Alliance.

## Methods in brief

- **Design.** Requirements from co-design with farmers and implementers, modular feedback sheet plus diploma.
- **Validation.** Feedback sessions in 10 communities, 5 states, 44 farmers, 3 languages.
- **Metrics.** Usefulness and ease-of-use scales, comprehension and icon matching rates, Net Promoter Score for farmers and implementers, qualitative observation.

## Stated limitations

- A small validation sample (44 farmers) in one country and crop.
- Icon comprehension was low and needs redesign.
- Producing durable materials quickly remains a constraint.
