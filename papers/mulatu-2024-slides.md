## Questions this paper answers

- Can tricot evaluate forage mixtures, not just single varieties?
- Which forage traits do Ethiopian farmers assess and value?
- How do improved forages and mixtures compare with the reference grass Desho?
- What enables scaling of improved forages?

## Summary

Livestock support the livelihoods of about 70% of Ethiopia's population, but about 90% of feed comes from traditional grazing, mostly on poorly managed natural pastures. Overgrazing makes livestock a main driver of land degradation. Improved forages can provide better feed and help restore land, and farmers' participation in choosing them is key to adoption, so the team used tricot to identify farmer-preferred multipurpose forage options.

Sites were chosen in mid-altitude mixed farming systems across agroecologies with dairy potential, forage scarcity and earlier experience promoting improved forages, including Dangla and Dera in Amhara and Bilate in Sidama. Woreda development agents were trained to run the trials. Each farmer received a package with three separate treatments, drawn from single grasses, single legumes and grass-legume mixtures. The options included the grasses Mombasa, Mulato II, Cayman and Desho, the legumes vetch, stylo, lablab, desmodium and crotalaria, and combinations of them.

Farmers assessed a long list of traits with best and worst questions at the first and second harvest. These covered establishment and ease of sowing, plant height, soil cover, plant, grass and legume populations, weeds, greenness, disease, pest and drought resistance, leaf-to-stem ratio, fertilizer needs, soil retention and soil fertility improvement, yield, ease of harvesting, livestock preference, ease of transplanting and overall preference.

Biomass was analyzed separately for Amhara (383 farmers) and SNNP (354 farmers), as the probability of each option outperforming the reference forage Desho. Kendall correlations showed which traits were most linked to overall preference at each harvest, and Plackett-Luce log-worth estimates by trait highlighted the best options.

The lessons point to scaling mixtures to similar suitable environments, and to the value of engaging farmers in technology selection for new insights and easier scaling. The authors note that familiarity with existing varieties influences preferences, that biophysical and socio-economic factors shape both performance and preference, and that options suit different niches. Next steps include studying the environmental and socio-economic impacts of replacing the traditional feed basket with improved forages. Availability of forage seed and extension services are key enabling factors.

## Methods in brief

- **Sites.** Mid-altitude mixed farming systems in Amhara and SNNP/Sidama, selected for forage potential and scarcity.
- **Design.** Tricot packages of three options from grasses, legumes and mixtures, run by trained woreda development agents.
- **Evaluation.** Best-worst rankings for about 20 traits at first and second harvest.
- **Analysis.** Plackett-Luce models, probability of outperforming Desho, Kendall correlations with overall preference.

## Stated limitations

- Preferences are influenced by farmers' familiarity with existing varieties.
- Slides present results without full statistics. Look for a peer-reviewed paper before citing findings.
