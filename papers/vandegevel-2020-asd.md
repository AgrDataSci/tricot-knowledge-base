## Questions this paper answers

- What did participatory agricultural research promise, and why has it not become mainstream?
- What is citizen science, and how does it differ from earlier participatory research?
- How can attention to participants' motivations change project design?
- What roles can participants take beyond "more participation is better"?
- What are the risks of digital citizen science for social inclusion?

## Summary

Participatory research has been part of agricultural science since the early 1980s, promising more relevant technologies, empowerment and sustainability. Yet after four decades it remains outside the mainstream of technology development, and in plant breeding it is still not standard. Jeske van de Gevel and Jacob van Etten, with Sebastian Deterding of the Digital Creativity Labs at the University of York, review the literature to ask whether citizen science can renew it.

They first set out the promises. Participation can make research more effective and efficient, empower farmers and support sustainability, though benefits are hard to capture in cost-benefit ratios. They then explain why it stalled. Institutions lack learning and knowledge management, staff turnover is high, incentives reward data production over impact, and accountability to end users is weak. Participatory findings, such as those from participatory variety selection, have no established route into research decisions. Researchers also did little research on participation itself, its designs, motivations and evaluation, and the field held a one-dimensional view that more participation is always better.

Citizen science, a term that spread over the previous 15 years, covers public involvement in research, often enabled by digital tools. It promises a "double win" of efficient knowledge generation and learning for participants. In agriculture, examples include crowdsourced crop variety evaluation with ClimMob and digital advisory tools such as PlantVillage. The authors identify four opportunities it opens. It brings interdisciplinary collaboration with designers and human-computer interaction researchers. It rethinks socio-computational configurations, distributing tasks across many participants with differentiated roles, from passive data contribution to co-design. It connects to research on democratizing science. And it creates new accountabilities to participants.

A central point is motivation. Citizen science studies why people take part, and the authors argue projects should align the kinds of work people do with what motivates them, designing the participant experience rather than assuming participation is its own reward.

They caution that citizen science is not simply cheaper. It shifts costs to citizen scientists and adds new ones for digital tools, data verification and community management. Unequal access to digital tools and literacy creates new inclusion risks, and extractive forms remain possible. With persistent institutional support and investment, they conclude, citizen science can change how agricultural science engages farming communities and fulfil more of the promises of participation.

## Methods in brief

- **Design.** Critical literature review of participatory agricultural research and citizen science across disciplines.
- **Structure.** Promises of participation, barriers to mainstreaming, and four new impulses from citizen science (definitions, motivation and engagement, rethinking participation, digital tools and inclusion), with a table of citizen science examples by discipline.

## Stated limitations

- Benefits of participatory and citizen science are hard to capture in cost-benefit ratios.
- Citizen science externalizes costs and introduces new ones, so cost savings alone will not drive adoption.
- Digital divides and extractive designs can undermine inclusion.
