## Questions this paper answers

- How accurately can farmers measure harvest output in on-farm trials?
- Is volumetric measurement better than weighing with a scale?
- Does training farmers to use a scale fix weighing errors?
- Do education, gender, eyesight or experience affect farmers' accuracy?
- What error should researchers expect when farmers report yield?

## Summary

Decentralized trials reach remote communities and cut costs, but harvesting and measuring many plots at once usually needs technicians on site. Researchers fall back on technicians partly because farmers' accuracy in measuring harvest is unknown. The study was motivated by the need to add metric yield to tricot, which relies mainly on rankings, and tests how accurately farmers measure harvest output once it is in hand.

The team worked with 40 farmers in five cocoa- and maize-growing communities in Ghana's Western North and Ashanti regions, all members of the Kookoo Pa farmer association and participants in its tricot trials. Farmers did four exercises with maize grain and cocoa beans. They ranked sacks by lifting them, ranked and estimated the weight of heaps by eye, measured volume with tin cans of known size, and weighed samples with a butcher's scale. Twenty farmers in two communities received hands-on training with a manual before weighing, and the others served as controls. A questionnaire and a Snellen eye chart recorded factors that might affect accuracy.

The literature review sets expectations. Crop cuts are costly and tend to overestimate yield, while farmers' estimates after harvest came within a few percent of true weights in earlier studies, and aggregated farmer volumes in Kenya matched researcher data closely. Lifting and visual comparison gave useful benchmarks. Farmers told the heavier of two bags apart correctly 75% of the time when the difference exceeded 6.3% to 8.5% of the reference weight. Visual estimates of absolute heap weight were less accurate but had little bias. Tin can measurement had the lowest error for almost every metric, with relative root mean squared deviations of 7.4% for maize and 6.7% for cocoa, and its accuracy did not differ by gender, education, eyesight or experience. Weighing was the most error-prone. Untrained farmers did no better than visual estimation (41.2% relative error for maize), and none of them completed all weighing steps correctly. Training raised correct execution to 55% for cocoa and 45% for maize and reduced errors, but taring mistakes, such as forgetting to subtract the container weight, persisted.

The authors conclude that volumetric measurement with a container of known volume is the practical choice where training is limited. Weighing could improve with electronic scales and procedures that avoid taring. They argue that farmer measurement can extend trial reach, reduce bias toward accessible communities, and free technician time, possibly with technicians measuring a subsample. The results agree with the parallel study of farmer volumetric yield in Tanzania (nabateregga-2025-eja).

## Methods in brief

- **Participants.** 40 farmers in five communities, mobilized by Kookoo Pa extension agents from ongoing tricot trials.
- **Exercises.** Lifting sacks, visual estimation of heaps, tin can volumetric measurement, weighing with a butcher's scale, each for maize and cocoa.
- **Training.** 20 farmers trained in weighing with a manual, 20 untrained controls.
- **Analysis.** Thurstonian models for just noticeable differences, RMSD, MAD and MSD against true weights, and regressions on farmer characteristics.

## Stated limitations

- Moisture content was not measured, so outputs cannot be converted to dry-matter yield.
- The exercises used small quantities and did not cover area estimation or separating marketable product.
- A preprint, not yet peer reviewed.
