## Questions this paper answers

- How accurately can farmers measure harvest output in on-farm trials?
- Is volumetric measurement better than weighing with a scale?
- Does training farmers to use a scale fix weighing errors?
- Do education, gender, eyesight or experience affect farmers' accuracy?
- What error should researchers expect when farmers report yield?

## Summary

Decentralized trials reach remote communities and cut costs, but harvesting and measuring many plots at once usually needs technicians on site. Researchers fall back on technicians partly because farmers' accuracy in measuring harvest is unknown. The study was motivated by the need to add metric yield to tricot, which relies mainly on rankings, and tests how accurately farmers measure harvest output once it is in hand.

The team worked with 40 farmers in five cocoa- and maize-growing communities in Ghana's Western North and Ashanti regions, all members of the Kookoo Pa farmer association and participants in its tricot trials. Farmers did four exercises with maize grain and cocoa beans. They ranked sacks by lifting them, ranked and estimated the weight of heaps by eye, measured volume with tin cans of known size, and weighed samples with a butcher's scale. Twenty farmers in two communities received hands-on training with a manual before weighing, and the others served as controls. A questionnaire and a Snellen eye chart recorded factors that might affect accuracy.

The literature review sets expectations. Crop cuts are costly and tend to overestimate yield. In one five-country study, farmers' visual estimates after harvest overestimated output by only 3% on average. Aggregated farmer volumes in Kenya matched researcher data closely.

In this study, lifting and visual comparison gave useful benchmarks. Farmers told the heavier of two bags apart correctly 75% of the time when the difference exceeded 6.3% to 8.5% of the reference weight. Visual estimates of absolute heap weight were less accurate but had little bias. Tin can measurement had the lowest error for almost every metric, with relative root mean squared deviations (RMSD) of 7.4% for maize and 6.7% for cocoa. In exploratory regressions, its accuracy showed no clear link to gender, education, eyesight or experience. The remaining bias came from how farmers filled the can, and it was consistent, so the authors suggest recalibration. With this correction and better reporting of can fractions, they expect a mean absolute deviation of 2% to 3% or lower. Weighing gave the largest errors among untrained farmers and did not consistently beat visual estimation. Untrained farmers had a relative RMSD of 41.2% for maize, worse than visual estimation, and none of them completed all weighing steps correctly. Training raised correct execution to 55% for cocoa and 45% for maize and cut the relative RMSD to 18.8% for maize and 7.6% for cocoa. Taring mistakes, such as forgetting to subtract the container weight, and confusing pounds with kilograms persisted.

The authors conclude that volumetric measurement with a container of known volume is the practical choice where training is limited. Volumes still need conversion to weight, for example by weighing a small subset of samples. Weighing could improve with electronic scales and procedures that avoid taring. They argue that farmer measurement can extend trial reach, reduce bias toward accessible communities, and free technician time, possibly with technicians measuring a subsample. The results agree with a parallel study of farmer volumetric yield measurement (nabateregga-2025-eja).

## Methods in brief

- **Participants.** 40 farmers in five communities, mobilized by Kookoo Pa extension agents from ongoing tricot trials.
- **Exercises.** Lifting sacks, visual estimation of heaps, tin can volumetric measurement, weighing with a butcher's scale, each for maize and cocoa.
- **Training.** 20 farmers in Addaikrom and Kojofordjourkrom trained in weighing with a ten-step manual. 20 untrained controls came from the other three communities.
- **Quantities.** Sacks of 20 kg (cocoa) and 25 kg (maize), with others ±6% and ±15%. Heaps of 64.3 kg (cocoa) and 25 kg (maize), with others ±5% and ±10%. The same 64.3 kg and 25 kg lots were measured with a tin can 15 cm wide and 12 cm high. Scale samples were 4 kg (cocoa) and 2.5 kg (maize).
- **Setup.** One lot of each crop per community, so moisture was uniform within a community. Farmers waited 50 m away and came one at a time.
- **Analysis.** Thurstonian models for just noticeable differences, RMSD, MAD and MSD against true weights, and regressions on farmer characteristics.

## Stated limitations

- Moisture content was not measured, so outputs cannot be converted to dry-matter yield.
- The exercises used prepared lots of known weight, not real plot harvests. The authors note that the parallel bean study used a more realistic setup. Area estimation and separating marketable product were left out.
- Training results may be optimistic, since in real trials farmers weigh harvests months after training.
- Scales were tested on single small samples (2.5 kg maize, 4 kg cocoa). Weighing a whole plot harvest in batches and adding the numbers by hand could add errors.
- A preprint, not yet peer reviewed.

- The PDF is filed as Ulzen-et-al-2024, but the agriRxiv DOI is from 2025. Replace with the published version when available, as was done for dorado-2026-tag.
- The preprint cites Nabateregga et al. 2024 (SSRN preprint) as r² = 0.95 between farmers' tin can estimates and researcher-determined weights. The published paper (nabateregga-2025-eja) reports r = 0.96 for farmer versus technician volumes and R² = 0.901 for predicting weight from farmer volumes. These are different comparisons. Cite the published values with their own definitions.
- Outside knowledge: the parallel study cited as Nabateregga et al. 2024 was done in Tanzania (see nabateregga-2025-eja). The preprint does not name the country.
- For tin cans, the authors suggest choosing can size in proportion to expected yield, drawing fraction lines inside the can, or using a large and a small can (p. 20).
- The authors suggest farmers estimate heap size before counting cans, as a check against large outliers (p. 21).

- Source problem: citation years differ between text and reference list (Verma et al. 1991 vs 1988, Casley and Kumar 1998 and 1989 vs 1988).
