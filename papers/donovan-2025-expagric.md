## Questions this paper answers

- How can breeders test product ideas with farmers before breeding them?
- What is product concept testing, and how does it use triadic comparisons?
- Which future maize hybrid concepts do Kenyan and Ugandan farmers prefer?
- How do farm context and market orientation shape concept preference?

## Summary

Public breeding has delivered major gains through step-change product design, but there is little guidance on how to identify forward-looking product opportunities. Drawing on product innovation literature, the authors apply product concept testing to hybrid maize in Kenya and Uganda, testing ideas for future seed products before investing in breeding them.

Through interactions with seed companies, breeders and farmers, the team defined eight maize seed product concepts, namely resilience (the current breeding target), drought escape, food and fodder, home use, green maize, livestock feed, intercropping and family nutrition. Each concept was presented in a short video in the local language (Luganda in Uganda, Swahili in Kenya), with 18 videos produced per country. Following tricot logic, each of 2,400 farmers watched three concept videos and ranked them. Focus groups of six to nine farmers, separated by sex, helped develop the concepts.

Farmers were most likely to choose resilience in both countries, drought escape in Uganda and intercropping in Kenya. Interest in home use, food and fodder and other concepts was mixed, suggesting they would need investment in product promotion and seed production as well as breeding.

Context explained differences between countries. Over 90% of farmers in Kenya bought seed at least once a year, compared with under 50% in Uganda. More than 80% of Kenyan farmers intercropped, mainly with beans. Ugandan farmers sold 88% of their maize harvest, while Kenyan farmers consumed about half of theirs.

The authors present concept testing as an entry point for transdisciplinary discussions on breeding priorities and market segments at regional and national levels.

The strong result for resilience confirms the current CGIAR and NARES priority of incremental yield gains under adverse smallholder conditions. Drought escape and intercropping point to new opportunities for product design. Public breeding for hybrid maize in East Africa has long focused on intermediate-maturity, drought-tolerant hybrids for mid-altitude areas and designs early-maturing hybrids only for dryland zones. Yet many mid-altitude farmers in this study were willing to trade some yield for early maturity, which gives flexibility to plant later and reduces exposure to late-season drought. The authors recommend that public programmes and seed companies test early-maturity hybrids in higher-rainfall zones and make them available there. Other evidence supports this, including farmers in western Kenya adopting early maturity as a climate adaptation strategy and agrodealer sales data showing farmers in wetter areas buying early-maturity seed despite lower yield potential. Concept testing thus tests ideas before breeding invests in them.

## Methods in brief

- **Concept development.** Interactions with seed companies, breeders and farmers, plus sex-disaggregated focus groups of 6 to 9 farmers.
- **Presentation.** Short videos of each concept in local languages, 18 per country.
- **Design.** Each farmer viewed three concepts in a balanced incomplete design and ranked them.
- **Analysis.** Plackett-Luce models of concept rankings with farmer and household covariates.

## Stated limitations

- Stated preferences for concepts may differ from purchase behavior once seed is available.
- Concepts with mixed interest may reflect weak understanding of new uses rather than low value.
