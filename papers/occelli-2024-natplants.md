## Questions this paper answers

- How have crop breeding programmes collected and analysed farmers' trait preferences since 1980?
- Which tools are used most to elicit trait priorities, and where do on-farm trials and tricot fit?
- How often do trait preference studies disaggregate by sex, age or region?
- Why can't findings from different trait preference studies be pooled?
- What should a robust trait prioritization study include?
- Who funds and authors this literature, and is it siloed?

## Summary

Public breeding programmes are trying to become demand-led, which starts with knowing which traits growers, processors and consumers want and turning that into target product profiles. M. Occelli, H. A. Tufan and colleagues, most of them at Cornell University, asked what tools and methods underpin trait prioritization studies. The authors say these methods are seldom examined, and an earlier review of preference studies covered rice only. They hypothesized that weak attention to methods gives biased data and blocks data-driven breeding decisions.

They ran a systematic scoping review following PRISMA-ScR, registered on OSF, searching five scholarly databases and 12 grey literature sources with no date limit, English only. From 17,786 records, 657 studies entered data extraction with a 40-question framework. The results describe 564 original studies, 83% peer-reviewed, more than half published since 2016.

Coverage is uneven. Ethiopia and India have the most studies, 57% are in sub-Saharan Africa, only 6% in South America and 2% in the Middle East and North Africa. Cereals dominate, while roots, tubers, bananas, fruits and vegetables, which matter for nutrition, get less attention.

Most studies (58%) ask farmers directly through individual surveys or focus groups. Direct experience, such as PVS, demonstration plots and on-farm trials, comes second (24%). In Fig. 2, tricot is a very small slice inside the direct questions category, not under direct experience. Analysis is mostly frequency counts and hypothesis tests. Only 5% analyse qualitative data, even though focus groups are among the most used tools. More than a third of studies use a single tool, and another third use two.

Designs are weak. 12% do not report how many people took part, 52% involve only farmers without saying whether they are also consumers or producers, and fewer than 19% include consumers, traders or other seed system actors. Many studies sample roughly 250 respondents regardless of crop, country or method. Sample size calculations were often overlooked, which may reflect habit and practicality rather than robust design. Of studies calling themselves participatory, 90% are formal-led and only 12.5% had farmers test varieties in their own fields with their own management. Participation is most common in cereal studies (54%) and rarest in vegetable and fruit studies (14%). The authors warn that weak respondent sampling could give breeders misleading and unrepresentative results.

Only 37% of studies collect sex-disaggregated data, and 1.7% intrahousehold data. Where they do, 84% find differences between women and men, often explained with stereotyped roles. Only 0.9% disaggregate by age. Trait descriptions vary so much that three Indian rice studies, with shared authors and similar methods, produced three different trait priority lists. Co-authorship forms about ten isolated clusters, and five donors fund nearly half the studies.

The authors recommend more work on nutritious crops and neglected regions, linking trait studies to breeding databases and Crop Ontology terms, sex-disaggregating at collection and analysis, and rigorous designs with representative sampling and power calculations. They note that on-farm testing and video-based concept testing may avoid some biases of direct questions.

## Methods in brief

- **Design.** Systematic scoping review under PRISMA-ScR. Protocol registered 11 March 2022 and finalized on OSF 8 July 2022. After peer review the search was broadened, and the scholarly databases were searched again on 23 June 2023. Grey literature was searched from 12 March to 6 July 2022.
- **Sources.** Scopus, Web of Science, CAB Direct, AgEcon Search, BIOSIS, plus 12 grey literature sources (FAO, World Bank, IFAD, J-PAL/ATAI, GARDIAN and others).
- **Screening.** 17,786 records, 11,978 after deduplication, 1,325 full texts assessed, 657 included, in Covidence with peer screening.
- **Extraction.** 40-question framework covering crop, tools, analysis, sampling, disaggregation, donors and degree of farmer engagement, classified as formal-led or farmer-led and consultative or collaborative.
- **Synthesis.** Descriptive analysis in R 4.2.3, author and donor networks in VOSviewer, four crop groups (commodity and ornamental crops, 3% of studies, excluded).

## Stated limitations

- English-language studies only, which may explain part of the South American gap. The authors note that reviews which included Spanish-language studies found a similar gap.
- Studies with several tools or methods were counted in several categories, which may double count.
- Sample size calculations may have been done but not reported.
- Whether donor and policy priorities drive the regional bias needs further analysis.

## Notes

- The abstract and Fig. 6 give 657 included studies, while the results describe 564 original studies. Use 564 for shares of original studies and say so.
- In Fig. 2, tricot is one of 21 tools. It appears as a very thin slice inside the direct questions category, next to preference voting, not under direct experience. The main text gives no count of tricot studies. Supplementary Table 1 (not in our PDF) gives the frequency of each tool. The Discussion cites the first tricot paper (ref. 38, see vanetten-2019-expagric) twice: as evidence that direct elicitation gives questionable results, and as an example of on-farm testing (direct experience) that may reduce these biases. So the paper is not consistent about where tricot belongs.
- The paper does not say which denominator (564 or 657) each percentage uses. Fig. 4b uses 216 sex-disaggregated studies, which is 38% of 564, close to the 37% in the text.
- Small errors in the source: the Extended Data Fig. 3 caption says "six" traits but lists seven. Table 1 credits the formal-led and farmer-led framework to ref. 23, a phenomics paper, which looks like a citation error (ref. 44, Sperling et al. 2001, is the likely source).
- The recommendation to link trait studies to Crop Ontology matches the team's data standards work (arnaud-2023-jsfa, dumschott-2023-fps, quiros-2024-compag).
- Different paper from occelli-2024-agecon, the same first author's randomized comparison of tricot and group PVS. See occelli-2026-ahv for the same Cornell group's policy review of farmer participation, and voss-2021-outlook and teeken-2021-fsufs on gender and trait preferences.
