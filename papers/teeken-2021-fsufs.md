## Questions this paper answers

- Do women and men prioritize different cassava traits?
- How do poverty, food insecurity and household type change trait preferences?
- Why is a simple women-versus-men comparison not enough for breeding?
- How do regional food cultures and markets shape trait priorities in Nigeria?

## Summary

Breeding programmes increasingly need information on specific user groups to build customer and product profiles, yet many studies of gendered trait preferences compare only women and men. This paper goes further, asking how gender interacts with poverty, food security, household characteristics and region in shaping cassava trait preferences in Nigeria, with a focus on gari, the main cassava food product.

The study combined a 1000minds trait trade-off survey, in which respondents choose between pairs of trait improvements, with an adapted Rural Household Multi-Indicator Survey (RHoMIS) that recorded household and farm characteristics, food security (HFIAS) and poverty (Poverty Probability Index). A total of 792 people took part, 310 men and 482 women, across four regions. Most were food insecure, with 34% severely and 45% moderately food insecure. Associations were analyzed with correlations, odds ratios and Wilcoxon tests.

The results confirm earlier findings that women prioritize food product quality traits while men prioritize agronomic and productivity traits. Women were more likely than men to prioritize root color, gari taste, texture, color and swelling, with men 31% to 59% more likely to rank these low. Gender differences grew among food-insecure households, and food-insecure respondents placed more weight on product quality, being less likely to rank gari texture low and more likely to rank root size and dry matter low. Poorer households ranked traits much like non-poor ones overall, but men and women within poor households differed notably. Women heading their own households prioritized quality traits more than women living with a spouse, and preferences shifted toward productivity when men controlled more of the value of household activities.

Region mattered too. In the South East, where household use of cassava is important and links to larger markets are weaker, quality traits and in-ground storability ranked higher. In the South South, gari taste and fresh root yield were high priorities, while gari and root color mattered little, possibly because fufu is more central to diets there. More commercial markets in the North Central zone favored larger roots.

The authors conclude that gender analysis must be central to breeding priority setting, because preferences track sex, women's roles in households and their control of resources. Breeding programmes should prioritize gari quality traits such as color, taste, texture and swelling, and invest in high-throughput phenotyping and genetic studies for them, so that new varieties serve more vulnerable users. They also stress recognizing heterogeneity among women and among men, rather than treating either as one group with fixed preferences.

## Methods in brief

- **Survey.** 1000minds pairwise trait trade-offs combined with an adapted RHoMIS.
- **Sample.** 792 respondents (310 men, 482 women) in four regions of Nigeria.
- **Indicators.** Household Food Insecurity Access Scale and Poverty Probability Index.
- **Analysis.** Pearson correlations among traits, odds ratios of ranking a trait low by group, chi-square and Wilcoxon tests.

## Stated limitations

- Correlations between traits can be spurious, so the authors focus on a few planned comparisons.
- The study uses stated trade-offs in a survey, not on-farm evaluation of varieties.
- Results are specific to gari and cassava in Nigeria.
