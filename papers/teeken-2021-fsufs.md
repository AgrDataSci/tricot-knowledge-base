## Questions this paper answers

- Do women and men prioritize different cassava traits?
- How do poverty, food insecurity and household type change trait preferences?
- Why is a simple women-versus-men comparison not enough for breeding?
- How do regional food cultures and markets shape trait priorities in Nigeria?

## Summary

Breeding programmes increasingly need information on specific user groups to build customer and product profiles, yet many studies of gendered trait preferences compare only women and men. This paper goes further, asking how gender interacts with poverty, food security, household characteristics and region in shaping cassava trait preferences in Nigeria, with a focus on gari, a major cassava food product.

The study combined a 1000minds trait trade-off survey, in which respondents choose between pairs of trait improvements, with an adapted Rural Household Multi-Indicator Survey (RHoMIS) that recorded household and farm characteristics, food security (HFIAS) and poverty (Poverty Probability Index). A total of 792 people took part, 310 men and 482 women, across four regions. Most were food insecure, with 34% severely and 45% moderately food insecure. Associations were analyzed with correlations, odds ratios and Wilcoxon tests.

The results confirm earlier findings that women prioritize food product quality traits while men prioritize agronomic and productivity traits. Women were more likely than men to prioritize root color, gari taste, texture, color and swelling, with men 31% to 59% more likely to rank these low. Men were 26% to 41% less likely to rank root size, in-ground storability, disease resistance and maturity time low. Gender differences grew among food-insecure households, and food-insecure respondents placed more weight on product quality, being less likely to rank gari texture low and more likely to rank root size and dry matter low. Poorer households ranked traits much like non-poor ones overall, but men and women within poor households differed notably. Women heading their own households prioritized quality traits more than women living with a spouse, and preferences shifted toward productivity when men controlled more of the value of household activities.

Region mattered too. In the South East, where household use of cassava is important and links to larger markets are weaker, quality traits and in-ground storability ranked higher. In the South South, gari taste and fresh root yield were high priorities, while gari and root color mattered little, possibly because fufu is more central to diets there. In the North Central zone, root size ranked higher, which the authors suggest may reflect more commercial markets that demand larger roots. Gender gaps also varied by region, and in the South South and South East women ranked fresh root yield higher than men.

The authors conclude that gender analysis must be central to breeding priority setting, because preferences track sex, women's roles in households and their control of resources. They see an opportunity to prioritize gari quality traits, especially color, taste, texture and swelling, supported by high-throughput phenotyping and genetic studies. Gari texture was the most cross-cutting trait. Quality traits and in-ground storability matter most for poor and food-insecure households, and regional trait groupings could support regionally focused breeding pipelines. They also stress recognizing heterogeneity among women and among men, rather than treating either as one group with fixed preferences.

## Methods in brief

- **Survey.** 1000minds pairwise trait trade-offs combined with an adapted RHoMIS.
- **Traits.** 11 traits (gari taste, texture, color and swelling, fresh root yield, root size, dry matter, in-ground storage, disease resistance, root color, maturity time), chosen from literature and expert input and checked in community focus groups. Each respondent's output is a rank from 1 (most preferred) to 11.
- **Sample.** 792 respondents (310 men, 482 women), 16 communities in eight states across four zones of Nigeria, February to March 2020.
- **Indicators.** Household Food Insecurity Access Scale and Poverty Probability Index.
- **Analysis.** Pearson correlations among the 11 trait ranks and between ranks and social characteristics (SPSS). Odds ratios of ranking a trait low from a cumulative logit model (SAS), comparing men and women, food insecure and food secure, poor and non-poor, and the four regions. Wilcoxon rank-sum tests of gender differences within each of these groups (R).

## Stated limitations

- The authors treat the correlation analysis as exploratory and warn that it may contain spurious associations, so they base their main comparisons on odds ratios for gender, region, food security and poverty.
- Food security and poverty were each reduced to two groups to keep the interaction analysis manageable. The authors note that merging the HFIAS categories into "food secure" and "food insecure" could mask more nuanced effects.
- No food-secure, non-poor men from the South South or the South East were among the respondents, so these groups are missing from the gender comparisons in the heatmap.

## Notes

- Not a tricot trial. Curator's note, not from this paper: related IITA cassava work in Nigeria by overlapping authors uses tricot to test varieties and gari-eba with users. See teeken-2023-slides, olaosebikan-2023-jsfa and alamu-2023-jsfa.
- The authors suggest labelling traits that matter across all groups, such as gari texture, as "non-negotiable" in product profiles (p11).
- The paper defines its PPI groups inconsistently. Methods (p4) call a PPI index below 10% "poor", the Results (p6, p7) call the same group "non-poor" (575 people), and Table 4 labels 575 people "poor". Treat the poor versus non-poor results with caution.
- HFIAS counts in the text (p6) sum to 785, not 792, and Table 4 lists 112 food-secure respondents where the text gives 105.
- Several men versus women percentages in Table 3 and the text (p6) differ from those implied by the Table 2 odds ratios (for example gari taste 51% in Table 3 against 43% from OR 1.428). The card uses the text and Table 3 values.
- Table 2 marks the men versus women difference in fresh root yield as significant (OR 0.768), while the text (p6) and Table 3 report no difference.
- The 148% figure for root color in the South East versus the South West (p7, Table 3) does not match Table 2, where root color is not significant and maturity time has OR 2.477 (about 148%). Do not cite this figure without checking.
- The contrast between women in female-headed households and women in couples (p5) is cited to Table 1B, but the table does not show household-type rows.
