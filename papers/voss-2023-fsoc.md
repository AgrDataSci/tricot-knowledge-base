## Questions this paper answers

- How can a conventional maize breeding project integrate gender in practice?
- How do you get women to host and evaluate on-farm trials?
- How did tricot scale within CIMMYT's maize work in Kenya?
- What intrahousehold issues arise when testing varieties with women?

## Summary

The paper documents how the Seed Production Technology for Africa (SPTA) project at CIMMYT, launched in 2016 with a second phase from 2020, integrated gender into a conventional maize breeding programme. It uses innovation-history analysis of project documents and the authors' own experience.

SPTA worked with Corteva on the Ms44 gene, which makes maize plants non-pollen producing and removes the need to detassel female parents in hybrid seed production. Hybrids made this way segregate one-to-one for pollen and non-pollen plants and are called 50% non-pollen producing (FNP) hybrids. The technology raises female seed yield, improves quality assurance in seed production and increases hybrid yield through reduced tassel growth.

The team, smaller than many breeding projects, took up recommendations from a donor gender audit and engaged gender specialists to study differences in management and resource constraints between women and men. Because a technology can only benefit women if they can access and evaluate it, SPTA prioritized equitable participation as trial hosts and in participatory variety selection, reaching over 50% women and many resource-poor farmers.

Tricot was central to this shift. The team piloted it in 2021 with 112 farmers (55% women) in Kenya. With the 1000FARMS project, it expanded to 356 farmers (65% women) in 2022 and 1,380 farmers (56% women) in 2023. Attention to intrahousehold dynamics was part of the method, including ensuring that the three trial varieties were stored, milled and cooked separately in over 100 households so that post-harvest traits could be compared.

The authors reflect on successes and challenges and draw lessons for other breeding teams, mainly that gender integration works when breeders own it, when it is backed by specialists and resources, and when testing methods such as tricot make broad, inclusive participation feasible.

Central to the case was handing on-farm trials to the social science team, which the authors describe as unprecedented in maize breeding. They discuss why breeders may resist this, including the wish to standardize across projects, doubts about the technical capacity of social scientists and farmers, reluctance to reform a system that has worked, and institutional divisions between breeding and gender programmes. They also weigh researcher-managed trials, which may offer more internally valid comparisons, against citizen science trials with higher external validity. Incentives that reward heritability but not reaching under-served groups must be examined. The case shows the value of starting small and building trust between breeders and social scientists. Its lessons apply to other breeding teams too.

## Methods in brief

- **Approach.** Innovation history from project documents and team experience.
- **On-farm testing.** Tricot trials in Kenya from 2021, recruiting women as trial hosts.
- **Post-harvest evaluation.** Separate storage, milling and cooking of the three varieties in households.

## Stated limitations

- A single-project case study based on the authors' own experience.
- Lessons may not transfer directly to larger breeding programmes with different structures.
