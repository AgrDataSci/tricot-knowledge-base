## Questions this paper answers

- How can a conventional maize breeding project integrate gender in practice?
- How do you get women to host and evaluate on-farm trials?
- How did tricot scale within CIMMYT's maize work in Kenya?
- What intrahousehold issues arise when testing varieties with women?

## Summary

The paper documents how the Seed Production Technology for Africa (SPTA) project at CIMMYT, launched in 2016 with a second phase from 2020, integrated gender into a conventional maize breeding programme. It uses innovation-history analysis of project documents and the authors' own experience.

SPTA worked with Corteva on the Ms44 gene, which makes maize plants non-pollen producing and removes the need to detassel female parents in hybrid seed production. Hybrids made this way segregate one-to-one for pollen and non-pollen plants and are called 50% non-pollen producing (FNP) hybrids. The technology raises female seed yield, improves quality assurance and increases hybrid yield through reduced tassel growth and greater partitioning of nitrogen to the ear.

The team, smaller than many breeding projects, took up recommendations from a donor gender audit and engaged gender specialists to study differences in management and resource constraints. Recognizing the potential benefits of the technology for women, SPTA prioritized equitable participation of women and men as trial hosts and in participatory variety selection, reaching over 50% women and many resource-poor farmers.

tricot was central to this shift. Earlier trials showed that farmers treated trials as demonstration plots, and that giving trials to women plot managers could cause tension when husbands did not support it. The team piloted tricot in 2021 with 112 farmers (55% women) in Kenya, under the farmers' own management. The pilot asked both spouses to evaluate where both managed the trial, and added end-use traits such as flour quality and taste. Keeping the three varieties stored, milled and cooked separately across over 100 households proved too hard at scale, so the team dropped end-use traits after the pilot and returned to one respondent per household. With the 1000FARMS project, trials expanded to 356 farmers (65% women) in 2022 and 1,380 farmers (56% women) in 2023.

The pilot data showed no major gender-based differences in preferences that would call for gender-intentional breeding. Instead, they supported gender-responsive breeding: FNP varieties showed no negative gendered impacts and appealed to women and men under their own conditions. The authors note that long-term impacts are yet to be seen, that no SPTA variety had been released, and that tricot data were not yet used in release decisions, which still rely on national performance trials.

The authors credit donor mandates, open-minded project leaders, funding for gender research, and respectful collaboration between breeders and social scientists.

Central to the case was handing on-farm trials to the social science team, which the authors call unprecedented in maize breeding. Breeders may resist this because they want to standardize across projects, doubt the technical capacity of social scientists and farmers, or are reluctant to reform a system that has worked. Divisions between breeding and gender programmes add to this. Researcher-managed trials may give more internally valid comparisons, and citizen science trials higher external validity. Incentives that reward heritability but not reaching under-served groups must be examined. The case shows the value of seeking allies, starting small and building trust between breeders and social scientists.

## Methods in brief

- **Approach.** Innovation history from project documents and team experience.
- **On-farm testing.** tricot trials in Kenya from 2021, recruiting women as trial hosts.
- **Trial protocol.** Each farmer got three varieties, 200 kernels each, with basic guidance only: plant in the middle of the maize field, away from trees, with similar slope and soil across subplots. Farmers used their own management, including intercropping, applied the same way to all subplots. The team collected spacing data to estimate yields instead of standardizing planting.
- **Post-harvest evaluation.** Piloted in 2021 only. Farmers ranked the three varieties on end-use traits such as storage, flour quality and taste, which needed separate storage, milling and cooking in over 100 households. The team dropped end-use traits after the pilot as too hard to manage at scale, and returned to one respondent per household.
- **Data quality.** Several tiers of supervision, spot-checks, and illustrated instruction booklets, variety scorecards and storage bags (p. 8).

## Stated limitations

- The long-term impacts of the changes to the breeding process are yet to be seen, and no variety developed through SPTA has been released yet (p. 6).
- tricot data have been useful to validate FNP varieties but have not yet been used in maize variety release decisions in eastern and southern Africa, which still rely on national performance trials (p. 8).
- Some data quality concerns persisted, and tricot trials were harder for NARES field staff to oversee than standardized trials (p. 8).
- End-use trait evaluation in tricot proved too challenging to manage at scale with the available capacity (p. 4, p. 8).
- The small size of the team allowed closer collaboration than is feasible in many larger projects (p. 7).

## Notes

- de Sousa et al. 2024 (desousa-2024-asd) cites Voss et al. 2023 for improved women's representation in CIMMYT tricot networks.
- The card writer notes that this is a single-project case study based on the authors' own experience and project documents. The authors do not state this as a limitation.
- A 2019 SPTA study in Zimbabwe (Cairns et al. 2021a, cited) found more intercropping and recycled seed on women's plots, and gaps between stated PVS preferences and varieties used at home, which led the team to tricot (p. 4).
- Figure 4 shows many women and men in Embu and Kirinyaga intercropping in the 2022 trials (counts read from a chart, approximate).
- Source issue: the paper describes tricot both as allowing many trials within a set budget (p. 4) and as a resource-intensive trial network (p. 8). It gives no cost data.
