## Questions this paper answers

- Are the farmers hosting on-farm maize trials representative of the wider farming population?
- Is setting a quota for women hosts enough for inclusive on-farm testing?
- How different are women-headed and men-headed farms, and how much do they vary within each group?
- How can a farm typology guide the selection of host farmers?
- How large a survey is needed, and which variables matter?

## Summary

On-farm testing only predicts how varieties will perform for farmers if the host farms represent the target population of environments, yet host farmers are often more prosperous and better educated than their neighbours. Jill Cairns, Frédéric Baudron and colleagues at CIMMYT, CIRAD and Rothamsted checked, after the fact, whether host farmers in CIMMYT's participatory maize testing network in Zimbabwe represented the wider population, and piloted a typology approach for selecting hosts.

In 18 randomly selected districts across five provinces, mostly in natural regions 3 and 4, extension agents randomly sampled 2,346 farms from village registries, with women and men in proportion to ward population. The 2021 survey covered maize production over two years, inputs, management, farm and livestock assets and demographics. Seventeen structural and functional variables fed a typology of 2,040 farms, with a classification random forest identifying the most discriminating variables. Trial hosts, chosen by extension agents trained to include women and resource-constrained farmers, were part of the sample and were compared with the typology.

Five farm types emerged, labelled average men-headed, average women-headed, resource-constrained women-headed, relatively prosperous, and primarily off-farm households. Host farms were distributed across these types much like the surveyed population, with the off-farm type slightly under-represented. Hosts did not differ from the population in equipment or livestock, but produced almost 20% more maize, partly because more of them relied on farming for income. Gender-based targets had therefore ensured adequate representation of women.

The typology also showed that gender alone hides important differences. The two women-associated farm types differed sharply, with one owning more equipment and livestock and producing almost three times as much maize as the other, which used less fertilizer and manure and intercropped more. One of the women-associated types was closer to a men-associated type than to the other women-associated type. Selecting hosts only by the sex of the household head would miss these distinct needs.

The authors recommend sampling strategies that explicitly incorporate agronomic and socio-economic diversity, using local extension knowledge while guarding against elite capture and unconscious bias, and reporting the characteristics of host farmers. Hosts were under 10% of those surveyed, so they suggest research on the minimum survey size needed to build a typology, which they expect to be much smaller, and note that key variables will differ by crop and region.

## Methods in brief

- **Sampling.** Five of Zimbabwe's ten provinces in CIMMYT's testing network, 18 random districts, up to 15 wards per district, 2,346 farms randomly drawn from village registries. Survey with KoboCollect, September to November 2021, by 10-15 trained extension agents per district. Ethics approval CIMMYT IREC 2024.005.
- **Typology.** 17 variables on demographics, endowments and maize production, five farm types from 2,040 farms, random forest to find discriminating variables.
- **Comparison.** 186 trial-hosting farms compared with the population by farm type and by gender of household head and plot manager.

## Stated limitations

- Plot manager gender recorded as binary, excluding joint management, and reported by one household member.
- Typologies reduce a multidimensional continuum to discrete types.
- Key variables are likely to differ across crops and regions.
- Not stated as limitations but relevant. High rainfall areas are outside the testing network and the survey, and plot manager age was not recorded.

## Notes

- The paper labels farm types inconsistently. Figure legends call type 2 average women-headed and type 3 resource-constrained women-headed, while the discussion names types 3 and 4 as the women-associated types and compares their wealth. Describe the types by their characteristics and say the numbering is inconsistent.
- The hosts here are in CIMMYT's researcher-designed participatory variety testing network, not tricot trials. The sampling argument applies to tricot, and cairns-2026-jxb builds on it for inclusive host-farmer sampling in maize breakthrough products.
- Cites laajaj-2020-scirep on host-farmer selection bias. See kool-2020-expagric on undefined research populations, voss-2021-outlook on gender and maize breeding, and voss-2023-fsoc on gender in CIMMYT tricot trials in Kenya.
