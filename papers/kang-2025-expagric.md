## Questions this paper answers

- How can tricot data be linked with socio-economic (RHoMIS), climate and station data?
- Which potato varieties do Rwandan farmers prefer, old or new?
- Do market-oriented and subsistence farmers prefer different varieties?
- How well do on-station results align with on-farm rankings?

## Summary

Potato is central to food security in Rwanda but production growth has slowed, and improved varieties must fit farmers' conditions and preferences to be adopted. The study tests the value of linking a tricot trial with environmental, socio-economic and on-station data.

In seasons 2021A and 2021B, 460 farmers in the main potato regions of Gicumbi and Nyamagabe each tested three of eleven varieties, allocated randomly in incomplete blocks. Of these, 283 farmers were also surveyed with the RHoMIS household tool. On-station multi-environment trials from 2018–2019 provided genotypic values for the varieties. The varieties included two released before 1990 (Cruza and Kirundo) and a set of recent releases from 2018 onwards.

Farmers preferred the pre-1990 varieties Cruza and Kirundo, while the newer Gisubizo and Kazeneza were also competitive. Seasonal temperature changed the yield rankings across regions. Plackett-Luce trees, with a minimum group size of 25, split farmers by temperature, with a much larger warm group (280 farmers) than cool group (113 farmers). Socio-economic factors and gender shaped preferences. Taste mattered most for home consumption, while yield came first for sales. A node of commercial farmers with high crop income, who prioritize yield, could be recommended Gisubizo, Izihirwe, Kazeneza, Kirundo, Seka and Twihaze, and their preferences varied relatively little.

The authors note challenges in aligning on-station and on-farm data, but argue that the integrated approach gives breeding programmes actionable insights for developing varieties suited to farmers' needs and conditions, and can improve breeding efficiency, adoption and productivity.

The newer varieties Gisubizo and Kazeneza ranked third and fourth but did not differ significantly from the older and farmer-grown varieties, so they remain viable alternatives for the Rwandan market. The authors explain the continued preference for Kirundo and Cruza by their broad adaptability, stable yields across agro-ecological zones and notable disease tolerance. All participants were smallholders whose farming conditions are often far from optimal, with limited access to fertilizer and pesticides, so stable performance under high disease and pest pressure matters most. Temperature changed the picture. Where cooler conditions prevailed, Twihaze yielded better, likely because lower disease pressure in cool environments makes disease resistance less critical. The study shows how linking rankings with climate and household data yields targeted recommendations by agro-ecology and farm type instead of one national list, and why breeding targets should consider both environment and the economic orientation of farm households. The authors see this integration as a way to improve breeding efficiency and adoption.

## Methods in brief

- **Tricot.** 460 farmers, 11 varieties, three per farmer, seasons 2021A (September 2020 to February 2021) and 2021B (March to July 2021).
- **Socio-economic data.** RHoMIS survey of 283 participants.
- **Station data.** Multi-environment trials 2018–2019 for genotypic values.
- **Analysis.** Plackett-Luce trees with climate and socio-economic covariates (90% confidence cut-off, minimum group size 25).

## Stated limitations

- Aligning on-station genotypic values with on-farm rankings was difficult.
- Only part of the participants (283 of 460) had RHoMIS data.
