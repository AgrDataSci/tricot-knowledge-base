## Questions this paper answers

- Has 35 years of participatory research changed how national agricultural policies involve farmers?
- What drives the institutionalization of participatory breeding and seed system practice?
- Why do participatory practices spread in research organizations but not in seed laws?
- What role do farmer organizations, donors and universities play?
- What should governments and donors do to embed farmer participation?

## Summary

The Farmer First movement of the late 1980s argued that farmers should co-design, test and spread agricultural technologies rather than receive them. Martina Occelli, Hale Ann Tufan and colleagues ask whether, after nearly four decades, national agricultural policies have institutionalized farmers as co-researchers and joint decision-makers in plant breeding and seed systems.

They studied three programmes with at least two decades of participatory work and strong policy engagement. FIPAH in Honduras uses local farmer research committees (CIALs) for participatory bean and maize breeding and has contributed to 12 bean and 7 maize varieties since 2005. The sorghum programme in Mali, with breeders from IER, ICRISAT and IPR and participatory work since 1998, helped two farmer organizations run farmer-managed trials and cooperative seed production. PABRA, the Pan-Africa Bean Research Alliance, has worked since 1996 through multi-stakeholder platforms, participatory variety evaluation and a bean corridor approach.

The authors reviewed 30 policy documents and held 25 key informant interviews in 2025, then coded them against four farmer-first principles of policy change drawn from Ashby (2022), namely inclusive governance, responsive innovation systems, decentralized knowledge and power, and transformative education.

In Honduras, farmer inclusion in policy is consultative at best. One FIPAH informant says the main national breeding programme now treats participatory approaches as central. Yet a bean breeding respondent says participatory breeding has never been accepted nationally, and the authors note that CIAL seed must still be sold as grain. In Mali, farmer organizations produce about 90% of certified seed, are consulted on policy, receive donor grants directly, and variety releases now rely on on-farm data with women and men among decision-makers. In eastern Africa, PABRA appears to have built a culture of accountability to farmers in national programmes. With Wageningen's ISSD project, it was a significant actor in Uganda's reform that let farmer organizations sell quality-declared bean seed. But it leaned towards market-led priorities, did not directly raise farmer participation in policy processes, and remains constrained by centralized certification. The authors note that no study has assessed whether PABRA actually changed national policies.

Three patterns run across the cases. Change is often driven by motivated champions, frequently without institutional buy-in. The authors suggest that these champions, rather than evidence from adapted and adopted varieties, are what move national policy, which raises questions about durability once they leave. Change is more likely when farmer-first organizations partner with more influential ones, such as Zamorano University or regional trade bodies, which can tilt agendas towards markets. National policy lags well-established practice, so participatory routines exist before, or without, codification. Education is the weakest principle in the text, and in two of the three cases (Honduras and Mali) long-term support came from smaller, "alternative" donors outside the CGIAR Trust Fund list.

The authors recommend strengthening farmer organizations as seed producers and sellers, decentralizing seed testing, certification and release, and building participatory research into breeding and seed curricula. They close by asking whether farmer-first policy change will survive shrinking donor patience.

## Methods in brief

- **Design.** Multiple case study using the CIAMO framework (context, intervention, actor, mechanism, outcome), scored against four farmer-first principles of policy change.
- **Case selection.** Plant breeding or seed system projects, geographically diverse, with sustained exposure to farmer-first principles, run at national or regional scale for at least a decade. All three also engaged extensively with policy actors, had a body of peer-reviewed evidence, and had lead researchers who took part in the Farmer First workshops or the 2009 book.
- **Policy definition.** "National agricultural policy" covers codified laws, strategies and programmes, and also uncodified practices and routines in research and extension organizations, such as routine research protocols.
- **Data.** 30 policy documents in English, Spanish and French, plus 25 semi-structured online interviews (5 Honduras, 13 Mali, 7 PABRA) between January and April 2025.
- **Analysis.** Deductive thematic coding per case, then an inductive cross-case synthesis. Co-authors involved in the projects supplied data and validation but did not design the study or draw the conclusions.

## Stated limitations

- No analysis of political economy, colonial history or cultural context, so cross-case patterns are hypotheses to test, not prescriptions.
- No welfare outcomes, so the study cannot say whether changes improved farmer prosperity or food security, or for whom.
- Limited to plant breeding and seed policy, not agronomy or agroecology.

## Notes

- Several co-authors led the programmes studied. The paper says they supplied data and validated the analysis but did not design the study or draw conclusions.
- This study was funded by USAID (Cooperative Agreement 7200AA-19LE-00005). The paper says this support ended on 27 February 2025, when the project was cancelled, which falls inside the January to April 2025 interview period.
- The authors find that farmer organizations gained policy voice in all three cases once they moved from on-farm testing into seed production, marketing and sale (p. 15).
- In all three cases, regional testing of varieties for different agroecological zones was what made decentralized participatory research necessary (p. 16). Relevant to tricot, but not evidence on tricot.
- Source issue: Fig. 2 rates PABRA's education dimension at the top level (5 of 5) and links it to farmer demand, while the text calls education the weakest principle everywhere and PABRA's progress market-led. The card follows the text.
- Source issue: the paper is not consistent on PABRA's policy influence. It says this has not been assessed (p. 9) and that farmer engagement did not directly influence policy participation (p. 12), but the cross-case synthesis and Fig. 2 rate PABRA's inclusive governance 4 of 5.
- Source issue: the author contributions (p. 17) say two Mali programme co-authors took part in interviews and manuscript writing for that case, which sits uneasily with footnote 2. E. Weltzien is also first author of the source for the ~90% figure.
- Source issue: Table 2 calls the 12 bean and 7 maize varieties "released", while the text says FIPAH-supported CIALs "contributed to" them. The card follows the text.
- Useful context for tricot institutionalization questions. It shows the gap between practice and codified policy that the team's own mainstreaming work faces (1000farms-2025-report, howland-2025-report). Do not read it as evidence on tricot itself.
