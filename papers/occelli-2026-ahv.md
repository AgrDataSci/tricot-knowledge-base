## Questions this paper answers

- Has 35 years of participatory research changed how national agricultural policies involve farmers?
- What drives the institutionalization of participatory breeding and seed system practice?
- Why do participatory practices spread in research organizations but not in seed laws?
- What role do farmer organizations, donors and universities play?
- What should governments and donors do to embed farmer participation?

## Summary

The Farmer First movement of the late 1980s argued that farmers should co-design, test and spread agricultural technologies rather than receive them. Martina Occelli, Hale Ann Tufan and colleagues ask whether, after nearly four decades, national agricultural policies have institutionalized farmers as co-researchers and joint decision-makers in plant breeding and seed systems.

They studied three programmes with at least two decades of participatory work and strong policy engagement. FIPAH in Honduras uses local farmer research committees (CIALs) for participatory bean and maize breeding and has contributed to 12 bean and 7 maize varieties since 2005. The sorghum programme in Mali, run by IER, ICRISAT and two farmer organizations since 1998, built farmer-managed trials and cooperative seed production. PABRA, the Pan-Africa Bean Research Alliance, has worked since 1996 through multi-stakeholder platforms, participatory variety evaluation and a bean corridor approach.

The authors reviewed 30 policy documents and held 25 key informant interviews in 2025, then coded them against four farmer-first principles of policy change drawn from Ashby (2022), namely inclusive governance, responsive innovation systems, decentralized knowledge and power, and transformative education.

In Honduras, farmer inclusion in policy is consultative at best. The national breeding programme now treats participatory approaches as central, yet informants say participatory breeding has never been accepted nationally and CIAL seed must still be sold as grain. In Mali, farmer organizations produce about 90% of certified seed, are consulted on policy, receive donor grants directly, and variety releases now rely on on-farm data with women and men among decision-makers. In eastern Africa, PABRA built a culture of accountability to farmers in national programmes and helped Uganda legalize quality-declared bean seed, but leaned towards market-led priorities and remains constrained by centralized certification.

Three patterns run across the cases. Change depends on motivated champions working without institutional buy-in. Change happens when farmer-first organizations partner with more influential ones, such as Zamorano University or regional trade bodies, which can tilt agendas towards markets. National policy lags well-established practice, so participatory routines exist before, or without, codification. Education is the weakest principle everywhere, and long-term support came mostly from smaller donors.

The authors recommend strengthening farmer organizations as seed producers and sellers, decentralizing seed testing, certification and release, and building participatory research into breeding and seed curricula. They close by asking whether farmer-first policy change will survive shrinking donor patience.

## Methods in brief

- **Design.** Multiple case study using the CIAMO framework (context, intervention, actor, mechanism, outcome), scored against four farmer-first principles of policy change.
- **Case selection.** Plant breeding or seed system projects, geographically diverse, more than a decade of farmer-first practice at national or regional scale, with lead researchers linked to the Farmer First workshops or books.
- **Data.** 30 policy documents in English, Spanish and French, plus 25 semi-structured online interviews (5 Honduras, 13 Mali, 7 PABRA) between January and April 2025.
- **Analysis.** Deductive thematic coding per case, then an inductive cross-case synthesis. Co-authors involved in the projects supplied data and validation but did not design the study or draw the conclusions.

## Stated limitations

- No analysis of political economy, colonial history or cultural context, so cross-case patterns are hypotheses to test, not prescriptions.
- No welfare outcomes, so the study cannot say whether changes improved farmer prosperity or food security, or for whom.
- Limited to plant breeding and seed policy, not agronomy or agroecology.
