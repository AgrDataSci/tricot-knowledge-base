## Questions this paper answers

- How can station multi-environment trials and tricot on-farm trials be combined for variety release?
- Which groundnut genotypes are high-yielding and stable in Tanzania?
- Do women and men groundnut farmers prefer different traits?
- Which traits should enter the groundnut target product profile?

## Summary

Groundnut breeding must deliver high kernel yield and the traits farmers and markets want, but yield and selection responses depend on genotype-by-environment interaction and management. The study assesses genotype-by-environment interaction and farmers' trait preferences together, to select genotypes for release and to guide breeding for broad or specific adaptation while integrating gender.

Sixteen genotypes, including two commercial checks, were evaluated in a randomized complete block design across 18 environments representing groundnut agroecological zones in Tanzania. In parallel, decentralized on-farm tricot trials ran across 42 locations, with 154, 178 and 871 farmers in 2021, 2022 and 2023 respectively, in 36 villages of 10 regions.

Genotype, environment and their interaction all significantly affected kernel yield. Mean kernel yield across environments was about 832 kg/ha. In Table 4, ICGV-SM 16645 had the highest mean kernel yield, about 936 kg/ha, followed by ICGV-SM 05534 at about 927 kg/ha, although the abstract names ICGV-SM 05534 as the top yielder. In the GGE biplot, ICGV-SM 16645 and ICGV-SM 10014 (about 878 kg/ha) were closest to the ideal genotype, combining high mean yield with stability. Genotype rankings agreed within 2023 but correlated weakly between seasons, pointing to strong genotype by year interaction.

For both women and men, marketability, kernel color and kernel size were the traits most closely linked to overall preference (Kendall correlation, 549 men and 654 women). Women gave more weight than men to early maturity, ease of harvesting and ease of shelling. The abstract says men preferred haulm yield, but in the figure haulm yield is only weakly linked to men's overall preference. Tan, small to medium kernels were most preferred, and early maturity matters for the market segment. By agro-ecological zone, preferred varieties differed, with Mangaka 2009 preferred in several zones for its kernel color and size.

The authors conclude that farmers' trait preferences should shape target product profiles at the design stage to increase adoption, and that the selected genotypes, test environments and preferred traits can guide breeding pipelines, targeted variety release and production for Tanzania's target populations of environments.

The GGE analysis suggested at least four mega-environments, which the authors see as useful for targeted evaluation of genotypes, breeding and seed production. The convergence of women and men on marketability, small to medium kernels and tan color shows that breeders must prioritize market-preferred phenotypes, yet kernel color and seed size are not consistently treated as priority traits in groundnut breeding. The authors recommend building them into target product profiles from the start, as CIMMYT's Eastern and Southern Africa groundnut programme has begun to do. The divergence between women and men on labor-saving traits and oil content shows the value of tailoring pipelines to gender-differentiated priorities, which they expect to raise adoption and varietal turnover. They also note that rankings aggregated from many farmers have been shown to distinguish technology performance, citing earlier tricot validation work. The study thus runs conventional stability analysis and farmer evaluation side by side to inform release decisions, although it analyses the two data sets separately.

## Methods in brief

- **Station trials.** 16 genotypes (14 ICRISAT elite lines and 2 released TARI checks, Mangaka 2009 and Mnanje 2009), randomized complete block design with four replications, nine locations in 2022 and 2023 (18 environments), covering six of seven agroecological zones.
- **Analysis.** Combined ANOVA and GGE biplot for stability and mega-environments.
- **On-farm trials.** tricot under natural rainfall and farmer management, 42 locations in 36 villages of 10 regions, with 154, 178 and 871 farmers in 2021, 2022 and 2023. Each farmer received three varieties, assigned with ClimMob, and a local check was added. Farmers ranked traits from the groundnut target product profile (for example yield, kernel color, maturity, harvest labor). Data were collected with ODK. The gender analysis used 549 men and 654 women.
- **Preference analysis.** Plackett-Luce models (log-worth, local check as reference) of trait and overall rankings by agro-ecological zone, and Kendall correlation between each trait and overall preference, separately for men and women.

## Stated limitations

- The breakdown of farmer preferences by agro-ecological zone ignores possible segmentation of preferences within zones (p. 10).
- More years of data are needed to understand which test locations in Tanzania give the same results and could be dropped (p. 13).
- Farmer preferences need further segmentation, including young men and young women (p. 13).
- The pathway from on-farm data to faster adoption and food security is diffuse (p. 13).

## Notes

- The abstract gives 927.232, 936.39 and 877.67 kg/ha. Table 4 (p. 7) gives 927.232, 936.393 and 877.669 kg/ha. Round when citing.
- Source inconsistency on the top yielder. The abstract calls ICGV-SM 05534 (927 kg/ha) the highest-yielding genotype. Table 4 (p. 7) shows ICGV-SM 16645 higher, at 936 kg/ha, and Section 3.4 (p. 9) calls ICGV-SM 16645 the highest yielder. Section 3.3 (p. 6) attaches 936.393 to ICGV-SM 16530 and 927.232 to ICGV-SM 03707, which does not match Table 4 (761 and 882 kg/ha). Its list of the five lowest genotypes also does not match Table 4. This card follows Table 4.
- Source inconsistency on gender traits. The abstract says early maturity, ease of harvesting and shelling were the traits women preferred most, and haulm yield the trait men preferred. Figure 5 (p. 11) shows marketability, kernel color and kernel size at the top for both, with haulm yield weak for men.
- Source inconsistency on Figure 5. The text says women ranked oil content slightly above yield, but the figure shows yield above oil content for women. The caption says bars are in descending order, but they are not. The text calls the same correlations both Pearson and Kendall (pp. 9 to 11).
- Genotype names. The text names ICGV-SM 16445 (pp. 9, 13), and Figures 6 and 7 show ICGV-SM 16630. Neither is in Table 1. They are probably ICGV-SM 16645 and ICGV-SM 16530. Figures 6 and 7 show 14 of the 16 station genotypes plus the local check (ICGV-SM 05702 and ICGV-SM 16519 are absent), although Methods says all 16 were used on-farm (p. 4).
- Table 3 (p. 6) gives identical values for Environment and Block (replication), which looks like a copy error. A replication df of 8 does not fit 18 environments with four replications. The text gives p < 0.001 for genotype, but Table 3 shows p < 0.01.
- The mega-environment text counts fourteen plus five environments (19), but 18 were tested (p. 13).
- Figure 6 panel sizes add up to 1,777 (our calculation), while 1,203 farmers took part (Figure 5, p. 11, and Methods, p. 4). The panels may count observations, not farmers.
- Methods says the tricot trials ran from 2021 to 2024, but farmer numbers are given only for 2021 to 2023 (p. 4).
- Lowest-yield sites. The text names Nakayaya as the lowest site overall, but the two-year mean of Chambezi (356 kg/ha) is slightly lower than Nakayaya (361 kg/ha) (our calculation from Table 4). The text names Kihinga as lowest in 2022, but Tumbi 2022 (97 kg/ha) is lower (pp. 6, 9, 13).
- Section 3.4 (p. 9) calls Mnanje 2009 outstanding in stability, but it sits far from the origin on PC2 in Figures 2 and 3.
- The climate data are cited as Table 1 but shown in Table 2 (p. 6).
