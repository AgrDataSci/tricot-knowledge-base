## Questions this paper answers

- How much does a maize tricot trial cost per farmer in a national programme?
- Which cost items drive the cost of a tricot trial?
- How much could a programme save by leaving routine monitoring to local field agents?
- Does participant support (transport refunds, refreshments) add much to the cost, and how can it be managed?
- Should researcher time on tricot be charged to the project or to the core breeding budget?
- How do operational choices, rather than data volume, shape the cost of on-farm testing?

## Summary

Stephen Angudubo and colleagues costed a maize tricot trial run by the Zambia Agricultural Research Institute (ZARI) as one of a series of 1000FARMS case studies on what drives the cost of on-farm testing. The trial tested eight options of an intermediate-maturity product profile with 110 farmers in nine districts across three agro-ecological regions, from November 2024 to April 2025. Extension field agents made four monitoring visits and the research team two. ZARI distributed seed centrally and field agents handled last-mile delivery, with digital data collection.

The team used the 1000FARMS costing template and its guidelines. The costing covers only marginal costs that grow with the number of farmers. Office-based work, such as choosing test lines and traits, designing the trial in ClimMob and central training of field agents, counts as fixed and is left out. A 15% share of researcher staff costs is charged to on-farm testing, the rest to the core breeding budget.

Marginal costs came to USD 15,644, or USD 142.22 per farmer. Travel expenses (per diem and allowances) were the largest item at 42%, followed by materials at 29% and field labour at 14%. Transport (fuel and vehicle costs) took 9% and communication 6%. Research team travel made up about 61% of travel expenses. Seed production took only about 10% of total cost. Participant support, mainly transport refunds and refreshments, took 59% of material costs, about USD 24 per farmer, because farmers met in many small, separate sessions.

The authors identify two cost drivers. The first is researchers doing routine fieldwork. Multiple research roles joined monitoring and data collection on about 25% of farms, duplicating the field agents' work and adding per diem and staff days without clearly better evidence. The second is participant support spread across many small meetings.

Scenarios show what different delivery choices would save. Leaving routine monitoring and data collection to trained field agents, with researchers doing spot checks and remote data verification, lowers the cost per farmer to USD 128 through travel savings and to USD 132 through labour savings. A 30% cut in participant support through clustering farmers into hubs of 10 to 20 lowers it to about USD 135. Larger cuts save more but risk lower participation. Combined, local delivery and clustered engagement bring the cost to about USD 103 per farmer, saving about USD 4,270.

The authors conclude that the trial is feasible and that costs depend less on the amount of data and more on how delivery is organized. They recommend a no-duplication model with audit-based supervision, clustered farmer engagement with a clear support package, separating delivery costs from core breeding costs, and SOPs that track cost per farmer, completion rates and audit findings.

## Methods in brief

- **Tool.** 1000FARMS activity-based Excel costing template, filled following the costing guidelines (angudubo-2026-guide).
- **Standard rates.** UN travel reimbursement rates for vehicle costs, ITU low-consumption mobile basket (70 minutes, 20 SMS, 500 MB a month) for communication, market prices adjusted for inflation for materials, and ZARI salary scales for labour.
- **Assumptions.** Trials follow the ten tricot steps. The research team leads preparation, design and seed packaging. Field agents lead monitoring and data collection. Researchers support yield measurement and farmer feedback. Seed for tricot is produced separately from other breeding trials.
- **Scope.** Marginal costs only. Fixed costs are excluded. A 15% share of researcher staff costs is charged to on-farm testing (10% is the share proposed by the ABI Transform team).
- **Scenarios.** Cost changes from delegating routine fieldwork to field agents and from cutting participant support by 30%, 50% and 70%.

## Stated limitations

- Fixed costs are excluded, so the total understates the full cost of running the trial.
- Cutting participant support too far risks lower participation and incomplete trials.
- Delegating to field agents needs adequate training and clear SOPs to keep data quality.

## Notes

- Part of the 1000FARMS costing case studies by Stephen Angudubo and colleagues, with angudubo-2026-naro (common bean, Uganda). Both apply angudubo-2026-guide. Together they are the main evidence in the knowledge base on what a tricot trial costs and why.
- Costs are marginal costs per farmer. They are not comparable with the cost per equivalent experimental unit in desousa-2024-asd, a single case study of potato in Rwanda that compares tricot with RCBD.
- The 24% cut in travel expenses (about USD 1,564) roughly matches the USD 1,550 scenario saving. The stated 50.5% cut in field labour (about USD 1,129) is slightly above the USD 1,106 saving reported.
- The costing guidelines set research visits at about 30% of trials, while the ZARI research team reached about 25% of farms.
