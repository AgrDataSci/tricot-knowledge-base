## Questions this paper answers

- Can tricot be used for consumer tasting, not just on-farm trials?
- Do centralized and home tasting give the same results?
- Which traits drive consumer preference for boiled sweetpotato?
- Do preferences differ by consumer segment or district?

## Summary

The paper adapts tricot to consumer preference testing of boiled sweetpotato. It aims to validate tricot for consumer testing, compare centralized and home tasting, and document how to run both. Breeding trials sometimes include more than ten genotypes, and tasting many samples tires consumers, so each participant tastes and ranks a random set of three.

The study tested 27 genotypes, 21 in Ghana (15 at late breeding stages and 6 released varieties) and 6 released, widely grown varieties in Uganda. A total of 1,433 participants took part. In each country, the trial was split into centralized tasting and home tasting. In centralized tasting, the team cooked the roots within 48 hours of harvest and served them near markets in Ghana, where sweetpotato buyers were intercepted, and at pre-arranged venues such as schools in Uganda. In home tasting, households received raw roots and cooked them their usual way. Participants chose the best and worst of their three genotypes. Only overall acceptability was analyzed in Ghana, because taste and color data were largely missing, while Uganda also had taste and color. Rankings were analyzed with Plackett-Luce models and trees, using district, age and gender as covariates.

The most preferred genotypes were consistent across tasting formats, while the order of the rest varied. In Ghana, the advanced clone PG17136-N1 ranked above the reference variety SARI-Nyumingre (Obare), the most popular variety in the area, in both trials, and ranked first in home tasting. In Uganda, consumers preferred Ejumula, an orange-fleshed, high-dry-matter variety, followed by NASPOT 13, both outperforming the reference NASPOT 8. Taste drove overall rankings in Uganda. In 88% of cases where a genotype ranked best for taste, it also ranked best overall, and 81% for worst, while color showed almost no correlation with overall acceptability (Kendall τ = −0.03). Across both countries, "good taste" and "sweet" were the commonest reasons for best rankings, and "tasteless" or "not sweet" for worst rankings.

The authors conclude that tricot is a robust tool that can be adapted to consumer preference studies. Using it for both on-farm trials and consumer tests gives breeders and food scientists one consistent method to inform variety release. They advise choosing home tasting, centralized tasting or both with care, planning sample sizes and recruitment, and recording food preparation and participant characteristics.

In Ghana, PG17136-N1 is a white-fleshed, high-dry-matter clone at the advanced trial stage. Its advantage over Obare was statistically significant only when home and centralized data were pooled (p = 0.019). Home tasting in Ghana had only 118 participants for 21 genotypes, which gave low statistical power, and the authors call for larger consumer samples, especially for home tasting. In the Ghana centralized trial, the tree split Bole and Bongo from Kasena-Nankana West, where SARI-Nan ranked just above PG17136-N1. In Uganda, both trees split Gulu from Soroti/Serere and Wakiso, and in Gulu home tasting NASPOT 12, not Ejumula, ranked first. Averaged across tree nodes, PG17136-N1 and Ejumula remained the best choices.

## Methods in brief

- **Genotypes.** 21 in Ghana, 6 in Uganda, with nutritional profiles of raw roots.
- **Design.** Random sets of three genotypes per participant, from country-specific pools.
- **Formats.** Centralized tasting within 48 hours of harvest, and home tasting.
- **Participants.** 1,433 in total: Ghana 897 centralized and 118 home, Uganda 144 centralized and 274 home.
- **Traits.** Best and worst for overall acceptability in both countries, plus taste and color in Uganda. Participants gave reasons for their best and worst choices.
- **Cooking.** Centralized samples were washed and boiled in plain water, covered with banana leaves in Gulu and Soroti/Serere, and peeled and steamed in banana leaves in Wakiso.
- **Analysis.** Plackett-Luce models with significance letters, Plackett-Luce trees with district, age and gender as covariates (α = 0.1, minimum node size 10), Bland-Altman comparison of home and centralized estimates, Kendall correlation between traits, and text analysis of reasons. Data and R code are on Zenodo.
- **Practical comparison.** Table 8 compares home and centralized tasting: home tasting needs at least two days and reached more people in Uganda but fewer in sparse Ghana areas, while centralized tasting gave better questionnaires and age control.

## Stated limitations

- Home tasting gives less control over cooking time, water, serving temperature and tasting conditions.
- Home tasting in Ghana had only 118 participants for 21 genotypes, which gave low statistical power. The authors call for larger consumer samples, especially for home tasting.
- Taste and color were not analyzed in Ghana because of missing or incomplete data.
- Home and centralized participants may differ in gender, socio-economic and age profile, which may explain part of the difference in rankings.
- Consumer segments covered were limited to participating communities.
- Centralized tastings reached only consumers free at that time, possibly missing a valuable segment.
- Raw-root nutrient profiles do not describe boiled roots, because sugars change and leach during boiling.
- Nutrient retention data for specific varieties after cooking are scarce.
- Some home participants let people under 18 fill questionnaires, which made them invalid. Ghana market interception gave less attentive answers.

## Notes

- Cited in desousa-2024-asd and vanetten-2020-rtbwp as the sweetpotato consumer testing example.
- Outside this paper: later tricot consumer studies, such as olaosebikan-2023-jsfa for cassava, use a similar design.
- The authors warn against adding experimental control to cut variation, because tricot's strength is testing close to real use (p. 15).
- Source issue: the Results say PG17136-N1 outperformed Obare in both trials (p. 8), but Figure 4 and the Discussion (p. 11 to 12) show the difference is significant only when the two trials are pooled.
- Source issue: the Conclusions say the top genotypes were consistent across segments (p. 14), but the trees show NASPOT 12 first in Gulu home tasting (Figure 8B) and SARI-Nan just ahead of PG17136-N1 in Kasena-Nankana West (Figure 7A).
- Source issue: the abstract says home and centralized tasting gave similar rankings, but in Ghana the Results report a marked difference and weak relationship between the two (p. 7, Figure 4C).
- Source issue: the paper calls the color correlation negative, but τ = −0.032 (Table 6) is close to zero. It shows color did not drive rankings, not that it worked against them.
- Source issue: the text says genotypes were given in roughly equal frequencies, but Table 3 shows five released Ghana varieties tasted about twice as often as the advanced clones, so the Ghana design was not balanced.
- Source issue: Table 7 omits Obare for Ghana. The listed Ghana probabilities sum to 0.92 (card's calculation).
- Source issue: one clone is named PGA14011-24 in Tables 1, 4 and 7 but PGN14011-24 in Table 3 and Figures 3, 4, 6 and 7.
- Source issue: tree node sizes do not match Table 2 (Ghana home tree n = 110 against 118, Uganda home tree 62 + 209 = 271 against 274), probably because of missing data.
