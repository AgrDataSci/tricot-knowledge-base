## Questions this paper answers

- Can tricot be used for consumer tasting, not just on-farm trials?
- Do centralized and home tasting give the same results?
- Which traits drive consumer preference for boiled sweetpotato?
- Do preferences differ by consumer segment or district?

## Summary

The paper adapts tricot to consumer preference testing of boiled sweetpotato, aiming to bring end-user preferences into breeding decisions earlier. Breeding trials often include more than ten genotypes, too many for a consumer to taste at once, so each participant tastes and ranks a random set of three.

The study tested 27 genotypes, 21 in Ghana (15 at late breeding stages and 6 released varieties) and 6 released, widely grown varieties in Uganda. A total of 1,433 participants took part. In each country, the trial was split into centralized tasting, at common locations such as schools with roots cooked within 48 hours of harvest, and home tasting, where participants cooked and tasted the roots themselves. Participants ranked their three genotypes for overall preference and sensory traits such as taste, and their rankings were analyzed with Plackett-Luce models and trees using consumer and location covariates.

The most preferred genotypes were consistent across tasting formats and segments, while the order of the rest varied. In Ghana, the advanced clone PG17136-N1 outperformed the reference variety SARI-Nyumingre (Obare), the most popular variety in the area, in both trials, and won in home tasting across all districts. It did not differ significantly from Obare when centralized and home tasting were analyzed separately. In Uganda, consumers preferred Ejumula, an orange-fleshed, high-dry-matter variety, followed by NASPOT 13, both outperforming the reference NASPOT 8. Taste drove overall rankings in Uganda. In 88% of cases where a genotype ranked best for taste, it also ranked best overall, and 81% for worst, while color was negatively correlated with overall acceptability. "Not sweet" and "tasteless" were the commonest descriptors for the worst genotypes.

The authors conclude that tricot is a practical, scalable tool for consumer testing that can capture preferences of many consumers at low cost and complement sensory panels, and they recommend pairing it with nutritional profiling of the tested genotypes.

In Ghana, PG17136-N1 is a white-fleshed, high-dry-matter clone at the advanced stage of the breeding pipeline. Its advantage over Obare became statistically significant when home and centralized data were pooled, showing the value of combining formats. Plackett-Luce trees for Ghana split consumers by district, separating Bole, Bongo and Kasena-Nankana West, while the top genotype stayed stable. The study shows that tricot can rank many genotypes with large consumer samples without overloading individual tasters, and that results are robust enough to feed breeding decisions on which clones to advance. Later tricot consumer studies, such as olaosebikan-2023-jsfa for cassava, follow a similar design.

## Methods in brief

- **Genotypes.** 21 in Ghana, 6 in Uganda, with nutritional profiles of raw roots.
- **Design.** Random sets of three genotypes per participant, from country-specific pools.
- **Formats.** Centralized tasting within 48 hours of harvest, and home tasting.
- **Analysis.** Plackett-Luce models with significance letters, and Plackett-Luce trees for segments and districts.

## Stated limitations

- Home tasting gives less control over cooking and storage.
- Consumer segments covered were limited to participating communities.
- Centralized tastings reached only consumers free at that time, possibly missing a valuable segment.
- Nutrient retention data for specific varieties after cooking are scarce.
