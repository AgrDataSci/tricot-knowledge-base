## Questions this paper answers

- What does "data-driven" crop diversity management mean, and why now?
- How can breeding move beyond broad adaptation to serve diverse environments and users?
- What is data-driven decentralized breeding (3D breeding)?
- What role can genebanks play in linking with farmers?
- What investments are needed (phenotyping, gender data, decision tools, capacity)?

## Summary

The perspective argues that large datasets and computational methods are changing how crop diversity is managed in the Global South, across genebanks, breeding programmes and farmers, which the authors treat as one field. Cheaper genomic and phenotypic data, faster breeding cycles and intensive analytics allow information-rich management that can handle the interactions between crop diversity, production environments and socio-economic heterogeneity.

The authors frame breeding information on two axes, complexity (how many traits and interactions are captured) and representativeness (how well test conditions reflect real use contexts). Conventional breeding favored low complexity and broad adaptation, focusing on mean response and reducing genotype-by-environment interaction. Ecological theory instead predicts that more, narrowly adapted varieties are more productive. Data-driven approaches, such as environmental covariates in trial analysis, crop models and data-driven decentralized breeding (3D breeding) with tricot on-farm testing, can raise both complexity and representativeness. Tricot predicts farmers' overall appreciation, a complex trait that integrates many attributes besides yield.

Examples include Ethiopian farmers identifying superior durum wheat landraces that were released directly as varieties, genomic models of crop history, unbalanced designs, and upland rice crop modelling in Brazil. The authors note that genomic success stories have mostly concerned traits controlled by few genes.

On end users, they argue that demand-led breeding must look beyond the household, since trait preferences differ between individual decision-makers, notably women and men, and female-headed households are only a small share of households. Farmers also combine varieties for different purposes and risks, so portfolios matter more than single best varieties.

The paper closes with five priorities for investment. These are helping genebanks link with farmers through data, designing low-cost phenotyping, generating more and better gender and socio-economic data, designing information products for decisions, and building data-science capacity. Breeding progress metrics should become composite indices weighted by socio-economic data, and coordinated policy is needed to avoid fragmented capacities.

On genebanks, the authors note that more than 5.7 million accessions are stored in 831 genebanks worldwide, according to FAO WIEWS, but most are poorly described, regeneration and characterization backlogs are common and duplication occurs. Genebanks have a very high benefit-to-cost ratio, yet missing accession data reduce their value and raise search costs, which data-driven characterization with farmers could address. On accountability, they argue that breeding progress must be quantified for public and social investors, but not only through output metrics such as varieties released or seed sales, which suit private profit better than social returns.

## Methods in brief

- **Type.** Perspective synthesizing published work, without new data.
- **Framework.** Two axes, complexity and representativeness, to position breeding approaches.
- **Evidence.** Case examples from genebank characterization, tricot, crop modelling and gender studies.

## Stated limitations

- 3D breeding and environmental covariate approaches have so far focused on climate and adaptation traits, not the full complexity of crop use contexts.
- Gender and socio-economic data remain scarce and household-based.
- Genomic successes mostly involve oligogenic traits.
