## Questions this paper answers

- What does "data-driven" crop diversity management mean, and why now?
- How can breeding move beyond broad adaptation to serve diverse environments and users?
- What is data-driven decentralized breeding (3D breeding)?
- What role can genebanks play in linking with farmers?
- What investments are needed (phenotyping, gender data, decision tools, capacity)?

## Summary

The perspective argues that large datasets and computational methods are changing how crop diversity is managed in the Global South, across genebanks, breeding programmes and farmers, which the authors treat as one field. Cheaper genomic and phenotypic data, faster breeding cycles and intensive analytics allow information-rich management that can handle the interactions between crop diversity, production environments and socio-economic heterogeneity.

The authors place data-generating approaches on two axes, complexity (how far biological, environmental and human aspects of the use context are captured) and representativeness (how well test conditions reflect real use contexts). Conventional breeding favored low complexity and broad adaptation, focusing on mean response and reducing genotype-by-environment interaction. Ecological theory instead predicts that more, narrowly adapted varieties are more productive. Data-driven approaches, such as environmental covariates in trial analysis, crop models and data-driven decentralized breeding (3D breeding) with tricot on-farm testing, can raise both complexity and representativeness. Linked to environmental covariates, tricot trials showed how Central American common bean responded to heat, Ethiopian durum wheat to cold and Indian bread wheat to rainfall and radiation. In the Ethiopian 3D breeding example, tricot gave better predictions of farmers' overall appreciation of varieties than a benchmark, and adding on-farm and environmental data greatly enhanced genomic selection.

Other examples include Ethiopian farmers identifying superior durum wheat landraces that were released directly as varieties, genomic models of crop history, unbalanced designs, and upland rice crop modelling in Brazil. The authors note that gene-based success stories are limited to traits controlled by few genes.

On genebanks, FAO WIEWS lists more than 5.7 million accessions in 831 genebanks, but this may be overestimated because most accessions are not described, backlogs are common and duplication may occur. Genebanks have a very high benefit-to-cost ratio, yet missing accession data reduce their value, which evaluation of accessions by farmers could address.

On end users, demand-led breeding must look beyond the household, since trait preferences differ between individual decision-makers, notably women and men, and female-headed households are only a small share of households. Farmers also combine varieties for different purposes and risks, so the authors suggest offering a "menu" of complementary varieties.

On accountability, breeding progress must be quantified for public and social investors, but not only through outputs such as varieties released or seed sales, which suit private profit better than public benefit. One option is a composite index weighted by socio-economic data, which is resource-intensive. Another is reliability, the probability that a variety outperforms a check, based on stakeholders ranking overall appreciation. Reliability is hard to compare across stakeholder groups and does not suit entirely new product categories.

The paper closes with five priorities for investment. These are helping genebanks link with farmers through data, designing low-cost phenotyping, generating more and better gender and socio-economic data, designing information products for decisions, and building data-science capacity. Coordinated policy is needed to avoid fragmented capacities.

## Methods in brief

- **Type.** Perspective synthesizing published work, without new data.
- **Framework.** Two axes, complexity and representativeness, to position breeding approaches.
- **Evidence.** Case examples from genebank characterization, tricot, crop modelling and gender studies.

## Stated limitations

- Genebank approaches that link accessions to agroclimatic data, such as FIGS, have so far focused on climate and adaptation traits, not the full complexity of crop use contexts.
- Gender and socio-economic data remain scarce and household-based.
- Gene-based innovations (transgenesis or introgression) have succeeded only for oligogenic traits, such as submergence tolerance and disease resistance.
- Analysis tools for GxE are new, sometimes immature, and rarely used together in breeding programmes.
- Empirical on-farm studies of GxExM interactions are still rare, and most analyses ignore management.
- Trait-preference studies are mostly reactive and focus on a single new variety rather than portfolios.
- Reliability against a check is hard to compare across stakeholder groups and does not suit new product categories.
- Drones and rovers will have limited use in public breeding in the Global South for the foreseeable future.

## Notes

- A perspective rather than an empirical study, so it carries few numbers. Use it to frame arguments and cite the primary studies it draws on.
- The Ethiopian durum wheat landrace releases are cited to van Etten et al. 2019 (ref. 10, see vanetten-2019-pnas) and Mengistu et al. 2016 (ref. 29). Ref. 29 is a study of phenotypic diversity, so check whether it documents the releases before citing it for them.
- Source issue: the genebank figure is credited to FAO WIEWS without a date or reference entry. Ref. 22 (FAO Second Report) supports only the caveats on description, backlogs and duplication.
- A Nigerian study cited here (ref. 9) found that women in food-insecure households prioritized gari quality traits more.
- The authors say data synthesis needs open data, dataset citation, refined synthesis methods and data standards.
