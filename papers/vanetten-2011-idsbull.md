## Questions this paper answers

- Where does the idea behind tricot come from?
- Why did participatory plant breeding and variety selection need a new way to scale up?
- How could crowdsourcing and citizen science be applied to crop improvement in Africa?
- Why compare varieties in sets of three, and why keep them anonymous?
- How should farmers be motivated to take part without paying them?
- How does crowdsourced variety evaluation relate to seed markets, input subsidies and crop diversity as a commons?

## Summary

This short essay is the first published proposal of the approach that later became tricot. Jacob van Etten starts from a problem in sub-Saharan Africa. Most farmers still grow their own varieties, and modern varieties from the formal seed sector cover only a small share of the cropped area, with maize as the main exception. Participatory plant breeding, participatory variety selection, community seed banks and seed fairs have produced valuable lessons over two decades, but none has been scaled up to reach large numbers of farmers.

The author proposes to borrow from crowdsourcing and online citizen science, where large numbers of volunteers contribute time and skill to collective research. Small packages of seed, a few grams each, of modern and farmer varieties would be distributed widely through existing networks such as shops, NGOs and churches. Farmers would grow three thumb-sized bags side by side under the same conditions, rank them after harvest, and report through their mobile phones, by voice or SMS, possibly with a field agent. Triadic comparisons are chosen because they are simple for farmers and give more information than pairs. Analysis would draw on preference learning, the field behind online recommendation systems, to rebuild a ranking of varieties and to find the environmental and social factors that shape preferences, using the location of each farmer.

Several design choices that tricot later kept appear here. Evaluation is blind, and farmers learn the names of the varieties only after reporting, which makes the data more objective and gives farmers a reason to report. Incentives rest on reputation, reciprocity and access to information rather than money, and seed is free so that the poorest households are not excluded. Farmers can also compare the new seed with their own, which shows adoption potential and helps target conservation of local varieties.

The essay then places the proposal in wider debates. It draws on work describing farmer seed innovation as a decentralized network in which better seeds spread through exchange, and argues that crowdsourcing would strengthen this process rather than replace it. It contrasts the proposal with input subsidies and small-package seed sales, and calls for subsidising information about crop diversity rather than physical inputs, especially for food crops grown for home consumption. Finally, it compares farmer varieties with open-source software, arguing that open data and protected, freely shared varieties would sustain innovation and could coexist with seed markets. The author closes by calling for staged, empirical testing, starting small but planned for scale from the start.

## Methods in brief

- Conceptual essay and proposal, with no new data.
- Builds the argument from literature on seed systems, participatory crop improvement, farmer innovation, citizen science, information economics and open-source software.
- Sketches a design of small blind seed packages, triadic comparisons, mobile phone reporting, free participation and open data, leaving implementation details to each context.

## Stated limitations

- Online citizen science methods cannot be transferred directly to rural sub-Saharan Africa, where internet access is far from universal.
- Whether the approach works in particular rural African contexts must be evaluated carefully and empirically.
- Using mobile phone tracking to locate farmers raises legal and privacy issues, so recording the point of seed delivery is offered as an alternative.
- Putting a price on seed packages might raise data quality but would exclude the poorest households.
- Seed selection may not be the main bottleneck in every seed system, and seed exchange, storage and promotion can matter as much.

## Notes

- `papers-pdf/van-Etten-2011.pdf` is a one-page pointer to the open copy and the DOI, not the article, because the article has no open licence. The card was checked against the full published text.
- This is a proposal, not an evaluation. For the first pilots and their lessons, see vanetten-2019-expagric. For the development of the approach since 2011, see desousa-2024-asd.
- The paper describes reporting after harvest by voice or SMS. In later tricot work, field agents register farmers and collect data through forms generated by ClimMob (see quiros-2024-compag).
- A footnote suggests including two identical seed lots in a package to check the consistency of answers, while noting that pairwise comparisons would also work in principle.
- The essay frames crowdsourced evaluation as a way to target food crops and farmer varieties for household food security. Much later tricot work applies the approach to breeding pipelines and commercial products as well.
