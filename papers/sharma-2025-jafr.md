## Questions this paper answers

- Which potato clones do Nigerian farmers prefer over the check variety?
- How do market orientation and household traits change variety preference?
- At which crop stages should tricot assessments be made for potato?
- How stable are Plackett-Luce tree splits under bootstrapping?

## Summary

Potato is emerging as an important crop in Nigeria, but top-down breeding often ignores farmer input, producing varieties that fit local conditions poorly. The study evaluates whether tricot, combined with digital tools, better reflects farmer needs.

Farmers in seven local government areas of Plateau State (Barikin Ladi, Bassa, Bokkos, Jos North, Jos South, Mangu and Pankshin) evaluated anonymized potato genotypes alongside the check variety Marabel. Trial design and allocation ran on ClimMob, and data were collected through a mobile app and ODK. Of 300 farmers, 295 had validated data. Assessments covered three stages, vegetative (germination, disease resistance, drought tolerance), harvest (yield, tuber appearance, marketability) and post-harvest (cooking quality, storability).

CIP clones CIP392797.22 and CIP393371.157 ranked top, with log-worth of 0.620 and 0.309 relative to the check, while CIP398208.29 and CIP398190.200 were least preferred (−0.085 and −0.465). Plackett-Luce trees split farmers by the share of potatoes they sell, at 42.4%. Farmers selling less retained CIP392797.22 as their top choice, while those selling more favored Marabel and CIP393371.157. Splits held in over 93% of 1,000 bootstrap samples, and key traits were retained in over 90%. Market-oriented farmers prioritized commercial traits, while subsistence farmers emphasized resilience. Cluster analysis also identified small farmer segments, such as larger households engaged in the potato business (8% of the sample).

The authors conclude that participatory, data-driven breeding that integrates local knowledge can improve adoption, resilience and food security.

The discussion asks whether farmers are truly at the center of national breeding and release programmes, and the results suggest often not. CIP398190.200, despite being officially released, was among the least preferred because dormancy-related poor germination undermined early establishment, a trait rarely examined in institutional trials. The check Marabel also ranked low. Farmers valued its early maturity for market timing, but its short dormancy, poor storability and limited post-harvest use reduced its appeal. The authors argue that farmer choices reflect behavioral factors, such as risk aversion and planning for storage, household consumption or sales, and not only field traits. Household size, age, market involvement and distance to markets shaped which traits farmers prioritized, in line with the idea that different farmer types need different options. Recognizing these dynamics, the authors conclude, explains why officially endorsed varieties can diverge from what farmers adopt. The study also shows how ClimMob and ODK enabled real-time tracking of the trial, which helped validate data from 295 of the 300 farmers recruited across the seven local government areas.

## Methods in brief

- **Design.** ClimMob allocation of anonymized genotypes plus check Marabel, three per farmer.
- **Data.** Mobile app and ODK, three assessment stages.
- **Sample.** 300 farmers in seven LGAs, 295 validated.
- **Analysis.** Plackett-Luce log-worth, Plackett-Luce trees on socio-economic covariates, 1,000 bootstrap samples for split stability, farmer clustering.

## Stated limitations

- One state in Nigeria.
- Socio-economic segments were small, limiting segment-specific recommendations.
