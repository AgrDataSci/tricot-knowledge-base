## Questions this paper answers

- Can farmers measure yield accurately enough for breeding decisions?
- How can tricot produce absolute yields, not just rankings?
- Does grain moisture distort farmer volumetric measurements?
- How many farmers are needed for reliable farmer-generated yield data?

## Summary

Tricot relies mainly on farmer rankings, which give relative performance but not the absolute yields breeders need to measure genetic gain, satisfy release committees or assess returns on breeding investment. The study tests whether farmers can generate accurate yield data for common bean in decentralized on-farm trials.

A yield determination survey in 2022 involved 30 farmers (24 men and 6 women) in Kilindi and Karatu districts in northern Tanzania. Farmers and trained technicians measured harvested grain volumes using common local containers (sado tins), after farmers sun-dried the beans to reduce moisture. Researchers then measured absolute yield in mass and moisture content for comparison.

Farmer and technician volumes correlated strongly (r = 0.96, p < 0.001), and the mean difference in log-yield between them was close to zero, showing agreement. Moisture content ranged from 14.5% to 16.0% and explained only 11.9% of differences in grain density and weight. A predictive model converting farmer volumes into absolute yield explained about 90% of variability in observed grain yield (R² 0.901), with a loss of performance under validation of less than 5%. Subsampling showed that data from as few as 25 farmers still gave highly accurate estimates.

The authors conclude that farmer-generated yield data are reliable for breeding decisions and offer a cost-effective, scalable way to add yield to tricot and accelerate variety turnover in sub-Saharan Africa.

Farmer-led estimates also differentiated between common bean genotypes. The authors note that earlier studies reported inconsistent performance for varieties such as Sweet Violet and RCB 593, which they attribute to genotype-by-environment interaction, a factor central to adoption that on-farm data capture well. In the model, farmer volume was the dominant predictor of yield, while variety and seed dimensions (thickness and width) had only minor effects, so intrinsic and extrinsic factors barely biased farmer estimates. Cross-validation with three folds and 100 repetitions gave a root mean square error of 0.233 and a mean absolute error of 0.133, with R² of 0.901. The practical implication is cost. Farmer volumetric data could largely replace researcher visits for yield measurement, which are the main expense in on-farm testing, and allow trials to report the absolute yields that breeders, release committees and investors need.

For tricot practitioners, the results support training farmers in simple volumetric measurement and adding a yield question to trial protocols, which de Sousa et al. 2024 and later guidance recommend. Subsampling showed this holds even with modest numbers of farmers.

## Methods in brief

- **Survey.** 30 farmers in two districts of northern Tanzania, 2022.
- **Measurements.** Farmer and technician volumes with sado tins, researcher mass and moisture.
- **Analysis.** Correlation and Bland-Altman-type agreement on log-yield, predictive model from volume to absolute yield, validation, subsampling to test sample size.

## Stated limitations

- A small sample (30 farmers), mostly men.
- Results are for common bean, a crop whose grain is easy to measure by volume.
- Moisture effects may be larger in other crops or seasons.
