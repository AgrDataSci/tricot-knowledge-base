## Questions this paper answers

- Do farmers run genuine experiments, and how do they differ from scientists' field trials?
- What is the difference between an epistemic experiment and a directly action-guiding one?
- Do farmers use controls, randomization, blinding and replication?
- Is "change one variable at a time" a sound rule for on-farm testing?
- Why might farmers' and scientists' experiments complement each other?

## Summary

Farmers have experimented for millennia, and many languages have their own word for it, yet philosophers of science have paid this tradition little attention. Sven Ove Hansson, a philosopher at KTH and SLU, uses the anthropological literature to examine what farmers' experiments are and how their methods compare with those of agricultural scientists.

He distinguishes epistemic experiments, which seek to explain how the world works, from directly action-guiding experiments, which test whether an intervention achieves a practical goal. Farmers' experiments belong to the second kind, alongside clinical trials and agricultural field trials, and should be judged against those rather than against laboratory science.

Experimentation is a necessity in farming, not a luxury, because pests, weeds, soils and climate keep changing. It is also part of risk management, as subsistence farmers spread many crops, varieties and practices to avoid total failure. Hypotheses come from causal understanding and chance observations, as in a Ghanaian farmer who curbed striga with onion leaf preparations.

Hansson then compares farmers' practice with six precepts of modern methodology. About 39% of African farmer experiments in one survey used side-by-side control plots, but farmers also rely on historical controls, carrying "the check in their heads". The rule of changing one variable at a time is often wrong for action-guiding experiments, where testing a promising combination makes sense. Farmers adjust experiments mid-season. Randomization and blinding are absent from farmers' practice, and he notes that agricultural scientists also rarely blind field trials, although seed varieties are easy to blind and blinding may change results. Replication is where farmers excel, since farmers everywhere retest new seeds and methods on small plots, even those already tested by scientists.

His evaluation is balanced. Farmers' experiments give results adjusted to local conditions and replicated at a scale no science can match, given more than 800 million people working on farms. Scientists use stricter controls and statistics against confounding. Which tradition gives better guidance depends on how strongly performance correlates across local conditions, an empirical question that differs by crop and region. Neither tradition can replace the other, and there is room for mutual, respectful learning.

He closes with links to the philosophy of science, including theory-ladenness, the testing of practical hypotheses, and the point that action guidance does not require knowing mechanisms. He calls for empirical studies of how farmers choose hypotheses, use controls and draw conclusions.

## Methods in brief

- **Design.** Conceptual and philosophical analysis based on a review of anthropological and agricultural literature on farmers' experiments.
- **Framework.** Six methodological precepts (controls, one variable at a time, following the plan, randomization, blinding, replication) used as descriptive tools.
- **Comparison.** Farmers' experiments compared with scientists' actual field trial practice rather than with methodological ideals.

## Stated limitations

- Whether farmers' local, massively replicated experiments outperform scientists' controlled trials is an empirical question that armchair analysis cannot settle.
- The evidence base is anthropological case material, so methodological detail on farmers' experiments is thin and needs dedicated empirical studies.

## Notes

- The paper does not discuss tricot. Its points on blinding, replication across many farms and testing under local conditions are useful background for why tricot uses blind-coded packages across many farms (vanetten-2019-expagric, steinke-2017-asd).
- Its observation that scientists seldom blind variety trials cites Bulte et al. 2014. Report it as Hansson's citation, not a result of this paper.
- Pairs with vandegevel-2020-asd on citizen science and participatory research, and with kool-2020-expagric on the external validity of researcher-run on-farm trials.
