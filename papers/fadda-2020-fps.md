## Questions this paper answers

- What is the Seeds for Needs approach and where has it been used?
- Can high-tech centralized breeding and decentralized participatory breeding be combined?
- How does genebank material reach farmers' fields under this approach?
- Where does crowdsourced citizen science (tricot) fit in the sequence?
- What does Seeds for Needs mean for seed systems and farmer motivation?
- Did Seeds for Needs speed up the release of new varieties?

## Summary

The review starts from a debate between two breeding strategies. High-tech centralized breeding uses genomics, rapid generation advance and higher selection intensity to accelerate genetic gain, with up to 5.7 selection cycles a year for wheat. Even so, it takes over 10 years to develop a new cereal variety and make it available to farmers in developing countries, and centralized breeding may not meet smallholders' needs for locally adapted varieties. Decentralized participatory breeding involves farmers and captures interactions between genetic diversity, environment and management, and local preferences. In six least developed countries, farmers get 90% of their seed from informal sources. The authors argue the two can be reconciled, and use the Seeds for Needs initiative to show how.

Bioversity International started Seeds for Needs in 2010 to bring genebank accessions directly to farmers' fields for evaluation in target environments, with Ethiopian durum wheat as its case study. It has since been used in a dozen countries in Africa, Asia and Latin America. The Ethiopian case combines three elements. First, 373 farmer varieties from the Ethiopian national genebank and 27 improved released varieties went through molecular and phenotypic characterization, and 60 farmers (30 women, 30 men) scored the same panel. This work found high genetic diversity not yet used by breeders, several farmer varieties outperforming recommended modern varieties in the studied environments (some above 5 t/ha, double the national average), disease resistance in landraces, and farmer scores that correlated with metric traits and could be traced to genetic loci. Second, a nested association mapping population (EtNAM) crossed 50 farmer varieties with one improved line to create over 6,000 recombinant inbred lines. A preliminary look at 1,200 lines from 12 families found that families bridging the Ethiopian and international gene pools were more diverse and higher yielding. Third, crowdsourced citizen science, the tricot approach proposed by van Etten in 2011, scaled up farmer evaluation in target environments. It uses digital tools, individual farmers instead of groups, and a widely distributed trial network. A global synthesis from Ethiopia, India and Nicaragua linked farmer-generated data to climate data to identify varieties tolerant to climate stress.

The authors argue that this combination can raise breeding efficiency. Crowdsourcing provides data from many environments at lower cost than large multilocation trials, gives breeders feedback that centralized programmes lack, and identifies variety portfolios that help farmers cope with climate variability. Farmers joined for reasons that differed by country, mainly to contribute to research and share information with experts, and most did not expect payment. Two varieties characterized in 2011 and 2012 were released in 2017, which the authors say is 4 years faster than average. The approach supports community seed banks, and the authors expect it could stimulate local seed businesses and better link formal and informal seed systems.

The review frames Seeds for Needs as a decentralized, participatory, gender-sensitive modern breeding approach. It proposes a feedback loop at landscape or community level in which mobile monitoring of variety performance informs crosses, release and seed delivery.

## Methods in brief

- **Type.** Mini review (6 pages) of Seeds for Needs studies, mainly the Ethiopian durum wheat case, set against the literature on centralized and participatory breeding. No search strategy or inclusion criteria are described.

## Stated limitations

- Seed policy, seed sovereignty and decommodification of seeds are outside the review's scope.
- Citizen science places extra work on farmers, who manage small experiments and collect data.

## Notes

- Connects mancini-2017-scirep (farmer scoring of the genebank panel) to vanetten-2019-pnas (crowdsourced testing) and anticipates gesesse-2023-pnas (EtNAM with genomic selection).
- The review describes the van Etten et al. 2019 synthesis as involving 12,409 farmers. vanetten-2019-pnas reports 12,409 plots. Use the primary source.
- This paper describes the panel as 373 farmer varieties held in the Ethiopian National Gene Bank plus 27 improved released varieties (p. 3). It does not state the total or cite a source for these counts. mancini-2017-scirep, which this paper cites for the farmer scoring of the same panel, reports 400 accessions from EBI: 25 improved varieties and 375 landraces. Use the primary source for the farmer-scored panel.
- Source issue: two varieties characterized in 2011 and 2012 were released in 2017. The review calls this 5 years and 4 years faster than average (p. 5), which implies a 9-year average. Elsewhere it cites over 10 years to develop and make a cereal variety available (Atlin et al. 2017, p. 2). The two figures cover different spans, and 5 years fits only the 2012 characterization.
- Source issue: the review says the more diverse and high-yielding EtNAM families were those bridging the Ethiopian and international gene pools (p. 3). Every EtNAM family crosses a farmer variety with the same improved line, so the meaning of this comparison is unclear. See Kidane et al. 2019 or gesesse-2023-pnas.
- Source issue: the reference list has errors. It cites the tricot first-experience paper as Van Etten et al. 2016 with the 2019 issue details (Exp. Agric. 55(S1)). The repository card is vanetten-2019-expagric. It also lists the same Annicchiarico pea paper twice (2018 and 2019), the 2018 entry with wrong journal details (pp. 5 to 6).
- The review uses the name tricot only in the author contributions (p. 5) and in a reference title. In the main text it calls the approach crowdsourced citizen science.
