## Questions this paper answers

- What is the Seeds for Needs approach and where has it been used?
- Can high-tech centralized breeding and decentralized participatory breeding be combined?
- How does genebank material reach farmers' fields under this approach?
- Where does crowdsourced citizen science (tricot) fit in the sequence?
- What does Seeds for Needs mean for seed systems and farmer motivation?

## Summary

The review starts from a debate between two breeding strategies. High-tech centralized breeding uses genomics, rapid generation advance and higher selection intensity to accelerate genetic gain, with up to 5.7 generations a year for wheat. Even so, a new cereal variety still takes over 10 years to reach farmers in developing countries, and its broadly adapted varieties often miss smallholders' needs in marginal environments. Decentralized participatory breeding involves farmers and captures genotype-by-environment interaction and local preferences, and in least developed countries farmers get about 90% of their seed from informal sources. The authors argue the two can be reconciled, and use the Seeds for Needs initiative to show how.

Bioversity International started Seeds for Needs in 2010 to bring genebank accessions directly to farmers' fields for evaluation in target environments, with Ethiopian durum wheat as the first case. It has since been used in a dozen countries in Africa, Asia and Latin America. The Ethiopian case combines three elements. First, molecular and phenotypic characterization of genebank farmer varieties, together with farmer scoring by 30 women and 30 men, showed high genetic diversity not yet used by breeders, farmer varieties outperforming recommended modern varieties (some above 5 t/ha, double the national average), disease resistance in landraces, and farmer scores that matched metric traits and could be traced to genetic loci. Second, a nested association mapping population (EtNAM) crossed 50 farmer varieties with one improved line to create over 6,000 recombinant inbred lines. A first look at 1,200 lines showed that families bridging the Ethiopian and international gene pools were the most diverse and highest yielding. Third, crowdsourced citizen science, the approach later called tricot, scaled up farmer evaluation in target environments, with a global synthesis covering Ethiopia, India and Nicaragua.

The authors argue that this combination is more efficient than either approach alone. Crowdsourcing provides data from many environments at lower cost than large multilocation trials, gives breeders feedback that centralized programmes lack, and identifies variety portfolios that help farmers cope with climate variability. Farmers joined for reasons that differed by country, mainly to contribute to research and share information with experts, and most did not expect payment. The approach also supports community seed banks and local seed businesses, linking formal and informal seed systems.

The review frames Seeds for Needs as a decentralized, participatory, gender-sensitive modern breeding approach, and calls for a feedback loop at landscape or community level in which mobile monitoring of variety performance informs crosses, release and seed delivery.

## Methods in brief

- **Type.** Narrative review of Seeds for Needs studies, mainly the Ethiopian durum wheat case, set against the literature on centralized and participatory breeding.

## Stated limitations

- Seed policy, seed sovereignty and decommodification of seeds are outside the review's scope.
- Citizen science places extra work on farmers, who manage small experiments and collect data.
