## Questions this paper answers

- How large should tricot plots be, and how many farms are needed?
- How can tricot be made inclusive of gender and social diversity?
- What ethics, privacy and traditional-knowledge rules apply?
- How can tricot produce yield estimates for genetic gain and release?
- Which ontologies and R packages support tricot data?
- How much cheaper is tricot than conventional PVS?

## Summary

The working paper takes stock of tricot as it matured into an approach ready for mainstreaming in breeding and extension, with examples mainly from cassava, sweetpotato and potato. It argues that on-farm testing is complicated because many parts must align, from farmer mobilization and seed delivery to analysis and reporting, and that tricot streamlines and standardizes these parts.

On trial dimensions, it argues for plots as small as possible on many farms, while avoiding interplot competition. tricot is driven by external validity, the ability to reproduce findings in target environments, which is best served by covering diverse growing conditions and users and recording them as covariates. Small plots fit on small and large farms alike, avoiding a bias toward larger farms, and reduce planting material. The authors suggest scientists' preference for large plots partly reflects the regression fallacy. Neighbor and border effects remain a valid concern, so plot size should follow biological knowledge of each crop, and border rows can be omitted. A PlackettLuce order effect was planned for 2021. For around 12 entries, 100 to 200 farms typically give solid results, in line with earlier cereal trials, and more environments call for proportionally more farms.

Inclusion is part of external validity. Trials must sample the gender and social diversity of users, and farmers' knowledge of a trait depends on their role in production, processing and cooking. So far no tricot analysis had found statistically detectable differences between men and women. The authors propose going beyond sex disaggregation, using a short RHoMIS survey and recruiting local task groups, as IITA did for cassava. tricot can extend beyond farmers to processors and consumers, as in sweetpotato product testing in Ghana and Uganda. As research with human subjects, tricot must meet ethics standards on consent, privacy and rights over traditional knowledge.

On genetic gain, rankings give yield-based reliability, the probability that a new variety beats the market leader, which suits product-replacement strategies. When absolute yields are required, as for release, some or all fields can be visited, and farmer volumetric measures (250 ml tins) have been compared with researcher estimates. The paper describes ClimMob's recent features, a standardized cassava trait list (Table 1), planned links to Crop Ontology, and R packages for analysis.

The cost figures are rough estimates without data or method. If conventional participatory variety selection (PVS) and tricot are both run intensively, with farmer groups, meetings and RTB planting material, tricot is estimated to cost roughly 40% less. At the other extreme, a tenfold reduction is possible in the US, where seed is mailed and farmers use smartphones. Part of the saving comes from shifting costs to farmers, who give their own labor, land and inputs. Farmers mainly join for seed and information.

Each variety should get the same number of seeds, not the same weight, so small-seeded varieties are not favored. In sweetpotato consumer tests, central and home testing agreed on the top varieties, and a minimum relative regret score is proposed to combine farmer and consumer scores.

## Methods in brief

- **Plot size.** Small plots on many farms, with environmental and social covariates.
- **Number of farms.** 100–200 for about 12 entries, more for more environments, based on Steinke et al. 2017.
- **Yield.** Rankings for reliability, field visits for absolute yields, farmer volumetric measures.
- **Data.** Proposed: link ClimMob trait lists to Crop Ontology, standardize trial metadata, and automate anonymized data publication.
- **Analysis.** PlackettLuce, ClimMobTools, gosset, chirps, climatrends.

## Stated limitations

- Precise cost comparisons are difficult because neither PVS nor tricot has a single standard implementation.
- Optimal trial dimensions need better evidence from future studies.
- Externalizing costs to farmers raises the question of whether their motivation justifies the investment.
