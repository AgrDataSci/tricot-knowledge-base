## Questions this paper answers

- How large should tricot plots be, and how many farms are needed?
- How can tricot be made inclusive of gender and social diversity?
- What ethics, privacy and traditional-knowledge rules apply?
- How can tricot produce yield estimates for genetic gain and release?
- Which ontologies and R packages support tricot data?
- How much cheaper is tricot than conventional PVS?

## Summary

The working paper takes stock of tricot as it matured into an approach ready for mainstreaming in breeding and extension, with a focus on root, tuber and banana crops. It argues that on-farm testing is complicated because many parts must align (farmer mobilization, timely seed, trial design, ethics, data collection, analysis and reporting) and that tricot streamlines and standardizes these parts.

On trial dimensions, it defends small plots and many farms. Tricot is driven by external validity, the ability to reproduce findings in target environments, which is best served by covering diverse growing conditions and users and recording them as covariates. Small plots fit on small and large farms alike, avoiding a bias toward larger farms, and reduce planting material. The authors suggest scientists' preference for large plots partly reflects the regression fallacy. For around 12 entries, 100 to 200 farms typically give solid results, in line with earlier cereal trials, and more environments call for proportionally more farms.

Inclusion is treated as part of external validity. Trials must sample the gender and social diversity of users, and farmers' knowledge of a trait depends on their role in production, processing and cooking. Tricot can extend beyond farmers to processors and consumers, as in sweetpotato product testing in Ghana and Uganda. As research with human subjects, tricot must meet ethics standards on consent, privacy and rights over traditional knowledge.

On genetic gain, rankings give yield-based reliability, the probability that a new variety beats the market leader, which suits product-replacement strategies. When absolute yields are required, as for release, some or all fields can be visited, and farmer volumetric measures (250 ml tins) have been compared with researcher estimates. The paper describes ClimMob's recent features, data standardization through Crop Ontology with cassava trait examples, and the R packages for analysis.

On cost, intensive implementations of PVS and tricot compare at roughly 40% savings for tricot, and up to tenfold savings are possible where seed is mailed and farmers use smartphones, partly because farmers contribute land and labor.

The paper also covers documentation and publication of trial data, digital development needs for ClimMob, and data standardization, illustrated with cassava trait definitions such as root yield, root size and shape, and cassava brown streak root necrosis linked to Crop Ontology. On ethics, it stresses that tricot involves human subjects and must respect consent, privacy and rights over traditional knowledge. Cost and motivation analyses for Rwanda and Ghana were reported to be under way, with results expected in 2021.

## Methods in brief

- **Plot size.** Small plots on many farms, with environmental and social covariates.
- **Number of farms.** 100–200 for about 12 entries, more for more environments, based on Steinke et al. 2017.
- **Yield.** Rankings for reliability, field visits for absolute yields, farmer volumetric measures.
- **Data.** Crop Ontology trait definitions, documentation and data publication standards.
- **Analysis.** PlackettLuce, ClimMobTools, gosset, chirps, climatrends.

## Stated limitations

- Precise cost comparisons are difficult because neither PVS nor tricot has a single standard implementation.
- Optimal trial dimensions need better evidence from future studies.
- Externalizing costs to farmers raises the question of whether their motivation justifies the investment.
