## Questions this paper answers

- Does tricot deliver the same farmer benefits as group-based PVS?
- What are the impacts of on-farm testing on varietal diversification and food security?
- How do adoption rates compare between tricot and group PVS?
- How much cheaper is tricot than group PVS?
- Do farmers drop out less from tricot than from group PVS?

## Summary

The paper is an impact evaluation comparing tricot (tricot-PVS) with the established group-based participatory variety selection approach (group-PVS) on outcomes for farmers, not just on the quality of variety information. Participatory approaches bring farmer knowledge and local context into breeding but are rarely streamlined or scaled. tricot addresses scale, but its effects on participants had not been tested against a strong benchmark.

The study covers common bean in the Trifinio area of Central America, where Guatemala, Honduras and El Salvador meet. It uses data from 1,978 smallholder farmers in 140 villages, which were randomly assigned to tricot-PVS, group-PVS or a control, with baseline and follow-up surveys. Outcomes were on-farm varietal diversification and household food security, estimated with a difference-in-difference model with inverse probability weighting and an instrumental-variable approach.

Farmers in both approaches reached comparable levels of on-farm varietal diversification relative to control farmers. Group-PVS appeared to be more effective at improving household food security, which the authors attribute to better agronomic management learned through group activities. This effect was significant against control farmers in treated villages but not against control farmers in control villages. tricot-PVS brought no food security gain. Adoption was similar. Three years later, 20% of tricot participants (146 of 739) still grew a delivered variety, against 18% in group-PVS (74 of 394), and the adoption probability under tricot was 4% lower than under group-PVS in the model.

A cost analysis found that tricot-PVS cost 28% less in total than group-PVS ($22,222 against $30,894), while it reached almost twice as many farmers (739 against 394). Per farmer, tricot-PVS cost $30 against $78. Fewer tricot farmers dropped out (17% against 32%), which the authors link to higher external validity of the data. The authors conclude that tricot achieves similar diversification at lower cost but lacks the learning effects of group approaches, and suggest combining tricot's scale with complementary agronomic training. They frame this as part of making participatory variety selection more inclusive and demand-driven.

The authors had expected both approaches to raise diversification, even though they expose farmers differently. tricot farmers observe three varieties intensively on their own farm, while group farmers observe the whole set less intensively. Both groups indeed diversified more than control farmers, and adoption was similar once the ability of each approach to deliver the preferred variety at each site was accounted for. An average adoption of about 20% is in line with other estimates and promising given that most tested varieties were new cultivars.

For food security, the authors expected group participants to benefit from agronomic training and exchanges with technical staff and other farmers, raising yields. That mechanism appears to explain the advantage of group-PVS. Among group-PVS farmers, attending agronomic meetings was more strongly linked to a lower hunger score than adopting a tested variety, although this analysis is descriptive. The results point to a trade-off between the scale and lower cost of tricot and the learning benefits of group-based approaches.

## Methods in brief

- **Design.** Villages randomly assigned to tricot-PVS, group-PVS or control.
- **Sample.** 1,978 farmers in 140 villages, baseline (2015) and endline (2018) surveys. 394 households in group-PVS, 739 in tricot-PVS and 845 controls. The design gave tricot-PVS more farmers because it was cheaper. In 77 villages, control farmers lived alongside treated farmers.
- **Treatments.** Group-PVS: 15 households per village evaluated 19 varieties on a common plot, with six meetings and five field visits over about 70 days, including agronomy training. tricot-PVS: 20 farmers per village each tested 3 of 19 varieties on their own farm, in a trial designed in ClimMob, with two central meetings and four data collection moments, results collected by phone. Both received 1.5 kg of seed of their preferred variety.
- **Outcomes.** On-farm varietal diversification (variety count and variety richness index) and household food security (Household Hunger Scale). Adoption, yield, delivery of the preferred variety and attrition were analysed as mechanisms.
- **Estimation.** Difference-in-difference with inverse probability weighting, plus an instrumental-variable approach.
- **Costs.** Itemized comparison of the two approaches (appendix table).

## Stated limitations

- Only three years passed between the end of the experiment and the endline survey, so farmers may still be testing the new varieties, in a transition stage when diversification is higher. The authors do not correct for this.
- Spillovers in the 77 villages where treated and control farmers lived together may break the conditions for the instrumental variable, so the authors read the treatment-on-the-treated effects with caution.
- Attendance could only be coded as yes or no, so different degrees of participation could not be analysed.
- The analyses of adoption, yield, seed delivery and attrition are descriptive and do not show causal relations.
- Farmers who completed the programmes differed from those who dropped out (larger households, more often female-headed, less land for beans).
