## Questions this paper answers

- How can tricot be used for late-stage evaluation of a clonal crop like cassava?
- Can trials with different sets of clones across cycles be combined?
- Do farmer sociodemographics or environment matter more for cassava performance rankings?
- How can reliability against a check support variety release?

## Summary

The study applies tricot to participatory variety selection of elite cassava clones from the NextGen breeding population at Uganda's National Crops Resources Research Institute (NaCRRI). It had three aims, namely to synthesize data from two evaluation cycles with different clone sets, to assess agronomic performance against sociodemographic and climatic factors, and to evaluate clones for both agronomic and food quality traits.

Trials ran over two cycles. In each parish, a farmer group of at least 30 men and women was selected, and the second cycle scaled out to more districts. In total, 20 elite clones and one check variety (NAROCASS 1) were evaluated with 294 men and 320 women farmers. Farmers ranked the best and worst of their three clones for traits across the season, from early vigor to harvest at 12 months after planting and processing and food qualities. After cleaning, 484 tricot trials remained (207 in cycle 1 and 277 in cycle 2), which were aggregated into one analysis.

Plackett-Luce trees were fitted with sociodemographic and climatic covariates, with a minimum node size of 10% of observations, and split stability was checked by 1,000 resampling runs on 80% of the data. Agronomic performance depended more on geographic than sociodemographic factors. The number of days with rainfall above 20 mm was the most influential climatic variable. In one node, UG130016, UG110164 and UG130007 performed best but did not differ from NAROCASS 1.

Reliability against the check, the probability of outperforming NAROCASS 1, identified clones with advantages for specific traits. MM16/1627, UG110164 and MM20180054 had high reliability for lower disease susceptibility (0.74, 0.68 and 0.65), and UG120198 and UG120156 for ease of drying (0.78 and 0.76). Overall, UG110164, UGC14170 and UG120193 emerged as candidates for release targeting food products.

The authors conclude that rank aggregation lets programmes merge cycles with different entries, saving time and resources, and that farmers' input on food quality traits is essential. They recommend keeping NAROCASS 1 as the check in future trials for comparability.

The authors explain why location outweighed farmer characteristics. Overall performance at harvest integrated fresh root yield, root shape and size and stem quality, all strongly shaped by the growing environment. Other studies in Uganda and Nigeria found that differences between women's and men's cassava preferences depend on location, and the authors argue that gender differences are too complex for simple binary models, which may explain why gender did not split the rankings. They hypothesize that UG130007 is drought tolerant, performing well in the drier node of the tree, while UG130016 is better adapted to wetter conditions, so recommendations should follow rainfall patterns.

## Methods in brief

- **Material.** 20 elite clones from NaCRRI uniform yield trials (cycles 0 and 1) plus check NAROCASS 1.
- **Recruitment.** One farmer group of at least 30 men and women per parish.
- **Evaluation.** Best and worst rankings for agronomic traits up to 12 months after planting and for processing and food traits.
- **Analysis.** Rank aggregation across cycles, Plackett-Luce trees with sociodemographic and climatic covariates (minimum node 10%), stability from 1,000 resampling runs on 80% of the data, reliability against the check.

## Stated limitations

- Men's and women's trait preferences may depend on location, as also found in Nigeria.
- Clone sets differed between cycles, so some comparisons rely only on connections through the check and shared clones.
