## Questions this paper answers

- How can tricot be used for late-stage evaluation of a clonal crop like cassava?
- Can trials with different sets of clones across cycles be combined?
- Do farmer sociodemographics or environment matter more for cassava performance rankings?
- How can reliability against a check support variety release?

## Summary

The study applies tricot to participatory variety selection of elite cassava clones from the NextGen population at Uganda's National Crops Resources Research Institute (NaCRRI), four from IITA. It had three aims: to synthesize data from two cycles with different clone sets, to assess agronomic performance against sociodemographic, geographic and climatic factors, and to evaluate clones for agronomic and food quality traits.

Trials ran in 10 districts across six agroecological zones, in 2019 to 2020 and 2021 to 2022. Cycle 1 tested 11 clones and cycle 2 tested nine, and the check NAROCASS 1 was the only entry in both. Farmer groups of at least 30 men and women per parish supplied 294 men and 320 women as hosts. Farmers ranked the best and worst of their three clones for 26 traits, from vigor at 3 months after planting to harvest at 12 months, and for a boiled product and a flour-based meal. Drought, hailstorms and some poor management caused losses, leaving 484 trials (207 in cycle 1, 277 in cycle 2) for aggregation.

Two Plackett-Luce trees were fitted on overall performance at harvest, one with sex, age and district and one with 30 climatic variables, with a minimum node size of 10%. District split the data, while sex and age were not selected. In one district node, UG130016, UG110164 and UG130007 ranked highest but did not differ from NAROCASS 1. In the climate tree, the number of days with rainfall of 20 mm or more separated drier sites (2 days or fewer) from wetter ones, and in the wetter node no clone outperformed the check. Both trees were fragile. In 1,000 resampling runs on 80% of the data, only 530 district models made a split, though district was chosen in almost 90% of those. The authors blame the single check linking the two cycles.

Reliability, the probability of outperforming NAROCASS 1, was computed for every trait. No clone beat the check for fresh root yield, size, shape or overall performance at harvest. MM16/1627, UG110164 and MM20180054 had high reliability for lower disease susceptibility (0.74, 0.68 and 0.65), UG120198 and UG120156 for ease of drying (0.78 and 0.76), and UG110164 and UGC14170 for mealiness and preferred boiled food. No clone was significantly better for flour-meal traits. UG110164, UGC14170 and UG120193 were put forward as release candidates targeting food products.

The authors conclude that rank aggregation lets programmes merge cycles with different entries, saving time and resources, and that end-users make an important contribution to crop improvement. Overall performance at harvest combined yield, root shape and size and stem quality, which may explain why location mattered more than farmer characteristics. Citing other studies, they add that gender differences can depend on location and are too complex for binary models. They hypothesize that UG130007 is drought tolerant and propose it as a parent for future crosses, to be followed by genome-wide association studies. They recommend keeping NAROCASS 1, or other clones from this study, in future trials for network connectivity.

## Methods in brief

- **Material.** 20 elite clones plus check NAROCASS 1, from uniform yield trials of cycles 0 and 1 of the NextGen population at NaCRRI. Four clones came from IITA. Cycle 1 tested 11 clones and cycle 2 tested nine, and NAROCASS 1 was the only entry in both cycles.
- **Recruitment.** One farmer group of at least 30 men and women per parish.
- **Hosts.** 10 men and 10 women per farmer group in cycle 1, 12 men and 12 women in cycle 2.
- **Plots.** 9 m by 12 m, 1 m between plants and 2 m between plots, clones blind-coded A, B and C.
- **Evaluation.** Best and worst rankings for 26 traits at 3, 6, 9 and 12 months after planting. Boiled roots were evaluated in all locations, and the flour-based meal (kwon) in five districts.
- **Analysis.** Rank aggregation across cycles, Plackett-Luce trees with sociodemographic and climatic covariates (minimum node 10%, α = 0.05 with Bonferroni correction, npseudo = 0.5, AgERA5 climate data from planting to harvest), stability from 1,000 resampling runs on 80% of the data, reliability against the check.

## Stated limitations

- Only one check variety, NAROCASS 1, linked the two cycles. This fragile link, with the smaller data in resampling, made the tree splits unstable (only 530 of 1,000 resampled district models made a split).
- With a single check, interpretation of the trial relies heavily on how that one variety responds to the environment.
- Trials were lost to drought, hailstorms and, in a few cases, poor farmer management, which reduced the sample size.
- The interaction between gender and location was not resolved and needs further study.

## Notes

- Ann Ritah Nanyonjo is first and corresponding author. The PDF states that Nanyonjo, Stephen Angudubo and Paula Iragaba share first authorship. Robert Kawuki is last author. Cite as Nanyonjo et al. 2024.
- The study covered 10 districts in total (abstract, Table 1, Figure 1). Cycle 1 used six districts and cycle 2 used eight. Four districts were added in cycle 2 (Buikwe, Busia, Kibaale, Tororo) and two were dropped (Kaberamaido, Mityana). The methods sentence "scaled out to four districts" means four new districts, not four in total.
- The counts of 26 traits and 30 climatic variables are this card's own counts of Table 3 and Table 4.
- Source issue: R20mm is defined as days with rainfall of 20 mm or more in Table 4, but the text says more than 20 mm. The table definition is used here.
- Source issue: the text calls UG130007 superior to NAROCASS 1 in the dry climate node, but Table 5 gives p = 0.064 (reliability 0.819). Treat the drought-tolerance claim as a hypothesis.
- Source issue: Section 3.3 cites p < 0.1 for UG130016 in the wet node, while Table 6 gives p = 0.278.
- Source issue: the Discussion links the district node with Mityana and Buikwe to high rainfall, mixing the district and climate trees. UG130007 ranked high in that district node too.
- Source issue: clone names vary in the source. UG15F265P001 is also written UG15FF265P001, and MM2018/0054 is also written MM20180054.
- Source issue: Table 1 gives an age SD of 47.5 for Serere in cycle 2 (mean 46.9), far above all other SDs. Probably a typo.
