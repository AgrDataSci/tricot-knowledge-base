## Questions this paper answers

- What do CGIAR and NARES teams see as the main obstacles to mainstreaming tricot?
- How should farmers and sites be sampled for representative trials?
- What should tricot SOPs specify for breeding-pipeline trials?
- How can tricot data feed variety advancement and on-farm genetic gain?
- How can tricot be scaled and its costs reduced?

## Summary

The report summarizes a meeting in Nairobi in December 2024 convened by the CGIAR Dryland Crops Program with the 1000FARMS project to mainstream tricot into the CGIAR-NARES breeding network. Twenty-six participants attended, including funders and CGIAR and NARES partners. Discussions ran in four breakout sessions, followed by cross-cutting teams that drafted action plans for the next 12 months.

On sampling and characterization, participants recommended mapping national and sub-national production zones to guide site selection, sampling across agro-ecologies and farmer typologies while avoiding biases introduced by intermediaries, prioritizing farmers with a record of timely data submission, and choosing sites that allow spatial replication.

On trial design and implementation, they called for SOPs that set minimum and maximum numbers of entries, plot sizes, seed quantities and yield measurement strategies by crop and purpose, mandatory metadata (such as trial purpose and candidate versus check), a shared glossary, and ClimMob tools for yield validation, trial classification and handling seed loss and attrition. Trials should align with market segments and target product profiles and be embedded in national release systems.

On analysis and decision-making, a framework to estimate genetic gain on-farm from tricot data was presented by Joost van Heerwaarden. Participants asked for more frequent market research, every two to three years, and for feedback to farmers before the next planting season.

On operations and scaling, they advised starting preparations six to twelve months before trials, identifying cost drivers early (seed, transport, data tools, agent payments), reducing traits, optimizing sample size, clustering trials geographically, setting up community data submission points, building national multi-crop coordination teams, and training field agents and partners. Cross-cutting teams covered the advancement process, a cross-crop coordination network and the digital ecosystem.

The final session organized participants into four cross-cutting groups, on the advancement process, cross-crop coordination networks, the digital ecosystem, and harmonized SOPs, each drafting practical steps for the next 12 months. Shared themes were institutional ownership, interoperability between tools, aligning data and decisions with market segments and target product profiles, and protocols that are both rigorous and user-friendly. The aim, in the report's framing, is for tricot to evolve from a method into a system supporting on-farm variety testing at scale in the CGIAR-NARES network.

The advancement group set six principles for using tricot in variety advancement decisions, namely inclusivity across farmer demographics and agro-ecologies, decisions based on quality multi-year data from farm, station and laboratory, pre-agreed thresholds tied to market segments and product profiles, the right stakeholders at each decision point (product design teams, NARES, breeders, extension), systems that scale across crops and regions, and continuous improvement each season. Its priorities were to define the role and weight of each data type in decisions, plan earlier with product design teams, and build capacity to interpret data and run advancement meetings.

## Methods in brief

- **Format.** Three-day in-person meeting with breakout sessions on sampling, trial design, analysis and operations, followed by cross-cutting teams.
- **Outputs.** Recommendations per theme and 12-month action plans.

## Stated limitations

- A meeting report reflecting participants' views, not an empirical study.
