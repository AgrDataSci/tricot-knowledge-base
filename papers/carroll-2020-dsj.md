## Questions this paper answers

- What are the CARE Principles and why were they created?
- How do CARE and FAIR relate, and why are both needed?
- What counts as Indigenous data?
- Why is "open by default" contested for data about communities?
- Who has started to adopt CARE?

## Summary

Open data, big data and machine learning have made data a source of power, discovery and commercial value. Indigenous Peoples, historically subject to data exploitation, feel a tension between protecting their rights and interests in data, including traditional knowledge, and supporting open science. Stephanie Russo Carroll and colleagues in the International Indigenous Data Sovereignty Interest Group of the Research Data Alliance present the first formal publication of the CARE Principles for Indigenous Data Governance.

The authors ground the principles in Indigenous rights affirmed by the 2007 UN Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples and in the movement for Indigenous data sovereignty. Indigenous data include information about lands, skies, resources and non-humans, data about Indigenous persons held by governments and institutions, and knowledge about Indigenous Peoples as collectives, such as oral histories and cultural sites. Most of these data are held by non-Indigenous institutions.

The principles were drafted at an Indigenous-led workshop in Gaborone, Botswana, during International Data Week 2018. Participants compared Indigenous and mainstream frameworks, including OCAP, the Māori data sovereignty principles and FAIR, and concluded that mainstream frameworks cover data-centric aspects well but neglect people and purpose. They then drafted and circulated four principles.

**Collective benefit.** Data ecosystems should let Indigenous Peoples benefit, through their own use of data, policy and service evaluation, and data that reflect community values. **Authority to control.** Indigenous Peoples should determine governance protocols and take part in stewardship decisions over their data, including data held by others. **Responsibility.** Those working with Indigenous data should nurture respectful relationships, build community data capacity and embed data in Indigenous languages and cultures. **Ethics.** Indigenous rights and wellbeing should guide the whole data lifecycle, with Indigenous Peoples assessing benefits, harms and future uses.

CARE is meant to work with FAIR, not replace it, under the slogan "Be FAIR and CARE". The paper gives early adopters. The Research Data Alliance affiliated with the Global Indigenous Data Alliance that hosts the principles, the Smithsonian is developing Collection CARE Notices, and the Open Data Charter discussed how CARE can refine its "open by default" principle.

The authors call for mechanisms, tools and policies to implement CARE, for applications in repositories, knowledge co-production and existing databases, and for joint implementation with FAIR. They note that the principles may also guide data about other minority or distinct communities that want trust and accountability in data use.

## Methods in brief

- **Process.** Indigenous-led workshop of academics and practitioners in Gaborone (2018), building on an analysis of Indigenous and mainstream data principles (Table 1), followed by circulation to Indigenous networks, leaders and allies for comment.
- **Framing.** Principles classified as oriented to data, people or purpose. CARE covers people and purpose, FAIR covers data.

## Stated limitations

- Implementation mechanisms, tools, policies and practices still need research and development.
- Application in different contexts (repositories, co-production, retrospective use in existing databases) remains to be explored.
