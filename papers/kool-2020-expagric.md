## Questions this paper answers

- How well do published on-farm trials in Africa describe the conditions that shape yield?
- Can on-farm agronomy experiments be reproduced from their papers?
- Do on-farm studies say for which farmers and environments their results apply?
- What is wrong with using a "farmer practice" control?
- How many farms do typical on-farm experiments use?
- What should on-farm trial reports include?

## Summary

Agronomists moved experiments from research stations into farmers' fields to make results relevant beyond the station, after learning that station findings often failed to carry over to smallholder conditions. Hanna Kool, Jens Andersson and Ken Giller at Wageningen University and CIMMYT asked whether on-farm studies actually report enough to be reproduced, and whether they define where and for whom their findings hold.

They searched Web of Science for on-farm experiments in sub-Saharan Africa published from 1986 to 2015 and kept 172 field studies run on farms by researchers. Most tested nutrients (75), followed by pests and weeds (36), tillage (26) and cultivars (22), with hotspots in East and West African countries with a strong CGIAR presence. Most studies (78%) used fewer than 20 locations, a quarter used only one or two, and 3% used 100 to 1,000 locations, so nearly all follow what the authors call common on-farm experimentation rather than stratified or at-scale designs.

To judge reproducibility they scored each paper on 23 yield-determining variables in seven categories based on production ecology (field history, crop information, land preparation, nutrients, water, weeds, pests). The average score was 8.9 of 23 (39%), ranging from 1 to 18. A high score means the design could be repeated, not that the findings would be, since conditions such as rainfall change between seasons. Nutrient management, weed management and crop information were best described. Even so, 69% named the variety, 41% gave the planting date and only 10% described all four crop variables. Pest management was not described at all in 76% of studies, land preparation in 59% and field history in 53%. Only 15% gave the previous crop, and some studies said only that farmers followed their usual practice, without saying what that was.

External validity fared worse. None of the studies explicitly defined the research population to which results apply. Only 26% described how farms, farmers or fields were chosen, and criteria such as accessibility, willingness, record keeping or being socially well integrated may shrink the population the results represent. 29% included a "farmer practice" treatment, mostly as the control. It was often poorly described and treated as uniform, although farmers' practices vary widely. The authors note that external validity is often poorly addressed even in stratified and at-scale studies. Agronomy textbooks say little about reproducibility or external validity.

The authors argue that this undermines the case for on-farm research in an era of "impact at scale". They propose that every study state under what conditions the treatment effect occurs and for whom the findings are relevant, and that journals add this to reviewer guidelines. Studies should be transparent about site and farmer selection, reflect on whether a farmer practice control represents real practice, and report the seven categories of yield-determining factors, possibly in supplementary material. Citing Coe et al. (2003), they note that comparing host farmers with the wider population is one way to check for selection bias.

## Methods in brief

- **Search.** Web of Science Core Collection, 28 October 2016, topic search for experiments or trials on farms in Africa, 1986-2015. 1,005 hits, 767 after limiting to agricultural and environmental research areas, 744 after removing reviews. 172 retained after excluding studies outside sub-Saharan Africa, on-station or with unclear location, pot or laboratory work, natural experiments, non-agronomy topics, data published elsewhere and set-ups such as rainfall simulators.
- **Reproducibility score.** One point for any information on each of 23 variables (yield-defining, yield-limiting and yield-reducing factors), maximum 23.
- **External validity.** Indirect assessment from descriptions of the study region, selection criteria, sample characteristics and farmer practice treatments.
- **Locations.** Largest number of farms or fields in one season per study.

## Stated limitations

- The search is not exhaustive. A search by country names doubled the hits, but the authors found the same distribution across countries and assume the findings also apply.
- External validity could only be assessed indirectly, from what papers report, without independent field research.
- The score records whether information is given, not whether a practice was applied. A study that clearly stated no fertilizer was used got full points for nutrients. A full score means the design could be repeated, not that the findings would be, since conditions such as rainfall vary between seasons.
- Annual publication counts (Figure 1) need caution, because later publication records hold more abstracts and keywords and so are more likely to be found.

## Notes

- Mostly about agronomy trials. Only 22 of 172 studies had cultivars as their main topic, and 16% varied cultivars between treatments. Use it as independent support for the argument that host-farmer sampling and research population definition matter for on-farm testing (cairns-2025-ppp, cairns-2026-jxb, desousa-2024-asd).
- The authors cite one study that let farmers manage the farmer practice plot their own way. This adds variation but likely raises external validity. Farmers there also copied practices from the trial plots (p. 600). This is relevant to tricot, where farmers manage their own plots.
- Its call to report field history, management and environment matches the tricot SOP emphasis on metadata such as planting date, GPS and plot size (chase-2026-book).
- See laajaj-2020-scirep for evidence that yield gains in researcher-run trials shrink under farmers' own conditions, and hansson-2019-ejps on farmers' own experiments.
