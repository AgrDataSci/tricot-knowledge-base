## Questions this paper answers

- How well do published on-farm trials in Africa describe the conditions that shape yield?
- Can on-farm agronomy experiments be reproduced from their papers?
- Do on-farm studies say for which farmers and environments their results apply?
- What is wrong with using a "farmer practice" control?
- How many farms do typical on-farm experiments use?
- What should on-farm trial reports include?

## Summary

Agronomists moved experiments from research stations into farmers' fields to make results relevant beyond the station, after learning that station findings often failed to carry over to smallholder conditions. Hanna Kool, Jens Andersson and Ken Giller at Wageningen University and CIMMYT asked whether on-farm studies actually report enough to be reproduced, and whether they define where and for whom their findings hold.

They searched Web of Science for on-farm experiments in sub-Saharan Africa published from 1986 to 2015 and kept 172 field studies run on farms by researchers. Most tested nutrients (75), followed by pests and weeds (36), tillage (26) and cultivars (22), with hotspots near CGIAR centres. Most studies (78%) used fewer than 20 locations, a quarter used only one or two, and 3% used 100 to 1,000 locations, so nearly all follow what the authors call common on-farm experimentation rather than stratified or at-scale designs.

To judge reproducibility they scored each paper on 23 yield-determining variables in seven categories based on production ecology (field history, crop information, land preparation, nutrients, water, weeds, pests). The average score was 8.9 of 23 (39%), ranging from 1 to 18. Nutrient management, weed management and crop information were best described. Pest management was not described at all in 76% of studies, land preparation in 59% and field history in 53%. Only 15% gave the previous crop, and many said only that farmers used their usual practice.

External validity fared worse. None of the studies explicitly defined the research population to which results apply. Only 26% described how farms, farmers or fields were chosen, and criteria such as accessibility, willingness, record keeping or being socially well integrated may shrink the population the results represent. 29% included a "farmer practice" control, usually poorly described and treated as uniform, although farmers' practices vary widely. Agronomy textbooks say little about reproducibility or external validity.

The authors argue that this undermines the case for on-farm research in an era of "impact at scale". They propose that every study state under what conditions the treatment effect occurs and for whom the findings are relevant, be transparent about site and farmer selection, reflect on whether a farmer practice control represents real practice, report the seven categories of yield-determining factors, possibly in supplementary material, and compare host farmers with the wider population.

## Methods in brief

- **Search.** Web of Science Core Collection, 28 October 2016, experiments or trials on farms in Africa, 1986-2015. 1,005 hits, 744 non-review papers, 172 retained after excluding on-station, pot, natural experiments, non-agronomy and duplicate data.
- **Reproducibility score.** One point for any information on each of 23 variables (yield-defining, yield-limiting and yield-reducing factors), maximum 23.
- **External validity.** Indirect assessment from descriptions of the study region, selection criteria, sample characteristics and farmer practice treatments.
- **Locations.** Largest number of farms or fields in one season per study.

## Stated limitations

- The search is not exhaustive; a search by country names doubled the hits, though the authors judge the distribution unchanged.
- External validity could only be assessed indirectly, from what papers report, without independent field research.
- The score records whether information is given, not whether the practice was done well.
