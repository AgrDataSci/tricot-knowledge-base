## Questions this paper answers

- Do women and men in Africa prefer different maize traits and varieties?
- Is there a gender gap in adoption of improved maize, and what explains it?
- Why is gender-intentional breeding harder for maize than for roots, tubers and legumes?
- What methods could capture maize preferences by gender at scale?
- What institutional barriers limit gender-intentional breeding?

## Summary

Donors and CGIAR centres increasingly ask breeders to develop varieties that respond to gender differences in trait preferences. Rachel Voss, Jason Donovan, Pieter Rutsaert and Jill Cairns of CIMMYT review the literature on gender and maize seed adoption, variety preferences and seed systems to take stock of what is known and what is not.

On adoption, maize shows a gender gap in several countries. In Uganda, women-headed households were 26% less likely to use drought-tolerant maize than men-headed households, and adoption was 29% for male heads, 11% for wives and 5% for female heads. Gaps also appear in Malawi, Ethiopia and Kenya. However, gender often stops predicting adoption once access to land, labour, cash, credit, extension and decision-making power are controlled for, since social norms shape that access.

On preferences, reviews across crops suggest women often prioritize end-use traits such as processing, cooking time, taste and storability, and food security traits such as early maturity, while men favour production and marketability. The CGIAR Gender and Breeding Initiative, launched in 2017, built tools for gendered market segments and trait identification, mostly on cassava, potato, banana and sweetpotato. Maize differs. It is largely a commodity eaten as flour with fairly narrow end uses in a given place, its production is often not divided by gender, and breeding has focused on production traits delivered by private seed companies. The authors find the evidence of gender-differentiated maize preferences inconclusive, partly because studies are small, fragmented and methodologically inconsistent, and some interviewed men and women at different crop stages.

They call for larger samples over wider areas, more gender-responsive and coordinated methods, and tools that capture preferences in the household production context. They name farmer-managed trials across large areas, such as tricot, and online choice tools as promising, while noting that donor demands for yield gain estimates and the costs of decentralized methods mean these will not easily replace researcher-managed trials. They also recall the lessons of participatory plant breeding, where involving women in priority setting increased uptake by women.

Their agenda has three parts. Understand gender relations in maize production and seed decisions, since individual preferences may not predict household seed purchases. Develop new ways to measure preferences and seed demand, since seed choice may be a foregone conclusion for many farmers. Resolve operational challenges in trait prioritization, segmentation and end-use breeding. They warn that breeding programmes are rewarded for varieties taken up by profit-driven seed companies, so incentives must change, and that gender equality in seed systems also requires attention to availability, affordability, information and agency.

## Methods in brief

- **Design.** Narrative review of literature on gender and improved maize adoption, gendered trait and variety preferences across crops and in maize, and maize seed systems in sub-Saharan Africa.
- **Output.** Knowns and unknowns, research priorities and institutional constraints for gender-intentional maize breeding.

## Stated limitations

- Existing maize preference studies are fragmented and inconsistent, so the review cannot settle whether gender-based differences exist.
- Large-scale quantitative preference studies and in-depth understanding of household dynamics are hard to combine.
