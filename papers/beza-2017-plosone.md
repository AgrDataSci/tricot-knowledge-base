## Questions this paper answers

- Why do farmers take part in tricot and citizen science trials?
- Should farmers be paid, and what rewards do they want?
- How do motivations differ between countries and education levels?
- Can mobile phones support tricot data collection?

## Summary

Agricultural citizen science depends on volunteers who give time and skills, so knowing why they join is key to recruiting and keeping them. The paper surveys farmers taking part in Bioversity's Seeds for Needs initiative, which has run crowdsourced tricot trials since 2011, on their motivations and on how they use mobile phones.

The authors built a questionnaire from motivational factors in the citizen science literature, grouped into collectivistic (contributing to science), altruistic (helping researchers), intrinsic egoistic (sharing information, pastime) and extrinsic egoistic (expecting something in return, networking with experts or community). In 2014 and 2015 they interviewed 426 farmers, 300 in India, 94 in Ethiopia and 32 in Honduras, and analyzed the answers with non-parametric tests, redundancy analysis and principal component analysis.

Motivations overlapped but differed by country. Indian farmers ranked contributing to scientific research highest. Ethiopian and Honduran farmers ranked sharing information highest. Pastime and community interaction mattered least everywhere. Country and education together explained only about 20% of the variation, with more educated farmers placing more weight on contributing to science. Two broad groups emerged, one driven by sharing, helping and contributing, and another by expectations of return and contact with experts.

About half of the farmers expected something back. What they wanted differed by country. In Ethiopia, 33% wanted agronomic advice and 24.5% production inputs. In Honduras, capacity building came first (50%). In India, 44% wanted access to new seed. Only 2% to 3% expected to receive research results, and around half of farmers in India and 44% in Ethiopia expected no reward for sharing agronomic information.

More than 90% of farmers in all three countries owned a mobile phone and already used it for extension advice and market information. The authors conclude that citizen science in agriculture is viable, that incentives should match local motivations (advice, training, seed) rather than cash, and that phones are a realistic channel for collecting data and returning results.

The country-by-country analysis adds detail. Indian farmers valued contributing to science above receiving something in return or sharing information, with helping researchers and expert interaction next. Ethiopian farmers put sharing information first, followed by helping and contributing. Honduran farmers also ranked sharing information first, followed by contributing and expert interaction. The authors relate these differences mainly to country and education, and argue that citizen science projects should design incentives around the dominant motivations of each community, using phones both to collect observations and to return advice and results.

## Methods in brief

- **Sample.** 426 structured face-to-face interviews in 2014–2015 with Seeds for Needs participants (India 300, Ethiopia 94, Honduras 32).
- **Instrument.** Motivational factors read out in the local language, farmers marking those that applied, plus questions on expected rewards and phone use.
- **Analysis.** Wilcoxon and Kruskal-Wallis tests within and between countries, redundancy analysis linking motivations to farmer characteristics, and PCA to group farmers.

## Stated limitations

- Farmers were selected by researchers and extension staff, so samples are not random and may favor engaged participants.
- Honduras had only 32 respondents.
- Farmer characteristics explained little (about 20%) of the variation in motivation.
- Phone use for trial data was not tested directly in these trials.
