## Questions this paper answers

- Why do farmers take part in tricot and citizen science trials?
- Should farmers be paid, and what rewards do they want?
- How do motivations differ between countries and education levels?
- Can mobile phones support tricot data collection?

## Summary

Agricultural citizen science depends on volunteers who give time and skills, so knowing why they join is key to recruiting and keeping them. The paper surveys farmers taking part in Bioversity's Seeds for Needs initiative, which has run crowdsourced tricot trials since 2011, on their motivations and on how they use mobile phones.

The authors built a questionnaire from motivational factors in the citizen science literature, grouped into collectivistic (contributing to science), altruistic (helping researchers), intrinsic egoistic (sharing information, pastime) and extrinsic egoistic (expecting something in return, networking with experts or community). In 2014 and 2015 they interviewed 426 farmers, 300 in India, 94 in Ethiopia and 32 in Honduras. Farmers rated each factor on a 5-point scale. The authors analyzed the answers with non-parametric tests, generalized linear models, redundancy analysis and principal component analysis.

Motivations overlapped but differed by country. Indian farmers ranked contributing to scientific research highest. Ethiopian and Honduran farmers ranked sharing information highest. Passing free time (pastime) was valued lowest in all three countries, below 2 on the 5-point scale in Ethiopia and Honduras (read from a chart) and 3.66 in India. Country and education together explained only about 20% of the variation, with more educated farmers placing more weight on contributing to science. Women and less educated farmers valued pastime more. Two broad groups emerged, one driven by sharing, helping and contributing, and another by expectations of return and interaction with experts and other community members.

The authors report that about half of the farmers expected something back. What they wanted differed by country. In Ethiopia, 33% expected agronomic advice. In Honduras, capacity building came first (50%). In India, 44% expected improved seed (seed innovation). Only 2% to 3% expected to receive research results, and money was named by only 1% of Indian farmers. Asked about rewards for sharing information in future (India and Ethiopia only), around half of farmers in India and 44% in Ethiopia expected none. Among Indian farmers who did want a reward, the paper reports that 11% named money.

More than 90% of farmers in all three countries owned a mobile phone. Use for agricultural advice varied (77% in Ethiopia, 37% in India, 21% in Honduras), and none had yet used a phone to report trial results. Most preferred calls to SMS, and only 21% of Ethiopian farmers could read SMS.

The authors conclude that citizen science in agriculture has high potential but needs easy-to-use mechanisms, such as Interactive Voice Response. Agronomic advice, capacity building and seed were the incentives most wanted. On gamification, they note that pastime scored low and intrinsic motives scored high, so game elements that stress extrinsic rewards, such as scoreboards or badges, would likely be counterproductive. They suggest that projects might need different approaches in each country to attract and keep farmers.

## Methods in brief

- **Sample.** 426 structured face-to-face interviews in 2014–2015 with Seeds for Needs participants (India 300, Ethiopia 94, Honduras 32).
- **Instrument.** Seven motivational factors read out one by one in the local language and rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = not important at all, 5 = very important), plus open questions on other motivations, expected returns and rewards (the reward question was asked only in India and Ethiopia), farmer characteristics and phone use.
- **Analysis.** Friedman tests with Wilcoxon signed-rank post-hoc tests within countries, Kruskal-Wallis tests with Dunn-Bonferroni post-hoc tests between countries, generalized linear models and redundancy analysis linking motivations to farmer characteristics, PCA to group motivations, and qualitative content analysis of open answers.

## Stated limitations

- The study was exploratory and does not claim to represent farmers in the three countries statistically, for example by age or gender. Farmers were chosen by researchers, extension workers or NGO staff, and in India and Ethiopia preference went to those with more than one season in the trials, so respondents were an already motivated group.
- Farmer characteristics explained little (about 20%) of the variation in motivation.
- Phone use for trial data was not tested directly in these trials.
- Answers were self-reported in interviews. The authors suggest role-playing games to cross-check them in future studies.
- The authors note that farmers may have read "pastime" as frivolous fun rather than enjoyment of work, which may explain its low score.

## Notes

- This is the motivation study cited by de Sousa et al. 2024 (desousa-2024-asd), which reports it as 426 farmers.
- The high male share (83%) means motivations of women participants are under-represented.
- Card note: Honduras had only 32 respondents, and education data were missing for 9 of them (28%, Table 2, p. 9). The authors do not discuss this sample size.
- Source issue: the Discussion (p. 20) says community interaction was valued less in all three countries, but Fig 2 and the Results (pp. 8 to 10) show it close to other factors in India and above expectation and helping in Honduras.
- Source issue: the Conclusions (p. 22) say about half of farmers expected something in return. No single result gives this. The closest is Fig 7 (India and Ethiopia only), where about 50% and 44% expected no reward for sharing information.
- Source issue: Figs 5 and 7 do not give a denominator, and bars do not sum to 100% (read from charts). Percentages may refer to different groups or allow several answers.
