The card is accurate on the review's main argument: the promises of participation, the institutional barriers, the four opportunities and the cautions on cost and inclusion. Nothing is contradicted. The most important fix is to add the farmer motivation evidence the review reports (Beza et al. 2017, and Dehnen-Schmutz et al. 2016 with 9 to 18% versus 37% rating payment essential, p. 9). It is the most tricot-relevant content of the paper, and adding it also brings the summary from 381 words into the 400 to 500 range. The gist and the motivation paragraph are a little more certain or more loosely paraphrased than the source and need small rewording.

## Fixes needed

1. **Summary is too short and misses the farmer motivation evidence (high).** The card says only that "Citizen science studies why people take part". The source (p. 9) reports that Beza et al. (2017) found farmers in India, Honduras and Ethiopia were mainly motivated by contributing to science and sharing information, and rarely mentioned money. It also reports that among British and French farmers (Dehnen-Schmutz et al. 2016), 9 to 18% rated financial compensation essential for observation and data collection tasks, but 37% did so for experimentation tasks. The summary has 381 words (target 400 to 500). Suggested full replacement summary (496 words), which also includes fixes 2, 4, 5 and 6:

   > Farmers have long taken part in agricultural research, but farmer participation became a specific research focus only in the early 1980s, promising more relevant technologies, empowerment and sustainability. Yet after four decades it remains outside the mainstream of technology development, and in plant breeding it is still not standard. Jeske van de Gevel and Jacob van Etten, with Sebastian Deterding of the Digital Creativity Labs at the University of York, review the literature to ask whether citizen science can renew it.
   >
   > They first set out the promises. Participation can make research more effective and efficient, empower farmers and support sustainability, though benefits are hard to capture in cost-benefit ratios. They then explain why it stalled. Institutions lack learning and knowledge management, staff turnover is high, incentives reward data production over impact, and accountability to end users is weak. In international rice research, for example, participatory variety selection had gained legitimacy, but its findings had no established route into research decisions. Researchers also did little research on participation itself, and the field held a one-dimensional view that more participation is always better.
   >
   > Citizen science, a term that emerged over the previous 15 years, covers public involvement in research, often enabled by digital tools. It promises a "double win" of efficient knowledge generation and learning for participants. Agriculture is still a small part of it, with less than 2% of peer-reviewed citizen science articles. Examples include on-farm crop variety evaluation with ClimMob (van Etten et al. 2019), whose results questioned current variety recommendations for overlooking important spatial aspects, and PlantVillage, which uses digital tools to monitor pests and diseases. The authors identify four opportunities it opens. It brings interdisciplinary collaboration with designers and human-computer interaction researchers. It rethinks socio-computational configurations, distributing tasks across many participants with differentiated roles, from passive data contribution to co-design. It connects to research on democratizing science. And it creates new accountabilities to participants and stakeholders.
   >
   > A central point is motivation. In India, Honduras and Ethiopia, farmers were mainly motivated by contributing to science and sharing information, and rarely expected payment. Among British and French farmers, 9 to 18% rated payment essential for observation and data collection tasks, but 37% did so for experimentation. Participatory agricultural research, by contrast, treated motivation mainly as a cost-benefit question and often relied on payments or free inputs. In the authors' view, the main difference from earlier participatory research is that citizen science can distribute work in more diverse ways and match each kind of work to a type of motivation. This needs careful design of the participant experience.
   >
   > They caution that citizen science is not simply cheaper. It shifts costs to citizen scientists and adds new ones for digital tools, data verification and community management. Unequal access to digital tools and literacy creates new inclusion risks, and extractive forms remain possible. With persistent institutional support and investment, they conclude, citizen science can change how agricultural science engages farming communities and fulfil more of the promises of participation.

2. **Motivation paragraph sets up a contrast the source does not make (medium).** The card says projects should design the participant experience "rather than assuming participation is its own reward". The source contrast is different (p. 9): participatory agricultural research looked at motivation from a narrow cost-benefit angle and often relied on payments or free inputs. It did not treat motivation as a design question. The source also calls the alignment of work with motivation "the main difference" from earlier participatory research, in the authors' own view (p. 12). Replace the paragraph with the fourth paragraph of the summary in fix 1.

3. **Gist is more certain than the source (medium).** The card says citizen science "can overcome the barriers". The source makes this conditional: only with appropriate and persistent institutional support and investment are such forms "likely to overcome" the barriers (p. 12, abstract p. 1). Suggested gist (25 words):

   `gist: Review arguing that citizen science, through digital tools, differentiated roles and motivation-aware design, could help participatory agricultural research reach the mainstream, given persistent institutional support`

4. **Opening sentence implies participation began in the 1980s (low).** The card says participatory research "has been part of agricultural science since the early 1980s". The source says farmers have always taken part in research, and farmer-participatory research became a specific focus in the early 1980s (p. 1). Replacement: "Farmers have long taken part in agricultural research, but farmer participation became a specific research focus only in the early 1980s, promising more relevant technologies, empowerment and sustainability."

5. **Participatory variety selection example is generalised beyond its context (low).** The card says findings from participatory variety selection "have no established route into research decisions" as a general fact. The source reports this from Sumberg et al. (2013) for international rice research (p. 5). Replacement: "In international rice research, for example, participatory variety selection had gained legitimacy, but its findings had no established route into research decisions."

6. **PlantVillage described as an advisory tool (low).** The source describes PlantVillage as using digital tools to monitor pests and diseases (p. 7), and Table 1 lists it as plant disease identification through a user-moderated Q&A forum (p. 8). It does not call it advisory. Use the wording in fix 1: "PlantVillage, which uses digital tools to monitor pests and diseases". Fix 1 also adds the result the review cites from van Etten et al. (2019): the project results questioned current variety recommendations for overlooking important spatial aspects (p. 10).

7. **Methods "Structure" bullet mislabels the sections (medium).** The card lists four impulses as "definitions, motivation and engagement, rethinking participation, digital tools and inclusion". Section 4 has five subsections: 4.1 citizen science and participatory research, 4.2 participant motivation and engagement, 4.3 rethinking participation, 4.4 changing accountabilities and challenging epistemologies, 4.5 designing participation (pp. 6 to 11). The card drops accountabilities. Also add that no search method is reported. Replacement bullets:

   `- **Design.** Critical (narrative) literature review of participatory agricultural research and citizen science across disciplines. No search strategy, databases or inclusion criteria are reported.`

   `- **Structure.** Promises of participation (effectiveness and efficiency, empowerment, sustainability), two barriers to mainstreaming (entrenched institutions and incentives, lack of research on participation itself), and five subsections on citizen science (definitions, motivation and engagement, rethinking participation, changing accountabilities and epistemologies, designing participation with HCI). Table 1 lists 12 citizen science examples by discipline, 2 of them in agriculture.`

8. **Notes state outside knowledge without a label (low).** "Conceptual background to tricot by its co-founder" is not in the source. The paper never uses the word tricot. It cites crowdsourced crop variety evaluation (van Etten et al. 2019, climmob.net). Replacement first note:

   `- Outside knowledge, not stated in the paper: Jacob van Etten co-developed tricot. The paper does not use the word tricot. It cites on-farm crop variety evaluation (van Etten et al. 2019, climmob.net) as agricultural citizen science. For the empirical work see beza-2017-plosone on farmer motivation, steinke-2017-asd on data quality and vanetten-2019-expagric on the method.`

   In the relations comment, "(tricot, ClimMob)" is also outside labelling. Suggest `# cites crowdsourced crop variety evaluation (climmob.net) as agricultural citizen science`.

9. **not_established is accurate but weak (medium).** Add the conditional nature of the conclusion, the review's method, and the compensation finding. Suggested field:

   `not_established: that citizen science lowers total research costs (it shifts and creates costs); that digital citizen science is socially inclusive by default (unequal access and literacy are flagged); that citizen science has been shown to overcome the barriers to mainstreaming (the review argues potential, conditional on persistent institutional support, from a narrative review without a stated search method); that farmers will join any task without compensation (in one cited survey 37% rated payment essential for experimentation tasks); quantitative effects of crowdsourced variety evaluation (tricot), which the review cites only as an example`

10. **Stated limitations miss the authors' own caveats (medium).** Add:

   `- Citizen science will face the same barriers as earlier participatory research once initial enthusiasm fades (p. 2).`

   `- Broad definitions do not clearly separate citizen science from participatory action research and participatory monitoring (p. 6).`

   `- Citizen science grew mainly in rich economies and academic natural science, while participatory agricultural research grew in applied work in poor rural areas, so lessons may not transfer directly (p. 8).`

   `- Citizen science data raise cross-disciplinary challenges such as opportunistic sampling and biases (p. 12).`

11. **Key numbers: add rows (medium).**

   `| four decades | Time agricultural research institutions have explored participatory research without it becoming mainstream | This paper |`

   `| <2% | Share of peer-reviewed citizen science articles that combine citizen science and agriculture | Ryan et al. 2018, cited in this paper |`

   `| 9 to 18% vs 37% | British and French farmers rating financial compensation essential, for observation and data collection tasks versus experimentation tasks | Dehnen-Schmutz et al. 2016, cited in this paper |`

   `| 12 (2 in agriculture) | Citizen science examples in Table 1, by discipline | This paper (Table 1, p. 8) |`
