The card is accurate and well attributed. Every Key numbers row matches the source and credits the right primary study. The most important fix is a gap, not an error: the summary leaves out the paper's critique of participatory varietal selection (PVS) and researcher-managed trials (p. 397 to 398), which is the direct rationale for the tricot mention and the part a tricot user most needs. The Notes also wrongly say the PDF carries no licence statement, but page 392 shows a CC BY badge.

## Fixes needed

1. **Add the PVS critique and the maize preference results (high importance).** The summary says the evidence is inconclusive but does not say what the maize studies found or why common evaluations may miss gender differences. Source p. 395 says yield, early maturity and stress tolerance were top traits regardless of gender, and differences were often minor. Source p. 397 to 398 says PVS trials are often researcher-managed, yield more than farmers' fields, exclude intercropping (which women practise more), sit late in the pipeline with narrow genetic variation, use traits chosen by researchers, and are scored near harvest, which leaves out processing, cooking and storage. The card's "studies are small" is also not stated as a reason (p. 400 says fragmented and methodologically inconsistent). In paragraph 3 of the Summary, replace the last sentence ("The authors find the evidence ... at different crop stages.") with:

   > In maize studies, yield, early maturity and stress tolerance were top traits for both women and men, and differences were small. The authors find this evidence inconclusive, partly because studies are fragmented and methodologically inconsistent. Participatory varietal selection may also miss differences. It is often built into researcher-managed trials that yield more than farmers' fields and exclude intercropping, which women practise more. Late-stage trials show little genetic variation, researchers choose the traits, and scoring near harvest leaves out processing, cooking and storage.

   Together with fix 2, the summary comes to about 497 words. Recount after pasting and trim a few words if it goes over 500.

2. **Separate the two Uganda studies and show the mixed Ethiopia evidence (medium).** The card puts the 26% figure (Fisher and Carr 2015) and the 29% / 11% / 5% figures (Fisher et al. 2019) in one sentence as if they came from one study. "Gaps also appear in ... Ethiopia" leaves out that a later Ethiopian study found no gap in the rate of use, only in intensity (Gebre et al. 2019, p. 393). Replace Summary paragraph 2 with:

   > On adoption, maize shows a gender gap in several countries. In Uganda, women-headed households were 26% less likely than men-headed households to use drought-tolerant maize (Fisher and Carr 2015), and in another study adoption was 29% for male heads, 11% for wives and 5% for female heads (Fisher et al. 2019). Gaps also appear in Malawi, Ethiopia and Kenya, though a later Ethiopian study found none in the rate of use. Gender often stops predicting adoption once access to land, labour, cash, credit, extension and decision-making power are controlled for, and social norms shape that access.

3. **Correct the licence note (Contradicted).** The card says the published PDF carries no licence statement. Page 392 (PDF p. 1) shows a CC BY icon under "© The Author(s) 2021". There is no written licence sentence. Replace the first Notes bullet with:

   > - The published PDF shows a CC BY badge on the first page (p. 392) but no written licence sentence. The acknowledgements (p. 401) say a CC BY 4.0 licence applies to the accepted manuscript under the Gates Foundation's grant conditions.

   (The card's claim about the publisher's page and Crossref was not checked, no web access.)

4. **Key numbers, "5-6 years" (Imprecise).** Source p. 401 says "at least five to six years". Replace the row with:

   > | at least 5-6 years | Time before new maize seed typically reaches farmers | This paper |

5. **Add missing key numbers (medium).** Paste these rows:

   > | 2-3 times | Women's greater willingness than men to give up yield for storability, drought tolerance and Striga resistance, choice experiment, Kenya | Marenya et al. 2021, cited in this paper |
   > | 52% | Adoption of modern maize varieties in Africa (range 31% pearl millet to 64% wheat across ten food crops) | Walker et al. 2015, cited in this paper |
   > | 10-15 (up to 36) | Agronomic traits required in national VCU testing (36 in Ghana), which leaves little room for new gender-relevant traits | Setimela et al. 2009, cited in this paper |
   > | over 200% | Increase in modern maize adoption among female-headed households with subsidies, Malawi | Fisher and Kandiwa 2014, cited in this paper |

6. **Strengthen not_established (low).** Add one item about the PVS critique, which is argued from design features and not tested. Suggested field:

   > not_established: that women and men do not differ in maize preferences (the review says evidence is inconclusive, not absent); that gender-differentiated trait preferences explain the gender gap in maize adoption; that PVS or researcher-managed trials hide women's preferences (argued from trial design, not tested); that tricot reveals gender differences in maize (cited as a promising option, not tested here); that gender-intentional breeding improves women's adoption

7. **Optional, Methods (low).** The review does not describe how it searched or selected the literature. Add a methods bullet:

   > - **Scope.** The paper does not describe a search strategy or inclusion criteria. It is a narrative review by CIMMYT authors, focused on eastern and southern Africa.
