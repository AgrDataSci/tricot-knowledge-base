The core facts are right: the genotype counts, the 1,433 participants, the top genotypes in each country, the pooled-versus-separate significance for PG17136-N1, and the 88% / 81% taste agreement all match the source. The weak parts are the conclusion paragraph, which credits the authors with claims they do not make (low cost, complementing sensory panels, pairing with nutritional profiling), the gist, which calls this the "first" tricot consumer test and states taste as the main driver without saying it was only tested in Uganda, and several high-importance omissions. The most important fix is to rewrite the conclusion paragraph from the actual Conclusions (p. 14 to 15) and to add the small Ghana home sample (n = 118 for 21 genotypes, p. 12) and the Uganda tree result where NASPOT 12, not Ejumula, ranked first in Gulu home tasting (p. 13, Figure 8B).

## Fixes needed

1. **Conclusion paragraph credits claims the source does not make.** The card says the authors conclude tricot is "practical, scalable", works "at low cost", can "complement sensory panels", and that they "recommend pairing it with nutritional profiling". The Conclusions (p. 14 to 15) say tricot is a robust tool that can be adapted to consumer studies, and that using it for both on-farm and consumer testing gives one consistent method to inform release decisions. Cost was not measured (the only cost statement is a general one in the Introduction, p. 2, citing Minet et al. 2017). Sensory panels are not mentioned. On nutrition, the authors only say raw-root profiles do not reflect boiled roots and that variety-specific nutrient retention data would be important (p. 13). Replace the fourth Summary paragraph with:

   > The authors conclude that tricot is a robust tool that can be adapted to consumer preference studies. Using it for both on-farm trials and consumer tests gives breeders and food scientists one consistent method to inform variety release. They advise choosing home tasting, centralized tasting or both with care, planning sample sizes and recruitment, and recording food preparation and participant characteristics.

2. **Gist has an unsupported "first" and an unscoped taste claim.** The source never says this is the first tricot consumer test. Taste was only analyzed in Uganda (p. 5, p. 13). Replace the gist with (29 words):

   > gist: tricot rankings by 1,433 consumers of boiled sweetpotato in Ghana and Uganda gave the same top genotype per country in home and centralized tasting, with taste driving Ugandan preferences

3. **Small Ghana home sample and low power are missing.** The authors say Ghana home tasting had low significance, probably because only 118 people tested 21 genotypes, and they call for larger consumer samples, especially for home tasting (p. 12). This is the main practical lesson for tricot users and it also qualifies the card's claim that tricot works "without overloading individual tasters". Add to Stated limitations:

   > - Home tasting in Ghana had only 118 participants for 21 genotypes, which gave low statistical power. The authors call for larger consumer samples, especially for home tasting.

4. **Sixth Summary paragraph overstates consistency and misdescribes the Ghana tree.** The card says the Ghana tree separated "Bole, Bongo and Kasena-Nankana West" while "the top genotype stayed stable". The split was found only in centralized tasting, and it grouped Bole with Bongo against Kasena-Nankana West. In Kasena-Nankana West, SARI-Nan had the top letter (a) and PG17136-N1 had ab (p. 10, Figure 7A on p. 12). The card also omits the Uganda trees, where both trials split Gulu from Soroti/Serere and Wakiso, and NASPOT 12 ranked first in Gulu home tasting (p. 10, Figure 8B on p. 13). The phrases "showing the value of combining formats" and "The study shows that tricot can rank many genotypes ... robust enough to feed breeding decisions on which clones to advance" go beyond the source, which says the PG17136-N1 result is robust "for the study area" (p. 12) and talks about release decisions (p. 14). The olaosebikan-2023-jsfa sentence is outside knowledge. Replace the sixth paragraph with:

   > In Ghana, PG17136-N1 is a white-fleshed, high-dry-matter clone at the advanced trial stage. Its advantage over Obare was statistically significant only when home and centralized data were pooled (p = 0.019). Home tasting in Ghana had only 118 participants for 21 genotypes, which gave low statistical power, and the authors call for larger consumer samples, especially for home tasting. In the Ghana centralized trial, the tree split Bole and Bongo from Kasena-Nankana West, where SARI-Nan ranked just above PG17136-N1. In Uganda, both trees split Gulu from Soroti/Serere and Wakiso, and in Gulu home tasting NASPOT 12, not Ejumula, ranked first. Averaged across tree nodes, PG17136-N1 and Ejumula remained the best choices.

   And add to Notes:

   > - Outside this paper: later tricot consumer studies, such as olaosebikan-2023-jsfa for cassava, use a similar design.

5. **Ghana traits misdescribed.** The card says participants ranked "for overall preference and sensory traits such as taste". In Ghana only overall acceptability was analyzed, because taste and color data were largely missing or incomplete (p. 5). Without this, a reader may think taste was shown to drive preference in both countries. Covered by the second paragraph replacement in fix 6.

6. **Centralized tasting venues and cooking misdescribed.** The card says centralized tasting was at "common locations such as schools". Schools were the Uganda venues. In Ghana, the team intercepted sweetpotato buyers near fresh-produce markets (p. 5). Replace the second Summary paragraph with:

   > The study tested 27 genotypes, 21 in Ghana (15 at late breeding stages and 6 released varieties) and 6 released, widely grown varieties in Uganda. A total of 1,433 participants took part. In each country, the trial was split into centralized tasting and home tasting. In centralized tasting, the team cooked the roots within 48 hours of harvest and served them at markets in Ghana, where sweetpotato buyers were intercepted, and at pre-arranged venues such as schools in Uganda. In home tasting, households received raw roots and cooked them their usual way. Participants chose the best and worst of their three genotypes. Only overall acceptability was analyzed in Ghana, because taste and color data were largely missing, while Uganda also had taste and color. Rankings were analyzed with Plackett-Luce models and trees, using district, age and gender as covariates.

7. **First Summary paragraph: aim and rationale imprecise.** "Bring end-user preferences into breeding decisions earlier" is not one of the stated aims. The three objectives are to validate tricot for consumer preferences, compare centralized and home tasting, and document key aspects of the method (p. 2 to 3). The source says breeding trials "sometimes" have more than 10 varieties (p. 2), not "often", and the problem it names is consumer fatigue (p. 2). Replace the first Summary paragraph with:

   > The paper adapts tricot to consumer preference testing of boiled sweetpotato. It aims to validate tricot for consumer testing, compare centralized and home tasting, and document how to run both. Breeding trials sometimes include more than ten genotypes, and tasting many samples tires consumers, so each participant tastes and ranks a random set of three.

8. **Third Summary paragraph: "outperformed", color and descriptors.** (a) PG17136-N1 "outperformed" Obare in both trials only as a point estimate. Within each trial it shared a significance letter with Obare (Figure 4A and 4B, p. 8, and p. 11 to 12). Use "ranked above". (b) The source calls the color correlation "negative", but Kendall τ is −0.032 (Table 6, p. 10), which is close to zero. (c) The descriptors come from both countries pooled (p. 10, Figure 6A), but the sentence sits after the Uganda results. (d) The sentence on non-significance when analyzed separately can move to the sixth paragraph, where the pooled result is given, to avoid repeating it. Replace the third Summary paragraph with:

   > The most preferred genotypes were consistent across tasting formats, while the order of the rest varied. In Ghana, the advanced clone PG17136-N1 ranked above the reference variety SARI-Nyumingre (Obare), the most popular variety in the area, in both trials, and ranked first in home tasting. In Uganda, consumers preferred Ejumula, an orange-fleshed, high-dry-matter variety, followed by NASPOT 13, both outperforming the reference NASPOT 8. Taste drove overall rankings in Uganda. In 88% of cases where a genotype ranked best for taste, it also ranked best overall, and 81% for worst, while color showed almost no correlation with overall acceptability (Kendall τ = −0.03). Across both countries, "good taste" and "sweet" were the commonest reasons for best rankings, and "tasteless" or "not sweet" for worst rankings.

   With fixes 1, 4, 6, 7 and 8 together, the Summary is 491 words (within 400 to 500). The fifth paragraph of the current card is dropped because its content moves into the new sixth paragraph.

9. **Add not_established.** The card has none. Suggested text:

   > not_established: that taste drives preference in Ghana (taste was not analyzed there); that tricot consumer testing costs less than sensory panels (cost was not measured); that home tasting alone, with about 100 participants, can separate 20 or more genotypes; that the same top genotype holds in every district or segment (NASPOT 12 led in Gulu home tasting); that raw-root nutrient profiles describe the boiled product; that consumer rankings predict adoption or market demand

10. **Stated limitations: fix one item and add missing ones.** "Less control over cooking and storage" is imprecise. The source names cooking time, water, serving temperature and tasting conditions (p. 13), not storage. Replace the first limitation with:

    > - Home tasting gives less control over cooking time, water, serving temperature and tasting conditions.

    And add (besides the item in fix 3):

    > - Taste and color were not analyzed in Ghana because of missing or incomplete data.
    > - Home and centralized participants may differ in gender, socio-economic and age profile, which may explain part of the difference in rankings.
    > - Raw-root nutrient profiles do not describe boiled roots, because sugars change and leach during boiling.
    > - Some home participants let people under 18 fill questionnaires, which made them invalid. Ghana market interception gave less attentive answers.

11. **Methods in brief: add participants, traits and cooking.** Suggested bullets:

    > - **Participants.** 1,433 in total: Ghana 897 centralized and 118 home, Uganda 144 centralized and 274 home.
    > - **Traits.** Best and worst for overall acceptability in both countries, plus taste and color in Uganda. Participants gave reasons for their best and worst choices.
    > - **Cooking.** Centralized samples were washed and boiled in plain water, covered with banana leaves in Gulu and Soroti/Serere, and peeled and steamed in banana leaves in Wakiso.
    > - **Analysis.** Plackett-Luce models with significance letters, Plackett-Luce trees with district, age and gender as covariates (α = 0.1, minimum node size 10), Bland-Altman comparison of home and centralized estimates, Kendall correlation between traits, and text analysis of reasons. Data and R code are on Zenodo.

    The last bullet replaces the current Analysis bullet.
