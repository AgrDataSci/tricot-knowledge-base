The card gets the three hypothesis outcomes right, attributes the secondhand numbers correctly, and the automated pre-check found no missing numbers. Nothing is contradicted. The weak points are the gender paragraph, which gives the wrong reason for the unresolved verdict and drops the authors' "some support" conclusion, the missing case-study results (banana hybrids, the Nigerian DNA fingerprinting figures), and a Stated limitations section that leaves out the two limitations the authors name themselves. The verdict sits at the upper end of Minor fixes because the fixes are additive and the summary has room (396 words now).

## Fixes needed

1. **Summary, gender paragraph (Imprecise, changes meaning).** The card says hypothesis 2 is unresolved "because most case studies did not collect gender-disaggregated data". The source (p. 1088, PDF p. 13) says the hypothesis cannot be fully addressed. Gender was not considered for yam, but banana, potato and cassava all had some gender evidence. The Kenyan potato survey compared men's and women's reasons for adoption, not their trait preferences. The authors conclude the case studies give "some support" to the hypothesis, while more evidence is needed. The abstract (p. 1076) calls it unresolved. The card also leaves out the banana finding (women prefer cultivars for eating, men hybrids for beer, p. 1081 and p. 1088). Replace the paragraph with:

   > The second hypothesis, that neglect of gender differences in quality and post-harvest preferences weakened product profiles, could not be fully tested because few studies recorded trait preferences by gender. Where they did, differences appeared. In banana, women tended to prefer cultivars for eating and men hybrids for beer. In cassava, differences appeared in cyanogen content, ease of peeling and varieties suited to particular foods. The authors see some support but call for more evidence.

2. **Stated limitations: the authors' own two limitations are missing (high-importance omission).** The source names two "principal limitations" (p. 1087, PDF p. 12): no national or large-scale farmer surveys for most RTB crops, and case studies of one crop in one country each. It also says the case studies used local studies and personal communications (p. 1081, PDF p. 6). Add these bullets at the top of the section:

   > - National or large-scale farmer surveys of RTB variety adoption are rare in sub-Saharan Africa, so the review drew on the few that exist.
   > - Each crop was studied in only one country with good adoption data. The authors say this is not a complete picture of sub-Saharan Africa, although the findings resonate with other countries in the region.

   And add to Methods in brief:

   > - **Sources.** Few nationally representative farm surveys were available, so the case studies also draw on local adoption studies, focus groups and personal communications from the authors.

3. **Summary, missing case-study results (high-importance omissions).** The summary has no banana result and no Nigerian DNA fingerprinting numbers. It also does not say that yield and disease resistance drive RTB adoption too, which is the answer to the card's own second question. Banana: hybrids made up only 6% of mats in a Uganda survey (Kagezi et al. 2012, p. 1081) and usually lack the organoleptic traits consumers want (p. 1082, p. 1087). Cassava: 46% modern-variety area from the 2009 expert estimate versus 39.9% from the 2015 survey with fingerprinting, with unreleased farmer selections making up more than half of the modern-variety area (p. 1082, Table 2). Yield and disease resistance: Walker and Alwang (2015), p. 1080, and p. 1088. The full replacement Summary below (fix 10) adds these within the 500-word limit.

4. **Summary, first sentence (Imprecise).** "adoption of modern varieties has been slower than for cereals." The source says SSA adoption is slow compared with other parts of the world (p. 1077). RTB crops have a slightly higher varietal age than cereals, "although the difference is not enough to draw any overall conclusions", and RTB adoption area is midway between cereals and legumes (p. 1079, Table 1). Replace with:

   > Roots, tubers and bananas are the main staples in much of humid sub-Saharan Africa, yet adoption of their modern varieties is low and varietal turnover is slow.

5. **Summary, last paragraph (Imprecise, two points).** (a) "for faster release of farmer-preferred landraces where breeding is hard". The source asks for more attention to the selection and distribution of landraces where breeding is especially hard and consumer preferences are marked (p. 1089). It does not mention faster release. (b) "use DNA fingerprinting, since breeder elicitations have overestimated adoption". In the source, DNA fingerprinting is needed because farmers misidentify varieties. Breeder overestimates are a separate point (p. 1089 to 1090). Also, the RTBfoods five-step method is hedged in the source: programmes "may improve" if they use it (p. 1088). Replace the paragraph with:

   > The authors call for demand-led breeding with product profiles disaggregated by region and gender, possibly using the RTBfoods five-step method, for more attention to landraces where breeding is hard, and for more inclusive priority setting. They suggest tools such as tricot to let farmers' choices guide dissemination through informal systems. Adoption surveys should record trait preferences and gender. Breeder elicitations have often overestimated adoption and farmers often misidentify varieties, so surveys should use DNA fingerprinting.

6. **Notes, Teeken bullet (Imprecise).** "which this review could not yet draw on" suggests the review had no gender evidence from Teeken. It does cite Teeken et al. (2018) on gender differences in Nigerian cassava trait preferences, where women more often ranked varieties on product quality and men on agronomic traits (p. 1083, PDF p. 8). Replace with:

   > - Béla Teeken is a co-author. The review already cites Teeken et al. (2018) on gender differences in cassava trait preferences in Nigeria, where women more often ranked varieties on product quality and men on agronomic traits. His later cassava work on gender and trait preferences (teeken-2021-fsufs) came out after this review.

7. **Summary and Notes, "RTBfoods special issue" (Imprecise, minor).** The PDF refers to "this special issue" (p. 1077) and acknowledges RTBFoods funding (p. 1090), but it does not name the special issue. Summary text: see fix 10. Notes, first bullet, replace "Part of the RTBfoods special issue." with:

   > - Published in a special issue of the journal and funded by the RTBfoods project (Breeding RTB Products for End User Preferences).

8. **Summary, author affiliations (Imprecise, minor).** The list omits CNRA (Côte d'Ivoire), the Syngenta Foundation and the University of Applied Sciences Bingen (p. 1076). Replacement in fix 10 uses "Graham Thiele and 15 colleagues from CGIAR centres, CIRAD, CNRA, Cornell and the Syngenta Foundation".

9. **Key numbers, 1971 / 1978 row (Imprecise attribution).** 1971 is cited to Martin and Rhodes 1973 (p. 1086). The 1978 date has no direct citation. The nearest citations are Rodriguez 1983 and Doumbia et al. 2004, attached to the mechanisation sentence before it, and footnote 8 (a personal communication) after it. 10. **Full replacement Summary (500 words, combines fixes 1, 3, 4, 5, 7, 8).** Ready to paste:

   > Roots, tubers and bananas are the main staples in much of humid sub-Saharan Africa, yet adoption of their modern varieties is low and varietal turnover is slow. In 2010, modern varieties covered about a third of the RTB area, with international centres behind three quarters of that. Graham Thiele and 15 colleagues from CGIAR centres, CIRAD, CNRA, Cornell and the Syngenta Foundation reviewed the literature to explore three hypotheses about what drives varietal change. It belongs to a special issue linked to the RTBfoods project.
   >
   > They examined five case studies with the best adoption data, namely banana in Uganda, cassava in Nigeria, potato in Kenya, sweetpotato in Uganda and yam in Côte d'Ivoire.
   >
   > The first hypothesis, that breeding programmes gave too little priority to consumer-preferred traits, was strongly confirmed. Yield and disease resistance also drive adoption, but every case showed some role for consumer traits. Where consumer traits lined up with good agronomy, turnover was fast, as with yam C18's suitability for foutou and potato Shangi's short cooking time. Banana hybrids show the reverse. They usually lack the taste and texture consumers want, and in Uganda they made up only 6% of farmers' mats. Landraces dominate yam, banana and sweetpotato, and several of the most successful modern varieties are introduced or selected landraces or farmer selections (Florido and C18 yam, TME 419 cassava, Shangi potato). Seed traits also matter, such as Shangi's short dormancy or TME 419's straight stems that are easy to bundle. Farmers keep portfolios of varieties for different uses, so asking which one they prefer can mislead.
   >
   > The second hypothesis, that neglect of gender differences in quality and post-harvest preferences weakened product profiles, could not be fully tested because few studies recorded trait preferences by gender. Where they did, differences appeared. In banana, women tended to prefer cultivars for eating and men hybrids for beer. In cassava, differences appeared in cyanogen content, ease of peeling and varieties suited to particular foods. The authors see some support but call for more evidence.
   >
   > The third hypothesis, that informal seed systems slow uptake, gave mixed results. Informal systems spread C18 yam and Shangi potato rapidly, and most RTB planting material reaches farmers informally. But inconsistent variety names cause frequent mix-ups. In Nigeria, DNA fingerprinting in 2015 put modern varieties at about 40% of cassava area, against an expert estimate of 46% in 2009. Unreleased farmer selections made up more than half of that area, and many farmers misidentified whether they grew a landrace. Free vine distribution in Uganda may have undermined a working informal system.
   >
   > The authors call for demand-led breeding with product profiles disaggregated by region and gender, possibly using the RTBfoods five-step method, for more attention to landraces where breeding is hard, and for more inclusive priority setting. They suggest tools such as tricot to let farmers' choices guide dissemination through informal systems. Adoption surveys should record trait preferences and gender. Breeder elicitations have often overestimated adoption and farmers often misidentify varieties, so surveys should use DNA fingerprinting.
