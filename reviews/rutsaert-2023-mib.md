What the card says is mostly accurate. Nothing is contradicted. But the summary leaves out the main results of the brief: the overall ranking (benchmark, drought avoidance and intercropping on top, green maize and feed lowest), the confirmed demand for the current resilient segment, and the finding that farmers did not want yellow or orange maize. Instead it adds a sentence the source does not support ("maturity preferences differed sharply") and a reference to a later paper. The most important fix is to rewrite the results part of the Summary (pp. 5 to 8, Figure 4 on p. 6, Table 3 on p. 7). A full replacement Summary is given below. Page numbers printed on the PDF match the PDF page numbers (p. 1 has no printed number).

## Fixes needed

1. **Summary results are incomplete, and one sentence is not in the source (pp. 5 to 8).** The card's third paragraph gives only the two country highlights. It then says "Maturity preferences also differed sharply between the countries." The brief never reports maturity preferences by country. The only country maturity data are geospatial estimates of segment size (Figure 1, p. 3), which are not farmer preferences. The card also says the brief "flags emerging opportunities" in nutritious, yellow and green maize, but does not say that farmers ranked these low and that yellow and orange maize were not in demand (p. 7 to 8, Figure 4 p. 6). It also omits the first of the three conclusions, that farmers confirmed demand for the current resilient segment (p. 7, Table 3). Replace the whole Summary with the text below (492 words, within 400 to 500). It also fixes items 2 and 3.

   > CGIAR's market segmentation for maize in East Africa had four segments based on agro-ecological zone and maturity class. Because developing a variety takes many years, the brief asks which future segments should already shape breeding investments, and introduces video-based product concept testing (VPCT) as a tool to answer that question with farmers.
   >
   > The brief explains the conceptual and methodological basis of VPCT, which combines concepts from product innovation with the triadic comparisons of tricot. Seven new product concepts were developed with breeders, seed companies and farmers (home use, intercropping, drought avoidance, nutritious, feed with yellow maize, green maize, and food and fodder). They were tested alongside the resilient benchmark concept, the current breeding target. Each farmer watched videos of three concepts and ranked them, through household surveys with 1,200 farmers in each of Kenya and Uganda, 2,400 in total, in wet mid-altitude areas.
   >
   > The rankings were analysed with the Plackett-Luce model. Across both countries, three concepts stood out and did not differ significantly: the resilient benchmark, drought avoidance and intercropping. All three address growers' agronomic needs. Home use came next, followed by food and fodder and nutritious. Green maize and feed ranked lowest. Rankings differed by country. Intercropping stood out in Kenya, while drought avoidance, an early-maturity concept, ranked highest in Uganda.
   >
   > From these results the authors propose three types of segment. Strong demand confirms the current mid-maturity resilient segment. Two near-term future segments, stress-tolerant early-maturity maize in Uganda and maize tailored to intercropping in Kenya, should be explored further and considered for target product profile design. Nutritious orange maize, yellow feed maize and green maize are possible long-term segments. Farmers ranked all three well below the top concepts. Yellow and orange maize would need an external driver, such as a growing chicken industry or school feeding programmes. The authors argue that concept testing makes a strong case for new investments that integrate farmers' agronomic practices and preferences into breeding, on-farm testing and seed systems.
   >
   > Women showed strong interest in the home-use concept, built around post-harvest use and family consumption. The authors argue this may not warrant a separate segment, because the existing mid-maturity resilient segment already targets food use, but that its target product profile should be checked for how well consumption traits are specified. VPCT can also make breeding more gender-intentional. Concepts can be built around challenges women face that current pipelines do not target, such as weeding or harvesting groundnut, and compared with current breeding directions.
   >
   > Once future segments are identified, the authors note, programmes need estimates of the crop area each would cover and how much it would take from existing segments, considering seed production, distribution and farmers' access to information, through qualitative or geospatial approaches. They conclude that first results were promising and that the method met its goals of being cost-efficient, quick to roll out with partners, intuitive for farmers, able to compare current and potential future breeding priorities, and useful to breeding programmes, while still needing further development and testing.

2. **Outside knowledge in the Summary (last sentence).** The card says "The full analysis is published in Donovan et al. 2025 (donovan-2025-expagric), with the statistical detail behind these results." The brief does not mention this paper (it only says future work will estimate the implications for current segments, p. 1). The brief itself already reports Plackett-Luce results (Figures 4 and 6). Remove it from the Summary (done in the replacement above) and add to Notes:

   > - Outside this brief: the same study (2,400 farmers in Kenya and Uganda) is reported in full in donovan-2025-expagric, with country context data and more detail on concept and video design.

3. **Gender framing dropped (p. 8).** The card says concepts "can also be built around production challenges outside current pipelines, such as harvesting in groundnut". The source places this under "Gender intentionality in concept testing" and speaks of challenges women face (weeding, planting rice, harvesting groundnut). Fixed in the replacement Summary above.

4. **Stated limitations are not the authors' limitations (p. 1, p. 8).** "A brief rather than a full research paper" is not something the authors state. Replace the section with:

   > - Further development and testing are needed to refine the method (p. 8).
   > - The brief does not estimate how much crop area the proposed future segments would cover or take from current segments. The authors leave this to future work (pp. 1, 8).
   > - Yellow and orange maize were not in demand. Without some form of demand pull, the authors expect little uptake of such varieties (p. 8).

5. **not_established is missing.** Add:

   > not_established: that the proposed future segments were tested in field trials or confirmed by seed sales; that ranking a video concept predicts seed purchase or adoption; that gender differences in concept rankings are statistically significant (the Plackett-Luce tree gender splits had p = 0.66 in Kenya and p = 0.13 in Uganda); that the results apply outside wet mid-altitude areas of western Kenya and central Uganda; that yellow or orange maize has no future market in East Africa

6. **Gist lists what the brief does, not what it shows.** Replace with (28 words):

   > gist: Video concept testing with 2,400 Kenyan and Ugandan farmers confirmed demand for the resilient benchmark and pointed to intercropping (Kenya) and early maturity (Uganda) as future maize segments

7. **Only 3 questions (template asks for 4 to 7).** Add:

   > - How did farmers rank the resilient benchmark against new maize concepts?
   > - Did men and women farmers rank the maize concepts differently?
   > - How were the concept videos made, and did farmers find them easy to follow?

8. **Methods in brief is thin.** Replace the three bullets with:

   > - **Concepts.** Seven new concepts plus the resilient benchmark (current market segment 1, wet mid altitude, intermediate maturity). Developed from focus group discussions with farmer organisations, seed companies, retailers and national and international breeding institutes. Each concept described its main requirement, why the seed meets it, and standard details (yield, fertilizer needs, stress tolerance, color, use).
   > - **Videos.** Ten studio videos in local languages (two explaining the exercise, eight presenting concepts), set at an agro-dealer counter. Each concept lasted about two minutes. Farmers were randomly shown a male or female actor version.
   > - **Design.** Each farmer viewed three of the eight concepts, in randomized triads in incomplete blocks, and chose the most and least preferred concept to buy. After each video, farmers answered questions on similarity to their current variety and interest in trying or replacing it.
   > - **Sample.** Household surveys with 1,200 farmers per country in western Kenya and central Uganda.
   > - **Analysis.** ClimMob for data collection and analysis. Plackett-Luce model (log-worth, benchmark as reference) and a Plackett-Luce tree with country and gender as covariates.

10. **Year and peer review notes (p. 10).** The brief's recommended citation gives 2022. The PDF file was created on 30 January 2023. The card uses 2023 without comment. The series also calls itself peer-reviewed. The card's peer_reviewed: no follows the template rule for briefs, which is fine, but say so. Add to Notes:

   > - The brief's recommended citation gives the year as 2022 (p. 10). The PDF was produced in January 2023, and the card uses 2023. Check the repository record.
   > - The Market Intelligence Brief series describes itself as peer-reviewed (p. 10). The card keeps peer_reviewed: no, following the knowledge base rule for policy briefs.
   > - The text discusses gender differences (home use, and intercropping among Ugandan women), but the Plackett-Luce tree splits on gender were not significant (p = 0.66 in Kenya, p = 0.13 in Uganda, Fig. 6). Treat the gender differences as indicative.

11. **Region (minor).** Make it more precise: `region: Kenya (western), Uganda (central), wet mid-altitude` (p. 2).
