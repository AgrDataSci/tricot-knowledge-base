The card is accurate on the project history, the Ms44 technology and the tricot sample sizes, and none of its claims is contradicted. But it presents the separate storage, milling and cooking in over 100 households as a working part of the method, while the source (p. 4) reports it as a challenge that led the team to drop end-use traits after the pilot and return to one respondent per household. The card also leaves out the main result (pilot tricot data showed no major gender-based differences in preferences, p. 6) and the authors' own limitations, and the stated limitations it gives are not the ones the authors state. Page numbers below are the printed page numbers, which match the PDF pages.

## Fixes needed

1. **Post-harvest evaluation presented as a success (Summary para 4, Methods, Key numbers).** The card says attention to intrahousehold dynamics included making sure the three varieties were stored, milled and cooked separately in over 100 households so that post-harvest traits could be compared. The source (p. 4) lists this as one of the challenges that emerged. Because of limited capacity, the team abandoned end-use trait evaluations after the pilot and narrowed evaluations to one individual per household (p. 4, Figure 1 on p. 3 gives 2022 for this change, p. 6 and p. 8 confirm the difficulty). Replace Summary paragraph 4 with the text in fix 3 below. Replace the Methods bullet with:

   - **Post-harvest evaluation.** Piloted in 2021 only. Farmers ranked the three varieties on end-use traits such as storage, flour quality and taste, which needed separate storage, milling and cooking in over 100 households. The team dropped end-use traits after the pilot as too hard to manage at scale, and returned to one respondent per household.

   Replace the Key numbers row with:

   | >100 households | 2021 pilot households where the three varieties had to be stored, milled and cooked separately. End-use evaluation was dropped after the pilot | This paper |

2. **Main result missing.** The source (p. 6) says the pilot tricot trials showed no major gender-based differences in preferences that would require gender-intentional breeding. They instead supported gender-responsive breeding, confirming that FNP varieties had no negative gendered impacts and appealed under women's and men's real-world conditions. The source adds (p. 7) that without clear signs of differing preferences there is no sign that varieties targeted at women are needed. The card does not report this. See the new paragraph 5 in fix 3.

3. **Suggested Summary (496 words).** This keeps the card's structure, fixes items 1, 2, 5 and 6, adds the authors' limitations, and stays within 400 to 500 words. Paste as the full Summary:

> The paper documents how the Seed Production Technology for Africa (SPTA) project at CIMMYT, launched in 2016 with a second phase from 2020, integrated gender into a conventional maize breeding programme. It uses innovation-history analysis of project documents and the authors' own experience.
> 
> SPTA worked with Corteva on the Ms44 gene, which makes maize plants non-pollen producing and removes the need to detassel female parents in hybrid seed production. Hybrids made this way segregate one-to-one for pollen and non-pollen plants and are called 50% non-pollen producing (FNP) hybrids. The technology raises female seed yield, improves quality assurance in seed production and increases hybrid yield through reduced tassel growth and greater partitioning of nitrogen to the ear.
> 
> The team, smaller than many breeding projects, took up recommendations from a donor gender audit and engaged gender specialists to study differences in management and resource constraints. Recognizing the potential benefits of the technology for women, SPTA prioritized equitable participation of women and men as trial hosts and in participatory variety selection, reaching over 50% women and many resource-poor farmers.
> 
> tricot was central to this shift. Earlier on-farm trials had shown that farmers treated trials as demonstration plots, and that giving trials to women plot managers could cause tension when husbands did not support it. The team piloted tricot in 2021 with 112 farmers (55% women) in Kenya, under the farmers' own management, including intercropping. The pilot asked both spouses to evaluate where both managed the trial, and added end-use traits such as flour quality and taste. Keeping the three varieties stored, milled and cooked separately across over 100 households proved too hard at scale, so the team dropped end-use traits after the pilot and returned to one respondent per household. With the 1000FARMS project, trials expanded to 356 farmers (65% women) in 2022 and 1,380 farmers (56% women) in 2023.
> 
> The pilot data showed no major gender-based differences in preferences that would call for gender-intentional breeding. Instead, they supported gender-responsive breeding: FNP varieties showed no negative gendered impacts and appealed to women and men under their own conditions. The authors note that long-term impacts are yet to be seen, that no SPTA variety had been released, and that tricot data were not yet used in release decisions, which still rely on national performance trials.
> 
> The authors credit donor mandates, open-minded project leaders, funding for gender research, and respectful collaboration between breeders and social scientists.
> 
> Central to the case was handing on-farm trials to the social science team, which the authors describe as unprecedented in maize breeding. Breeders may resist this because they want to standardize across projects, doubt the technical capacity of social scientists and farmers, or are reluctant to reform a system that has worked. Researcher-managed trials may give more internally valid comparisons, and citizen science trials higher external validity. Incentives that reward heritability but not reaching under-served groups must be examined. The case shows the value of starting small and building trust between breeders and social scientists.
4. **Stated limitations do not match the source.** The card's first limitation (single-project case study based on own experience) is a fair description of the design but the authors do not state it as a limitation. The second (lessons may not transfer to larger programmes) is close but turned around: the source says the small team allowed closer collaboration than is feasible in many larger projects (p. 7), while it also calls SPTA a proof of concept for larger projects (p. 1). Replace the section with:

   ## Stated limitations

   - The long-term impacts of the changes to the breeding process are yet to be seen, and no variety developed through SPTA has been released yet (p. 6).
   - tricot data have been useful to validate FNP varieties but have not yet been used in maize variety release decisions in eastern and southern Africa, which still rely on national performance trials (p. 8).
   - Some data quality concerns persisted, and tricot trials were harder for NARES field staff to oversee than standardized trials (p. 8).
   - End-use trait evaluation in tricot proved too challenging to manage at scale with the available capacity (p. 4, p. 8).
   - The small size of the team allowed closer collaboration than is feasible in many larger projects (p. 7).

   And add to Notes: "The card writer notes that this is a single-project case study based on the authors' own experience and project documents. The authors do not state this as a limitation."

5. **Reason for equitable participation.** The card says SPTA prioritized participation "because a technology can only benefit women if they can access and evaluate it". This reasoning is the card's own. The source (p. 8) says SPTA did this recognizing the potential benefits of the technology to women. Fixed in the Summary in fix 3.

6. **Lessons paragraph is the card's synthesis.** The card says gender integration "works when breeders own it, when it is backed by specialists and resources, and when testing methods such as tricot make broad, inclusive participation feasible". The source credits donor mandates, open-minded project leaders, funding for gender research and respectful collaboration (p. 1, p. 6), and its stated lessons are to seek allies, build institutional capacity and trainings, examine incentives and start small (p. 8 to 9). Fixed in the Summary in fix 3.

7. **FNP yield mechanism.** The card says hybrid yield increases through reduced tassel growth. The source (p. 2 to 3) adds greater partitioning of nitrogen to the ear, which increases nitrogen use efficiency. This matters for the low-input argument. Fixed in fix 3.

8. **Gist lists topics instead of the finding.** Replace with (28 words):

   gist: A CIMMYT maize project integrated gender by moving on-farm trials to social scientists and tricot, reaching over 50% women. Pilot data showed no major gender differences in preferences.

9. **not_established missing.** Add:

   not_established: that women and men in Kenya prefer different maize varieties; that household end-use (post-harvest) evaluation in tricot worked at scale in this project; that tricot data were used in maize variety release decisions; that FNP hybrids had been released by 2023; that the approach has been tested in large breeding programmes; that tricot trials cost less per data point than researcher-managed trials

10. **Methods: add the trial protocol.** Add this bullet after "On-farm testing":

   - **Trial protocol.** Each farmer got three varieties, 200 kernels each, with basic guidance only: plant in the middle of the maize field, away from trees, with similar slope and soil across subplots. Farmers used their own management, including intercropping, applied the same way to all subplots. The team collected spacing data to estimate yields instead of standardizing planting.

11. **Key numbers: add.**

   | 200 kernels per variety | Seed given to each tricot farmer per variety, Kenya | This paper |
   | 200 kg/ha | Yield benefit of FNP hybrids across yield levels in early large on-farm trials | Collinson et al. 2022, cited in this paper |

12. **Metadata.** Change license to "CC BY" (the PDF states CC BY with no version, p. 1). The type "community case study" matches the source label (p. 1) but is not in the template's type list. The core team should decide whether to accept it or map it to an existing type.
