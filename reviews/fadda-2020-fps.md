The card is accurate on numbers and follows the review's structure well. Most problems are wording that is more certain than the source ("more efficient", "linking formal and informal seed systems") and two attribution errors in Key numbers (the ">10 years" figure is cited from Atlin et al. 2017, and the 60-farmer figure is not cited to Mancini at that point). The most important fix is to add the one concrete outcome the review gives for speed: two varieties characterized in 2011 and 2012 were released in 2017, which the authors call 4 years faster than average (p. 5).

**Answer to the panel question.** This paper (p. 3) says the study started with molecular and phenotypic characterization of 373 farmer varieties conserved ex situ in the Ethiopian National Gene Bank, managed by the Ethiopian Biodiversity Institute (EBI), and 27 improved varieties released for cultivation in Ethiopia. It then says "the same panel" was scored by farmers. It does not state the total (400 is a sum), gives no citation for these counts, and places only the 373 farmer varieties in the genebank. In the next bullet it calls the farmer varieties "traditional wheat landraces". So the card's "373 + 27" is correct for this paper. Mancini et al. 2017 (Sci Rep, PDF p. 2), which this review cites for the farmer evaluation, says 400 accessions were obtained from EBI, of which 25 were improved varieties and the rest landraces (so 375). The two papers disagree by two varieties. This is a source issue (see below), and the card's Note already flags it.

## Fixes needed

1. **Add the release-time result (high-importance omission).** The card does not mention that two varieties characterized by Seeds for Needs in 2011 and 2012 were released in 2017 "after 5 years", which the authors call 4 years faster than the average release time (p. 5). This is the only concrete evidence in the review that the approach saves time. It is included in the replacement Summary below. Also add this Key numbers row:

   `| 5 years | Time from Seeds for Needs characterization (2011 and 2012) to release of two farmer varieties (2017), which the authors call 4 years faster than average | This paper |`

2. **Key numbers, ">10 years" row: wrong attribution and context.** Card: "Time to develop and deliver a new cereal variety", primary source "This paper". Source (p. 2): over 10 years to develop a new cereal variety and make it available to farmers in developing countries, "before multiplying and delivering seed", cited to Atlin et al. 2017. Replace with:

   `| >10 years | Time to develop a new cereal variety and make it available to farmers in developing countries, before seed multiplication and delivery | Atlin et al. 2017, cited in this paper |`

3. **Key numbers, "60 farmers" row: attribution not in source.** Card credits Mancini et al. 2017. The source states the 60 farmers (p. 3) with no citation in that sentence. Mancini is cited later, in the bullet on farmer scores and metric traits. The number is right in fact (Mancini PDF p. 2 also reports 60 farmers), but the column should reflect what this paper does. Replace with:

   `| 60 farmers (30 women, 30 men) | Farmers who scored the genebank panel on a Likert scale for phenology, spike morphology and overall desirability | This paper (no citation at this point. The scoring study is Mancini et al. 2017, cited nearby) |`

4. **Summary wording more certain than the source, and small scope errors.** Specific problems:
   - "broadly adapted varieties often miss smallholders' needs" (source p. 1: centralized breeding "may not adequately address" these needs)
   - "a new cereal variety still takes over 10 years to reach farmers" (source p. 2: to make it available, before seed multiplication and delivery)
   - "in least developed countries farmers get about 90%" (source p. 2: 90%, in six named countries)
   - "with Ethiopian durum wheat as the first case" (source p. 3: "a case study", and the most developed one. "First" is not stated)
   - "farmer varieties outperforming recommended modern varieties" (source p. 3: "several" farmer varieties, "in the studied environments")
   - "farmer scores that matched metric traits" (source p. 3: significantly correlated)
   - "the most diverse and highest yielding" families (source p. 3: "more diverse and high yielding", from a preliminary characterization)
   - "this combination is more efficient than either approach alone" (source p. 2: "can lead to higher breeding efficiency". No comparison is measured)
   - "supports ... local seed businesses, linking formal and informal seed systems" (source p. 4: "could also stimulate" local seed businesses, and the system "would contribute" to integrated seed systems)

   The summary also leaves out the 27 improved varieties in the panel, the features of the tricot approach and the result of the global synthesis. Suggested replacement Summary (498 words, fixes all of the above and adds the release result):

   > The review starts from a debate between two breeding strategies. High-tech centralized breeding uses genomics, rapid generation advance and higher selection intensity to accelerate genetic gain, with up to 5.7 selection cycles a year for wheat. Even so, it takes over 10 years to develop a new cereal variety and make it available to farmers in developing countries, and centralized breeding may not meet smallholders' needs for locally adapted varieties. Decentralized participatory breeding involves farmers and captures interactions between genotype, environment and management, as well as local preferences. In six least developed countries, farmers get 90% of their seed from informal sources. The authors argue the two can be reconciled, and use the Seeds for Needs initiative to show how.
   >
   > Bioversity International started Seeds for Needs in 2010 to bring genebank accessions directly to farmers' fields for evaluation in target environments, with Ethiopian durum wheat as its case study. It has since been used in a dozen countries in Africa, Asia and Latin America. It combines three elements. First, 373 farmer varieties from the Ethiopian national genebank and 27 improved released varieties went through molecular and phenotypic characterization, and 60 farmers (30 women, 30 men) scored the same panel. This work found high genetic diversity not yet used by breeders, several farmer varieties outperforming recommended modern varieties in the test environments (some above 5 t/ha, double the national average), disease resistance in landraces, and farmer scores that correlated with metric traits and could be traced to genetic loci. Second, a nested association mapping population (EtNAM) crossed 50 farmer varieties with one improved line to create over 6,000 recombinant inbred lines. A preliminary look at 1,200 lines from 12 families found that families bridging the Ethiopian and international gene pools were more diverse and higher yielding. Third, crowdsourced citizen science, the tricot approach proposed by van Etten in 2011, scaled up farmer evaluation in target environments. It uses digital tools, individual farmers instead of groups, and a widely distributed trial network. A global synthesis from Ethiopia, India and Nicaragua linked farmer rankings to climate data to identify varieties tolerant to climate stress.
   >
   > The authors argue that this combination can raise breeding efficiency. Crowdsourcing provides data from many environments at lower cost than large multilocation trials, gives breeders feedback that centralized programmes lack, and identifies variety portfolios that help farmers cope with climate variability. Farmers joined for reasons that differed by country, mainly to contribute to research and share information with experts, and most did not expect payment. Two varieties characterized in 2011 and 2012 were released in 2017, which the authors say is 4 years faster than average. The approach supports community seed banks, and the authors expect it could stimulate local seed businesses and better link formal and informal seed systems.
   >
   > The review frames Seeds for Needs as a decentralized, participatory, gender-sensitive modern breeding approach. It proposes a feedback loop at landscape or community level in which mobile monitoring of variety performance informs crosses, release and seed delivery.
