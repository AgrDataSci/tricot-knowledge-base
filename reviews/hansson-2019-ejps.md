## Fixes needed

1. **Note on blinding is misattributed (p. 14).** The card says the observation that scientists seldom blind variety trials cites Bulte et al. 2014. In the source, the author says without a citation that agricultural scientists do not normally blind field trials. The Bulte et al. 2014 citation supports only the next sentence: a recent study indicates that blinding may have a large impact on outcomes. Replace the second Note with:
   > - Its statement that agricultural scientists do not normally blind field trials is the author's own observation, given without a citation. The citation to Bulte et al. 2014 supports only the next point, that blinding may have a large impact on field trial outcomes. The reference title shows that Bulte et al. 2014 is a double-blind field experiment in Tanzania on farmers' behavioural responses to new technologies. Report both as the author's statements, not as findings from new data.

2. **Key numbers row for 860 million is credited to ILO 2018, but the paper computes it (p. 15, footnote 12).** ILO gives a workforce of about 3,300 million and 26% in agriculture. The author multiplies them (3,300 × 0.26 = 858, about 860 million). He also notes it may be an underestimate because family members on farms are not included. The main text says "well over 800 million". Replace the row with:
   > | ~860 million | People working in agriculture worldwide (26% of a workforce of about 3.3 billion). The main text says well over 800 million, and the author notes this may be an underestimate | This paper, computed from ILO 2018 figures |

3. **Randomization and blinding stated as absent, without the source's hedge (pp. 13 to 14).** The card says randomization and blinding "are absent from farmers' practice". The source says that, judging by the published literature, randomization does not take place, and that there seems to be no trace of blinding in the published literature. The replacement summary below uses: "The published literature shows no trace of randomization or blinding in farmers' practice." Add the same hedge to not_established (see fix 9).

4. **Summary is under the word limit (393 words) and has several small imprecisions.** Replace the whole Summary with the text below (499 words). It fixes items 2, 7, 8, 11, 14, 17, 19 and 25 in the claim table and adds the medium omissions (hypothesis formation limits, adopted technologies, women as experimenters, small plots, scientists dropping randomization in joint trials).

   > Farmers have experimented for millennia, and several languages have their own word for it, yet philosophers of science have paid this tradition little attention. Sven Ove Hansson, a philosopher at the Royal Institute of Technology and the Swedish University of Agricultural Sciences, uses the anthropological literature to examine what farmers' experiments are and how they compare with agricultural scientists' trials.
   >
   > He distinguishes epistemic experiments, which seek to explain how the world works, from directly action-guiding experiments, which test whether an intervention achieves a practical goal. Farmers' experiments belong to the second kind, alongside clinical trials and agricultural field trials, and should be judged against those rather than against experiments that seek causes and mechanisms.
   >
   > Experimentation is a necessity in farming, not a luxury, because pests and weeds keep evolving and soils keep changing. It is also part of risk management, as subsistence farmers spread many crops, varieties and practices to avoid total failure. Almost every farmer experiments, women are often the main experimenters, and new crops or methods are first tried on small plots. Most experiments test technologies adopted from others rather than new inventions. Hypotheses come from causal understanding and chance observations, as in a Ghanaian farmer who reportedly curbed striga with onion leaf preparations. Without knowledge of microbes and insect life cycles, however, useful hypotheses on pests and diseases are hard to form.
   >
   > Hansson then compares farmers' practice with six precepts of modern methodology. About 39% of African farmer experiments in one survey used side-by-side control plots, but farmers also rely on historical controls. Researchers working with Rwandan bean farmers said some "carry the check in their heads". The rule of changing one variable at a time is often wrong for action-guiding experiments, where testing a promising combination makes sense. Farmers adjust experiments mid-season, which he sees as rational. The published literature shows no trace of randomization or blinding in farmers' practice. Scientists in joint trials have often dropped randomization for practical reasons, and they rarely blind field trials, although varieties whose seeds look alike are easy to blind and blinding may change results. Replication is where farmers excel, since farmers retest new seeds and methods on small plots, even those already tested by scientists.
   >
   > Farmers' experiments give results adjusted to local conditions and replicated at a scale no science can match, given more than 800 million people working on farms. Scientists use stricter controls and statistics against confounding. Which tradition gives better guidance is an empirical question. It depends, among other factors, on how strongly what works in one place also works elsewhere. This may differ by crop, practice and how variable the target region is. Neither tradition can replace the other, and there is room for mutual, respectful learning.
   >
   > He closes with links to the philosophy of science, such as theory-ladenness, the testing of practical hypotheses and the point that action guidance does not require knowing mechanisms. He calls for empirical studies of how farmers choose hypotheses, use controls, deal with confounders and draw conclusions.

   If the curator prefers to keep the current summary and make only sentence-level edits, these are the minimum changes:

   - "rather than against laboratory science" → "rather than against experiments that seek causes and mechanisms" (p. 17).
   - "because pests, weeds, soils and climate keep changing" → "because pests and weeds keep evolving and soils keep changing" (p. 5 does not name climate as a changing factor).
   - "a Ghanaian farmer who curbed striga" → "a Ghanaian farmer who reportedly curbed striga" (p. 7, the source says "Reportedly").
   - "carrying 'the check in their heads'" → "as researchers said of some Rwandan bean farmers, who 'carry the check in their heads'" (p. 11, the quote describes some female Rwandan bean farmers).
   - "Randomization and blinding are absent from farmers' practice" → see fix 3.
   - "although seed varieties are easy to blind" → "although varieties whose seeds look alike are easy to blind" (p. 14).
   - "depends on how strongly performance correlates across local conditions, an empirical question that differs by crop and region" → "is an empirical question. It depends, among other factors, on how strongly what works in one place also works elsewhere, which may differ by crop, practice and region" (p. 16).

5. **Stated limitation 2 says methodological detail is "thin" (p. 18).** The source calls the anthropological literature extensive and rich in empirical detail. It asks for new studies that focus on method, but does not call the existing detail thin. Replace the bullet and add two more limitations the author states:
   > - The anthropological literature is rich in empirical detail but rarely focuses on method. The author calls for empirical studies of how farmers choose hypotheses, use control fields, deal with confounders and draw conclusions.
   > - The absence of randomization and blinding in farmers' experiments is judged only from the published literature.
   > - The line between experimenting and merely trying needs more discussion than the article gives (footnote 3).

6. **Methods: the literature base is not a systematic review.** The card says "a review of anthropological and agricultural literature", which some readers may read as systematic. The paper gives no search method or inclusion criteria. Add:
   > - **Evidence base.** Narrative use of published case studies from anthropologists and agricultural researchers. No search method or inclusion criteria are reported, so this is not a systematic review.

7. **Region omits Europe.** The text discusses European farmer-led experimentation before the mid nineteenth century (p. 3), European beliefs about crop failure (p. 9) and a Scottish writer from 1852 (p. 15). Replace with:
   > region: global (examples from Africa, the Andes, Asia, Central America and Europe)

8. **Optional, crops.** "various" is correct. A more searchable value is:
   > crops: various (examples include millet, sorghum, rice, beans, cassava and potato)

9. **not_established can be stronger.** Replace with:
   > not_established: that farmers' experiments give better or worse decisions than scientists' trials (the author calls this an empirical question that armchair analysis cannot settle); that farmers never randomize or blind (the author finds no trace of it in the published literature only); that blinding changes the results of variety trials (the paper says it may, citing Bulte et al. 2014); that changing one variable at a time is always wrong (the argument applies to action-guiding experiments); any figure on how often farmers experiment beyond the cited surveys; claims about tricot, which the paper does not discuss
