# Short vs long card, de Sousa et al. 2024

Short card ~450-word summary plus key numbers (~5.9 KB). Long card adds "Questions this paper answers", "Methods in brief" and "Stated limitations" (~9.5 KB). Each question was answered using only one card.

## Q1. How many farmers and entries do I need for a tricot trial?

**Short.** About 12 entries across 150 farms is enough to detect large effects (de Sousa et al. 2024). Start with a pilot of about 50 farmers.

**Long.** About 12 entries across 150 farms gives roughly 37 readings per entry, enough to detect a 0.8 standard-deviation difference with 95% probability. Pilot with about 50 farmers in the first season. Use more than one check, and keep blocks A-optimal so the network stays connected if some farms drop out.

**Verdict.** Long wins. It gives the reasoning (readings per entry, effect size) and the design rules that make the number hold.

## Q2. Are farmer rankings valid compared with researcher measurements?

**Short.** Aggregated farmer rankings agreed with breeder evaluations, and low individual reliability is offset by sample size.

**Long.** Same answer, plus the explicit limitation that validity holds only in aggregate, and a pointer to Steinke et al. 2017 (Honduras) as the primary evidence.

**Verdict.** Long wins narrowly. Neither card holds the validity statistics, so a full expert answer needs the Steinke card or the full text. The long card says so.

## Q3. Release committees want yield in t/ha. How does tricot handle that?

**Short.** Teams combine rankings with yields measured on a subset of plots or by farmers.

**Long.** Three concrete routes. Measure yield on a subset of plots, combine ranks with metric data (van Heerwaarden et al. 2023, groundnut in Tanzania), or train farmers to measure with volumetric tins for grain or spring scales for cassava. Plackett-Luce log-worth tracks BLUP yield in simulation.

**Verdict.** Long wins clearly. The short card names the problem, the long card gives a protocol and a citation.

## Q4. Is tricot cheaper than conventional on-farm trials?

**Short.** About 27% cheaper per equivalent experimental unit than RCBD in Rwanda (USD 4.45 vs 6.10), and about 40% cheaper than PVS in Central America.

**Long.** Same figures, plus total cost about 17% lower, and seed as the largest saving (USD 283 vs 523) because tricot plots are smaller.

**Verdict.** Long wins narrowly. The 17% figure prevents a common overstatement of the 27% saving.

## Q5. Should I pay farmers to participate?

**Short.** No. Payment can crowd out intrinsic motivation, while recognition reinforces it. Farmers join to contribute to science and learn from experts.

**Long.** Same answer, plus practice. Farmers keep the harvest unless entries are hybrids or regulations forbid it, and recognition such as certificates or branded t-shirts works.

**Verdict.** Long wins. It turns a principle into something a project manager can act on.

## Overall

- The short card answers every "what" question correctly. The long card adds the "how" that makes an answer sound like an expert, in all five cases.
- The gain comes from the methods and limitations sections, not from a longer summary. The summary was left unchanged.
- Cost is about 3.6 KB more per card. Opening four long cards per question adds roughly 2,500 words of reading, which is acceptable.

**Recommendation.** Use the long format for method-heavy and landmark papers, and the short format for commentaries, perspectives and narrow case studies.
